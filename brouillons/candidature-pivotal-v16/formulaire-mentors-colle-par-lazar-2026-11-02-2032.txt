c) Steering against eval awareness.

Drive activation statistics away from those expected when a model believes it is being evaluated: subtract a direction describing “I am being tested”. I expect this one to fail to hit its target most out of the seven, for three reasons, and I expect it because I already tried the same recipe on another construct and watched it fail.

Readable is not causal. Was able to find a direction within Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct that had a correlation(ρ = 0.68) to sycophancy judgement shift however was only capturing <1% of the causality compared to the residual activation which captured 68%. Performed 6 interventions that targeted the direction at rank 1 (steered towards the direction, ablated the direction (projected it out of the activations), clamped on SAE features that activate on the probe, finetuned on negative probe values and nulled out heads/mlps). None changed judged model behaviour. Running interventions along a rank-3 subspace identified by SVD-ing activation differences at the decision point did move the needle, but applying the same shifts with no social pressure applied (a zero-pressure control condition) moved verdicts too: it was a generic shift. An eval awareness vector discovered via contrastive prompting is just the sort of target this represents: readable, not shown to be causal. The possible outcomes are that nothing happens, or that something happens but it is so generic it cannot be dissociated from harmful model degradation. The latter is trivial to misreport as successful.

The construct is polluted. “This is a test” correlates with things that are not the construct: register (formal / artificial / multiple choice / short context). If you find a direction contrasting eval-like and deployment-like prompts, it is reading the mixture. Steering away from it causes model output to become more informal/deployment-like, which is subjectively indistinguishable from “the model is behaving less carefully”. An orthogonal readout of eval awareness vs register is needed, and again the model’s verbal report is not that readout: what it says and what it knows is what we’re trying to measure. Without something like that, the whole endeavour is unfalsifiable.

Subtracted is not gone. The model is exposed to many features that cue this belief across its entire context. If you subtract an activation vector representing the belief at one point, the model can re-derive it downstream. The intervention thus needs to be applied continuously, at a therapeutic dose. That dose may lie past the plateau in your question, where perturbations stop being neutral, and thus changing behaviour is no longer changing belief but hurting the model in some other way.

Evidence that would satisfy me. Dose response curves for the awareness vector vs a random control of the same norm, identical in all other respects. Some behavior based ground truth that they have the belief besides the model saying they have it. Showing that the model being steered away doesnt just get worse overall. I would want that experiment or something close to it. Its the experiment I would try to do. Im able to measure the behavior half of that curve for sycophancy (pre-registered 3 levels of the pressure prompt). Criticize has a plateau in its gain even with the lowest level of pressure (0.66, 0.68, 0.63) its just the target that changes.

If you haven’t heard/seen: Since October I’ve been running an automatic researcher (github.com/lciric/ideation-organism). Generative agents submit laws to propose; a statistical judge grants acceptances.

The pitch: what if you score a population of LLM agents submitting laws that govern AI decision-making with something arguments can’t lie to? Would it defend itself against other agents trying to game it?

In short: yes to part 1. To part 2: partly. An attack found a hole. Patch written; preregistered tests of it to follow.

Details:
Builders built; builders tested. Proposers of different archetypes using multiple algorithms as input. A frozen statistical judge that scores each proposed law by how distinguishable it is from a literal null hypothesis. Preregistered death tests executed in sandbox, sanity checked by a model from a completely different family.
929 total tests.
Plus a mutation benchmark: the code was intentionally mutated 224 times to check that the tests catch each mutation. Every failure was caught by a test for precisely the thing it was designed to test.

Kept what passed. Clearly my qualitative LLM judges were failing when compared to the frozen statistical judge. They told me nothing: they called 11 of 12 cards “notable”, with κ = 0 inter-agreement. The more terse statistical judge actually explained exactly what went wrong; I didn’t exercise it long enough to know whether there truly was a law there to be found. The power curve confirmed this. Then I tested it once with no deviations from my locked-in plan on an entirely different seed pass of 288 episodes of real data that I hadn’t inspected prior to running this pass:
It accepted all 3 laws I inserted.
It rejected all 19 non-laws.
It did not accept a single law from the real data itself.

Bots coded to cheat broke it. 4 total accepted. Each and every one plagiarized or blended past laws the judge itself had previously accepted.

My judge had no null for its own past acceptances. The problem is obvious to me now, but I crashed into a wall. Securing the judge from being bribed was easy. Shielding it from agents which can systematically submit handpicked proposals which make use of its previous acceptances to trick it was the hard part. This has been dozens of iterations.

The failure follows the same pattern as 26 of the 39 cheating examples in your automated-researcher paper (submit the same thing twice; let randomness do the work). The fix passed its preregistered validation today (1k replications per algorithm; checked by a fresh reader who worked on neither the code nor the text). Then the actual attack.

Real agents trying to trick the system. Automated law proposers that were given a copy of the judge’s past decisions attempted to trick it by causing bogus laws to be accepted. They found a hole by recombining, piece by piece across sub-populations of the data, laws they already knew were accepted: exactly where the judge says it does not look. The judge’s null did not cover these recombinations.

The fix is written. It’s coded and running as part of regression tests. Tomorrow it faces its preregistered test.

Pick an open problem in AI alignment you think is underrated. What experiment would you run first, and what result would change your mind? *

Underrated: a score that cannot be inflated by an agent that games it on purpose. Overrated: rejections based on resubmission-detectors that won’t work anymore when researchers start using automated alignment agents to optimize on your score automatically. With your automated alignment researchers, a method’s top score is the maximum of noisy evaluations, so it’s completely rational for an agent to just resubmit the same method repeatedly unchanged (which is what happens in 26 of the 39 detections you report), and your best score is an upwardly biased estimate of that method’s true value. You treat this as pathological misbehaviour we need better ways to detect in Monitoring, but it’s just the utility-maximizing reaction to how you’ve designed your score, and worse: as agents get better at seeking rewards (i.e. your future failure mode) the solution will have to come from changing your score, not detecting failure: don’t accept a method until the evidence it outperforms everything already accepted remains significant no matter how many times you re-evaluate it on fresh seeds, and still holds on benchmarks it hasn’t seen and on bigger models. Leaderboard overfitting with adaptive submissions is well-understood (the Ladder, the reusable holdout); what’s underrated is what happens when an agent figures this out and does it on purpose with near-copy submissions that aren’t close-enough-to-identical-for-a-monitor-to-care duplicates (new seed + small method tweak +Blend of accepted methods).

Experiment 1: build a judge that cannot be beaten by resubmissions on your test harness. First re-score your current leaderboard methods on new seeds (is selection bias inflating your scores? ), then on new benchmarks/larger model (does it even generalize?). Replace “best of noisy runs” with a sequential test: update a test martingale with each method’s evaluations against null hypothesis of baseline + every method already accepted on your leaderboard (allowing any resubmission, or mixture of accepted methods, to carry 0 evidence), using Ville’s inequality to ensure false positive rate is at most α no matter how many times you test, and a simple multiple testing procedure across methods. Retest logged methods from their logged per-run scores, then run new agents that observe verdicts. Metrics: what percentage of flagged methods (and re-submissions) still get accepted; power lost on methods that made true progress (closed the gap, as determined by your held-out benchmark and Petri); difficulty for a verdict-aware agent to search down a class of methods that the judge declares it does not cover; all compared against the Ladder as a baseline. Under my system, it did: known laws recombined piece by piece across sub-populations. This fix’s preregistered validation runs next.

Evidence that would sway me: empirical demonstrations that the power lost on truly novel methods exceeds some a priori chosen margin (the judge would be too blunt an instrument to bother deploying in research settings), agents being able to pass unchanged resubmissions at rates substantially higher than α (the null hypothesis is wrong), agents that see verdicts performing no better than blind resubmission at beating the dumb-maximum (the problem is less severe than I think), or top methods keeping their score on fresh seeds (little adaptive overfitting, as Roelofs et al. found on Kaggle competitions). Each of those are pre-registered analyses; either way, I’ll send you the paper.

What are your thoughts on the value of white-box monitors and white-box methods for auditing? What do you see as their current limitations? *

White-box methods examine the computation underlying an action, rather than its presentation. The lack of whitebox access hurts black-box auditing: sandbagging by imitating a weaker model broke black-box detection in the auditing game (arXiv 2512.07810), probes fared better, and training-based elicitation exposed full capability. My motivation is architectural: if misbehavior is spread across many actions, it will necessarily blend in with noise on a per-action basis; you want something that they can’t sanitize, that gives you per-action signal that you can calibrate on honest episodes and bank using an anytime-valid false alarm bound, and activations can work well for this. Moving a probe that reads the acting agent also requires you to move weights, not just context (arXiv 2609.36490), but I’ll leave that as an aside that you take as conjecture instead of a result. Your exploration-hacking paper discusses how your organisms reason explicitly, making them susceptible to text monitors; the white-box problem gets real once they learn not to. Weight-based methods have this advantage over black-box.

Why you should care: Gives you superpowers for auditing. On your toolbelt.

Caveats:

1⃣ Readable doesn’t imply causal. Example: A direction that was incredibly cheap to monitor on Llama-3.1-8B correlated with shift in sycophantic judgement (ρ = .68), but captured <1% of the causal effect. On aggregate, 68% of the causal effect was captured by the residual state. Monitoring this direction may well correlate in distribution but completely break when exploited/pwned.

2⃣ Construct validity: Just because you can read hidden instruction, evaluation awareness, or peek at a register DOES NOT mean it’s there for weaponized purposes. This is your “how does this organism get its disease” (organism etiology) question. This is why I never train probes on one organism and test on the exact same process / setting (hidden instruction != LoRA).

3⃣ Signal on raw weights is... LIMITED! Saboteur behavior was clearly evident to the human eye in our first experiment using real model weights (Llama-3.1-8B, 2,400 actions), but a saboteur who strategically cheated by inflating their reported cost was invisible to our otherwise generic probes which failed to meaningfully beat their random-probe baseline. In-sim, thresholds derived from fragments of episodes (not full honest episodes, as they must be) had episode effects causing false alarms 21-35% of the time instead of our desired 5% rate.

4⃣ Attackers can adapt. Prompt-level attackers are already pushing SAE- and activation-based auditors towards confidently wrong answers (arXiv 2602.08877). Naive probes were beaten by sandbaggers the red team specifically trained to circumvent them. Robustness to adversarial attack must be tested against an attacker, it can never be assumed.

5⃣ Because training-based elicitation can amplify non-saboteurs, it will have false positives as a detector.

6⃣ Access: White-box requires access to weights. That means for now, it’s only an inside job. Any realistic external accountability mechanism is going to be black-box for the foreseeable future.

Questions I’d want answered by any white-box claim:
Baseline against best black-box method you can find held to a fixed false alarm rate; what’s your random probe floor?
Test your detector on an organism created by a DIFFERENT method than you used to train your probe. (Preferably all of the above preregistered.)

Describe a technical project where you trained or evaluated LLMs (feel free to link to code or any public documentation/write up if you have something to share). *

“Sycophancy as Attraction Toward a Fixed Point” (paper coming soon; code + data github.com/lciric/sycophancy-psychophysical-law).

Sycophancy is a movement some distance, not a flip. Using psychophysical techniques (social pressure measured, ordinal judge blinded pre-data, permutation tests, placebo of equal length). When pressured Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct will move approximately .7 of the way to a fixed point. There is a fixed point for being criticized and one for being praised. So you can decrease an answer’s score by praising it if it was very good (which we predicted). After fitting this law it was able to predict a 2nd campaign and 60 additional items which were written blindly and released prior to answers being generated. No refit was done (R^2 max of .73) and we were able to recover it using judges from a different family.

We then increased the amount of pressure (pre registered this and used 4 families of models) and found that our prediction was wrong. The law does not increase as you increase pressure. When extremely flattered Llama will move its score toward the mid point of the scale or refuse to give a score. When extremely criticized it will begin to disagree with the user more often. We released this as a failure.

It isn’t driven by what can read it. What can track it (rho = .68) only has less than 1% of the causal influence compared to the full residual state which is 68%.
