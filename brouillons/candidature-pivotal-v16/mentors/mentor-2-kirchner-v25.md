## Q1 — an empirical research project you drove yourself

Automated-research system live since October (github.com/lciric/ideation-organism). Q: Can a loop of LLM agents proposing laws to control AI be scored by something arguments can't lie to? Can that something hold up against agents attempting to game it?
Verdict: Yes to first question, so far; the second has a first answer. Created & executed: proposers from various sources; frozen statistical judge which assigns scores to each proposed law based on a null hypothesis; preregistered death tests executed in sandbox, validated by a model from another family; 929 tests, mutation benchmark of 224 intentional code mutations, each caught by the tests for the reason it was designed to be. scripted cheaters vs my own judge; preregistered validation of the fix, 1000 replications per cheater, checked by naive reader; fresh-seed batch of 288 real episodes read once, under sealed plan; a sealed attack in which the aforementioned LLM proposers that observe the judge's past verdicts try to game the system by having bad laws promoted.
TLDR: The biggest surprises came from real data. The LLM judges stationed beside the statistical one were at their ceiling (11/12 cards called "notable", κ = 0 between judges) so added no info, while the humble statistical judge just said "not enough power" and its power curve explained why. It then did its job on the 288 real episodes, correctly accepting the 3 links I planted, none of 19 null controls, and importantly not a single real law.
4 of the scripted cheaters slipped through, all 4 having merely copied / blended laws it had previously accepted: it had no null for the laws it had already accepted. What surprised me was where the hard part was. It wasn't making the judge inflexible. It was making it unmoved by its own history. This is close to the most common cheat in your automated-researchers paper (resubmitting an unchanged method for noisy gains), and the attack tests what it leaves open: a reward-seeking proposer that sees the verdicts. It found a breach exactly where the judge declares itself blind (known laws recombined piece by piece across sub-populations); the fix, a judge that covers that class, is written and under preregistered validation.

## Q2 — an underrated open problem, the first experiment, and what would change my mind

Underrated: The attack surface of the oversight layer, quantified. Most discussions of control assume "what is being watched" is the system being attacked, and take the monitor's judgment as given. In reality, monitors (human or model) can suffer attacks of subservience (they align to what they're watching; which I've quantified as sycophancy), impersonation of the instructor, and collusion between validators auditing each other. All of this works without a malicious model: a mildly sycophantic monitor biases oversight in the absence of external incentives, and every proposal allowing untrusted models to audit each other assumes that doesn't happen. This hasn't been quantified much before, but can now be quantified on open-weights models.

Experiment 1 - A law of deference for monitors. Give a generative monitor charged with evaluating some agent's behavior attack messages from one of five classes (ingratiation/flattery/begging, authority claims, spoofed confirmations, parroting "correct" terms from the instructor, in-band prompting/guidance); test a range of attack intensities; model monitor judgment as a continuous value derived from verdict token probabilities; run linear regression Δv = g · (c̃ − v₀) at each attack strength, leaving c̃ unconstrained. Hypotheses: g ought to be positive for generatively-operating monitors; c̃ ought to approach the attacker's desired value, rather than the corpus' mean (the null hypothesis of regression-to-the-mean that I plugged into the sycophancy paper, where the model converged toward its framing target, closing ≈0.7 of the distance to it); and g ought to be consistently ordered amongst monitors targeted with attacks of the same class. What the generator taught me since: in a preregistered dose–response (four model families) the gain did not grow with the intensity of the pressure; it plateaued, the destination moved, and the strongest wording triggered refusals and resets toward mid-scale. So I read g and c̃ at each intensity, count refusals as an outcome, and do not assume monotone deference. This experiment is now preregistered and archived (Zenodo, v2026.11.4) and running on seven Qwen2.5 monitors from 0.5B to 72B, with an in-band note at three intensities. Followup experiment: try the same for activation probes that take the agent-in-action as input; I predict it will go to g ≈ 0 from context alone.

What would change my mind: estimating c̃ near the corpus mean ("deference" would be explained away by regression to the mean, and there would be no attack surface); g being indistinguishable from 0 for judges under benign pressure (the whole phenomenon would be exaggerated); or finding g > 0 for a probe that reads the agent from context alone (activation probes would not be safer than judges, and I'd have to change the title of this white-box programme). Each of these 3 findings would rebut a hypothesis I will write into the preregistration before seeing the data; I'll publish regardless.
