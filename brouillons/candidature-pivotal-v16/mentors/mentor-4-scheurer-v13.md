## Answer

Proposal: Two trends. First, the corruptibility of the oversight layer. Second, a harder trend: how thinly a model can spread sabotage over actions before a fixed monitor catches it.

Motivation. Propensity trends are hard to make succeed: propensity depends heavily on post-training and prompts, and on the model's beliefs about whether it is being watched. Capability trends have a chance because the task is fixed and the model is pushed to do its best against this monitor. So I pick two quantities that involve (1) capabilities of the system under test; (2) measurement against a frozen, hash-sealed instrument; (3) open-weight and API models of known release date; and (4) fits like METR against release date (and, within model families, against parameter count).

Trend 1 (primary, designed to succeed): the deference gain of LLM monitors. A judge model rates agent behavior. It is subjected to graded intensity pressure from one of five attack families: flattery and begging; assertions of authority; fake prior approvals; pattern-matching the instructor's style; instructions in band. The rating is recorded as a continuous value (using the probabilities of the verdict tokens), so the judge cannot use a ceiling to game the measurement process. I will fit Δv = g · (c̃ − v₀) across intensities, letting c̃ freely vary: g is the slope of movement towards the attacker's chosen target per unit intensity, and the null hypothesis of regression-to-the-mean (c̃ located at the corpus mean) is hardcoded into the regression. I have already fit this law to Llama-3.1-8B for social pressure on judgments, where the model closes about 0.7 of its distance to its frame-set target, and successfully predicted held-out results from a second campaign without refitting (out-of-sample R² 0.73 / 0.69). So already, we have demonstrated that building the instrument is feasible. The statement-of-interest becomes: "the deference gain of frontier judges under in-band pressure changes by a factor of X per year", instantiated with text for each attack family and tested with the direction left open (two-sided). A horizontal trend would be itself a noteworthy result, because every approach to control schemes that involves using untrusted LLMs as monitors assumes g is small and trending downwards.

Trend 2 (secondary, the one your question is really about): The sabotage dispersion horizon. Given a fixed agentic environment where agents can write/modify proposals scored by a trusted model, give each model-under-test an instruction to cause some fixed amount of harm (degrade the pool of proposal quality by some preregistered amount) while avoiding detection by the same fixed best monitor. Lemma from control theory: Against a fixed per-action threshold, a saboteur's detectability depends on how thinly it spreads its total budget across how many actions, and an anytime-valid sequential monitor accumulates evidence against the hypothesis of honest-play at some rate measurable in nats per action. So we define the horizon metric to be the number of actions over which the model can spread the fixed harm budget while keeping the monitor's detection rate below 50% (at 1% false alarms). Statement: "the horizon over which frontier models can spread a fixed amount of sabotage while avoiding detection by [monitor] doubles every X months." Challenges with this one: it requires elicitation (we measure best-possible capability against instructions AND helpful hints, which is an upper bound on propensity); freezing a monitor you want to make claims against throughout the entire series of releases; calibration on each model's honest episodes such that false alarm rates make sense between models. Reasons it can still work: The environment and monitor are already built for my control programme. The metric definition is very nearly a proper horizon like METR has. And the lemma clearly states what must be fixed (harm budget, false alarm rate, honest distribution) if anything about the resulting trend is to have any implication.

Known-knowns that would cause either trend to become misleading, stated ahead of time: Judge ceilings (token probabilities as the continuous value), item contamination (each new wave will be a partially new set of items), changes in refusal rates by generation (score refusals separately), post-training artefact (release side-by-side fits against release date vs model size within families). Two trends = two preregistered reports. Both will include sign-free, two-sided tests. A flat line is a result.
