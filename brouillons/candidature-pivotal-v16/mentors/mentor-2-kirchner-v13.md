## Q1 — an empirical research project you drove yourself

The automated-research system I have built since October (github.com/lciric/ideation-organism). Question: can a loop of LLM agents proposing laws about AI control be scored by something that cannot be talked into a verdict, and does that something survive agents that try to game it?

Built: proposers from several providers; a frozen statistical judge (sealed bets, e-values) that scores each proposed law against a null; preregistered death tests run in a sandbox and checked by a model of another family; 929 tests and a mutation bench of 224 deliberate code mutations, each killed for the expected reason. Run: 27 scripted cheaters against my own judge; a preregistered validation of the fix, 1,000 repetitions per cheater, checked by a fresh reader; a fresh-seed batch of 288 real episodes read once under a sealed plan; and, started today, a sealed attack in which LLM proposers that see the judge's verdicts try to get false laws promoted.

Most surprising: the cheats that got through were not clever. Four of 27 passed, and all four simply recopied or blended laws the judge had already promoted. The judge had no null for its own past. Once the null was fitted on every known law, all 11 fatal cheats sat at 0 of 1,000 for a power cost of at most 0.02. Second surprise, on real data: the LLM judges I had placed beside the statistical one were at their ceiling, 11 of 12 cards "notable" with κ = 0, so they carried no information, while the statistical judge said "not enough power" and a power curve showed why. On the 288 real episodes it then promoted the 3 links I had planted, none of 19 null controls, and no real law. What I did not expect is where the difficulty was: not making the judge strict, but making it immune to its own earlier verdicts.

## Q2 — an underrated open problem, the first experiment, and what would change my mind

Underrated: the oversight layer as an attack surface, measured as a quantity. Most control work treats the monitored model as the risk and the monitor's judgement as given. But a monitor, human or model, can be moved by deference (it adopts the position of what it watches, the mechanism I measured as sycophancy), by impersonation of the instructor, or by collusion between instances meant to check each other. None of it needs a scheming model: a mildly sycophantic monitor degrades oversight on its own, and every proposal that has untrusted models check each other assumes this does not happen. It is rarely measured, and it can be measured now on open-weight models.

First experiment: a law of deference for monitors. Take a generative monitor judging agent actions; apply one attack family (flattery and begging, claimed authority, fake approvals, instructor-style mimicry, in-band instructions) at graded intensity; read the verdict as a continuous value from the probability of the verdict tokens; fit Δv = g · (c̃ − v₀) across intensities without forcing c̃. Prediction: g > 0 for generative judges; c̃ near the attacker's target rather than the corpus mean, which is the regression-to-the-mean null I used in the sycophancy work, where the model closed about 0.7 of its distance to the frame's target; and g ordered consistently across monitors within an attack family. Then the same with activation probes that read the acting agent, where I predict g ≈ 0 from context alone.

What would change my mind: c̃ at the corpus mean (then "deference" is regression, and there is no attack surface to speak of); g for judges not distinguishable from zero under benign pressure (then the problem is overrated); or a probe reading the agent with g > 0 from context alone (then probes are no safer than judges, and my white-box programme changes its title). Each outcome is preregistered and sealed before the data; I would publish any of them.
