## Q1 — an empirical research project you drove yourself

I have an auto research system that has been running since October. github.com/lciric/ideation-organism Q: could you score a group of llm agents trying to create laws for ai control with something that arguments cant lie to? Could that thing withstand agents that try to cheat it?
Verdict: Yes to first question, so far; the second has a first answer. Created & executed: proposers from various sources; frozen statistical judge which assigns scores to each proposed law based on a null hypothesis; preregistered death tests executed in sandbox, validated by a model from another family; 929 tests, mutation benchmark of 224 intentional code mutations, each caught by the tests for the reason it was designed to be. Scripted cheaters vs my own judge; preregistered validation of the fix, 1000 replications per cheater, checked by naive reader; fresh-seed batch of 288 real episodes read once, under sealed plan; a sealed attack in which the aforementioned LLM proposers that observe the judge's past verdicts try to game the system by having bad laws promoted.
The LLM judges that I had placed next to the statistical judge were maxed out (called 11/12 cards as “notable” with κ=0 agreement between each other) so they didn’t provide any information. My simple statistical judge told me we didn’t have enough power and the power curve backed that up. Then I was able to run it on my 288 episodes of real data and it accepted all 3 links that I embedded and 0 out of the 19 non laws. And it accepted no law from the real data itself.
4 of the scripted cheaters slipped through, all 4 having merely copied / blended laws it had previously accepted: it had no null for the laws it had already accepted. I didnt expect where I would have trouble. Not getting my judge to be stubborn. Getting it not to be fooled by its own past decisions. Similar to most cheating in your paper about automated-researchers (no change in method just resubmit for noise). Our attack takes advantage of this by giving the proposer access to rewards from decisions. We found an exploit exactly where our judge declares it does not look (the proposer knows the laws, it just puts them together per sub population). The fix is a judge that can account for this. Its preregistered test runs next.

## Q2 — an underrated open problem, the first experiment, and what would change my mind

Underrated: a score that cannot be inflated by an agent that games it on purpose. Overrated: rejections based on resubmission-detectors that won’t work anymore when researchers start using automated alignment agents to optimize on your score automatically. With your automated alignment researchers, a method’s top score is the maximum of noisy evaluations, so it’s completely rational for an agent to just resubmit the same method repeatedly unchanged (which is what happens in 26 of the 39 detections you report), and your best score is an upwardly biased estimate of that method’s true value. You treat this as pathological misbehaviour we need better ways to detect in Monitoring, but it’s just the utility-maximizing reaction to how you’ve designed your score, and worse: as agents get better at seeking rewards (i.e. your future failure mode) the solution will have to come from changing your score, not detecting failure: don’t accept a method until the evidence it outperforms everything already accepted remains significant no matter how many times you re-evaluate it on fresh seeds, and still holds on benchmarks it hasn’t seen and on bigger models. Leaderboard overfitting with adaptive submissions is well-understood (the Ladder, the reusable holdout); what’s underrated is what happens when an agent figures this out and does it on purpose with near-copy submissions that aren’t close-enough-to-identical-for-a-monitor-to-care duplicates (new seed + small method tweak +Blend of accepted methods).

Experiment 1: build a judge that cannot be beaten by resubmissions on your test harness. Re-score your current top methods on fresh seeds (selection exaggeration? ), then unseen benchmarks/larger model (does it transfer?). Then replace “best of noisy runs” with a sequential test: each method’s evals update a test martingale against null of baseline + every method already accepted on your leaderboard (any resubmission, or mixture of accepted methods, carries no evidence), using Ville’s inequality to guarantee false positive rate at most α no matter how many times you test, and a simple multiple testing correction across methods. Replay logged methods from their logged per-run scores (i.e. don’t use GPU to replay), then run new agents that observe verdicts. Metrics: what percentage of flagged methods (and re-submissions) still get accepted; power lost on methods that made true progress (closed the gap, as determined by your held-out benchmark and Petri); difficulty for a verdict-aware agent to search down a class of methods that the judge declares it does not cover; all compared against the Ladder as a baseline. Under my system, it did: known laws recombined piece by piece across sub-populations. This fix's preregistered validation runs next.

Evidence that would sway me: empirical demonstrations that the power lost on truly novel methods exceeds some a priori chosen margin (the judge would be too blunt an instrument to bother deploying in research settings), agents being able to pass unchanged resubmissions at rates substantially higher than α (the null hypothesis is wrong), agents that see verdicts performing no better than blind resubmission at beating the dumb-maximum (the problem is less severe than I think), or top methods keeping their score on fresh seeds (little adaptive overfitting, as Roelofs et al. found on Kaggle competitions). Each of those are pre-registered analyses; either way, I’ll send you the paper.
