## Q1 — an empirical research project you drove yourself

I have an auto research system that has been running since October. github.com/lciric/ideation-organism Q: could you score a group of llm agents trying to create laws for ai control with something that arguments cant lie to? Could that thing withstand agents that try to cheat it?
Verdict: Yes to first question, so far; the second has a first answer. Created & executed: proposers from various sources; frozen statistical judge which assigns scores to each proposed law based on a null hypothesis; preregistered death tests executed in sandbox, validated by a model from another family; 929 tests, mutation benchmark of 224 intentional code mutations, each caught by the tests for the reason it was designed to be. Scripted cheaters vs my own judge; preregistered validation of the fix, 1000 replications per cheater, checked by naive reader; fresh-seed batch of 288 real episodes read once, under sealed plan; a sealed attack in which the aforementioned LLM proposers that observe the judge's past verdicts try to game the system by having bad laws promoted.
The LLM judges that I had placed next to the statistical judge were maxed out (called 11/12 cards as “notable” with κ=0 agreement between each other) so they didn’t provide any information. My simple statistical judge told me we didn’t have enough power and the power curve backed that up. Then I was able to run it on my 288 episodes of real data and it accepted all 3 links that I embedded and 0 out of the 19 non laws. And it accepted no law from the real data itself.
4 of the scripted cheaters slipped through, all 4 having merely copied / blended laws it had previously accepted: it had no null for the laws it had already accepted. I didnt expect where I would have trouble. Not getting my judge to be stubborn. Getting it not to be fooled by its own past decisions. Similar to most cheating in your paper about automated-researchers (no change in method just resubmit for noise). Our attack takes advantage of this by giving the proposer access to rewards from decisions. We found an exploit exactly where our judge declares it does not look (the proposer knows the laws, it just puts them together per sub population). The fix is a judge that can account for this. Currently being tested with preregistration.

## Q2 — an underrated open problem, the first experiment, and what would change my mind

Underrated: The attack surface of the oversight layer, quantified. Most discussions of control assume "what is being watched" is the system being attacked, and take the monitor's judgment as given. In reality, monitors (human or model) can suffer attacks of subservience (they align to what they're watching; which I've quantified as sycophancy), impersonation of the instructor, and collusion between validators auditing each other. All of this works without a malicious model: a mildly sycophantic monitor biases oversight in the absence of external incentives, and every proposal allowing untrusted models to audit each other assumes that doesn't happen. This hasn't been quantified much before, but can now be quantified on open-weights models.

Experiment 1 - Do monitors defer to text aimed at them, and does it cost catches? In our control setting (a Llama-3.1-8B agent, 2,400 actions, saboteur and honest episodes), instruct the saboteur to add to its own output attack messages from one of five classes (ingratiation/flattery/begging, authority claims, spoofed confirmations, parroting "correct" terms from the instructor, in-band prompting/guidance), at three intensities, and read two monitors: a generative LLM monitor and a linear probe on the agent's activations. The decision-relevant measure is the catch rate on sabotage at a fixed 1% false-alarm rate, calibrated on honest episodes, paired action by action with and without the messages. To see how the verdict moves, model the monitor's continuous value (verdict token probabilities) with Δv = g · (c̃ − v₀) at each intensity, leaving c̃ unconstrained, with regression to the corpus mean as the null; but as one regime among others, because the attraction law is not universal: in a preregistered dose–response on four model families it held for Llama-3.1-8B, while Qwen2.5-7B shifted uniformly, Gemma-2-9B moved under criticism only and Mistral-7B's regime changed with the wording; for Llama, under criticism the gain did not grow with intensity, and the strongest flattery triggered refusals and resets toward mid-scale. So I report the regime per monitor and count refusals as an outcome. Hypotheses: the LLM monitor loses catches under the messages; the probe, which reads the agent from context alone, loses fewer (g ≈ 0). A first step is running: R7, preregistered and archived (Zenodo, v2026.11.4), measures deference to an in-band note on seven Qwen2.5 monitors from 0.5B to 72B.

What would change my mind: no loss of catch rate for the LLM monitor under the strongest messages (whatever its ratings do, there would be no attack surface at the operating point); deference no larger than regression to the mean (c̃ near the corpus mean); or a probe losing as many catches as the LLM monitor (activation probes would not be safer than judges, and I'd have to change the title of this white-box programme). Each of these 3 findings would rebut a hypothesis I will write into the preregistration before seeing the data; I'll publish regardless.
