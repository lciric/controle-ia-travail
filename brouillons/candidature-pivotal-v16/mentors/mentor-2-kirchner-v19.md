## Q1 — an empirical research project you drove yourself

Automated-research system live since October (github.com/lciric/ideation-organism). Q: Can a loop of LLM agents proposing laws to control AI be scored by something arguments can't lie to? Can that something hold up against agents attempting to game it?
Verdict: Yes to first question, so far; started today on second. Created & executed: proposers from various sources; frozen statistical judge which assigns scores to each proposed law based on a null hypothesis; preregistered death tests executed in sandbox, validated by a model from another family; 929 tests, mutation benchmark of 224 intentional code mutations, each caught by the tests for the reason it was designed to be. 27 scripted cheaters vs my own judge; preregistered validation of the fix, 1000 replications per cheater, checked by naive reader; naive batch of 288 real episodes read once, under sealed plan; started today, sealed attack in which the aforementioned LLM proposers that observe the judge's past verdicts try to game the system by having bad laws promoted.
TLDR: The biggest surprises came from real data. The LLM judges stationed beside the statistical one were at their ceiling (11/12 cards called "notable", κ = 0 between judges) so added no info, while the humble statistical judge just said "not enough power" and its power curve explained why. It then did its job on the 288 real episodes, correctly accepting the 3 links I planted, none of 19 null controls, and importantly not a single real law.
4 of the 27 cheaters slipped through, all 4 having merely copied / blended laws it had previously accepted: it had no null for the laws it had already accepted. What surprised me was where the hard part was. It wasn't making the judge inflexible. It was making it unmoved by its own history.

## Q2 — an underrated open problem, the first experiment, and what would change my mind

Underrated: the attack surface of the oversight layer, quantified. Work on control generally assumes "what is being watched" is the thing under attack, and the monitor's judgement is unchanging. But monitors (whether humans or models) can be influenced by subservience (they become biased towards the position of what they're watching; which I've quantified as sycophancy), by impersonation of the instructor, or by collusion between validators trying to audit one another. None of it requires a malicious model: a lightly sycophantic monitor skews oversight even without outside incentives, and every proposal that lets untrusted models audit each other assumes that doesn't occur. It's rarely been quantified, but can now be quantified on open-weights models.

First experiment: a law of deference for monitors. Consider a generative monitor judging the behavior of some agent; pressure it with messages from one of five attack classes (flattery/begging, appeals to authority, counterfeit confirmations, parroting the instructor's vocabulary, in-band instructions); use scaled intensities of attack; model the judgment as a continuous value read from the probabilities of the verdict tokens; linearly regress Δv = g · (c̃ − v₀) over attack strengths without constraining c̃. Predictions: g > 0 for generative evaluators; c̃ near the attacker's target value rather than the corpus average (the null hypothesis of regression-to-the-mean I used in the sycophancy paper, where the model closed ≈0.7 of the distance to its frame target); and g should rank consistently for monitors within an attack class. Repeat for activation probes that take the acting agent as input, which I predict will show g ≈ 0 by context alone.

What would convince me: finding c̃ near the corpus mean ("deference" would be accounted for by regression, and there would be no attack surface). g statistically indistinguishable from 0 for judges under benign pressure (the phenomenon would be overstated). Or g > 0 for a probe that reads the agent from context alone (then activation probes aren't safer than judges, and my white-box programme changes its title). Each of these outcomes is written into the preregistration before the data; I'll publish any of them.
