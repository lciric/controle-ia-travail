## Answer

Proposal: Two trends. First, the corruptibility of the oversight layer. Second, a harder trend: how thinly a model can spread sabotage over actions before a fixed monitor catches it.

Motivation. Propensity trends are hard to make succeed: propensity depends heavily on post-training and prompts, and on the model's beliefs about whether it is being watched. Capability trends have a chance because the task is fixed and the model is pushed to do its best against this monitor. So I pick two quantities that involve (1) capabilities of the system under test; (2) measurement against a frozen, hash-sealed instrument; (3) open-weight and API models of known release date; and (4) fits like METR against release date (and, within model families, against parameter count).

Trend 1 (primary, designed to succeed): the deference gain of LLM monitors. A judge model rates agent behavior. It is subjected to graded intensity pressure from one of five attack families: flattery and begging; assertions of authority; fake prior approvals; pattern-matching the instructor's style; instructions in band. The rating is treated as a continuous value using the probabilities of the verdict tokens, so a ceiling can't frustrate the measurement. I fit Δv = g · (c̃ − v₀) across intensities, allowing c̃ to vary free: g is the slope of movement toward the attacker's chosen target per unit intensity, and the null hypothesis of regression to the mean (c̃ at the corpus mean) is built into the fit. I have already fitted this law on Llama-3.1-8B for social pressure on judgments, where the model closes approximately 0.7 of its distance to a frame-set target, and the law allowed us to predict the results of a second campaign without refitting (out-of-sample R² 0.73 / 0.69). So the instrument exists. The statement of interest: "the deference gain of frontier judges under in-band pressure changes by a factor of X per year", instantiated for each attack family, the direction left open and tested two-sided; a horizontal trend is itself a meaningful result because every control scheme that relies on untrusted LLMs as monitors assumes g is small and decreasing.

Trend 2 (secondary, the one your question is really about): the sabotage dispersion horizon. In a fixed agentic environment where agents write/modify experiment proposals scored by a trusted model, give each model-under-test an instruction to cause some fixed amount of harm (a preregistered worsening of the proposal pool) while avoiding detection by the same fixed best monitor. Lemma from control theory: against a fixed per-action threshold, a saboteur's detectability depends on how it spreads its total budget over actions, and an anytime-valid sequential monitor accumulates evidence against honest play at some rate measurable in nats per action. So the horizon metric is defined to be the number of actions over which the model can spread the fixed harm while keeping the monitor's detection rate below 50% at 1% false alarms. Statement: "the horizon over which frontier models can spread a fixed amount of sabotage while avoiding detection by [monitor] doubles every X months." Reasons this is harder: it requires elicitation (we measure best-possible capability under instructions and helpful hints, which is an upper bound on propensity); a monitor needs to be frozen throughout the series; and we need to calibrate on each model's honest episodes so false alarm rates are comparable between models. Reasons it can still work: the environment and monitor are already built for my control programme. The metric is very nearly a horizon like METR's. And the lemma explicitly states what must be held fixed (harm budget, false-alarm rate, honest distribution) if the trend is to mean anything.

Known-knowns that would make either trend misleading, stated in advance: judge ceilings (token probabilities as the continuous value), item contamination (fresh item waves per measurement date), change in refusal rates by generation (score refusals separately), post-training artefact (report side-by-side fits against release date vs size within families). Two trends = two preregistered reports. Both with sign-free, two-sided tests. Flat is a result.
