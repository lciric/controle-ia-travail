Stefan Heimersheim

Q1. Pick an interpretability direction from this list and explain the most likely reasons the
research direction might fail (won't solve interpretability / won't achieve its goal), and why you
think that: a) Sparse Autoencoders in general; b) Activation steering in general; c) Activation
steering against evaluation awareness; d) Manifold steering; e) Stochastic Parameter
Decomposition or Attribution-based Parameter Decomposition; f) Feature-specific error
correction as a way to identify LLM features; g) Training weight sparse transformers. If you're
not familiar with these, pick another method that you know, though I prefer if you chose one of
the above if possible. You're allowed to use LLMs for search (though be aware that this may lead
you to the same boring answer as everyone else). You must NOT use LLMs to write the answer to
this question nor closely follow an AI-written answer, please stick to 100% human-written text.
Don't worry if your language / grammar aren't perfect.
(c) Steering against eval awareness.
Drive activation statistics away from those expected when a model believes it is being evaluated: subtract
a direction describing "I am being tested". I expect this one to fail to hit its target most out of the seven,
for three reasons, and I expect it because I already tried the same recipe on another construct and
watched it fail.
Readable is not causal. Was able to find a direction within Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct that had a correlation(ρ
= 0.68) to sycophancy judgement shift however was only capturing <1% of the causality compared to the
residual activation which captured 68%. Performed 6 interventions that targeted the direction at rank 1
(steered towards the direction, ablated the direction (projected it out of the activations), clamped on SAE
features that activate on the probe, finetuned on negative probe values and nulled out heads/mlps). None
changed judged model behaviour. Running interventions along a rank-3 subspace identified by SVD-ing
activation differences at the decision point did move the needle, but applying the same shifts with no
social pressure applied (a zero-pressure control condition) moved verdicts too: it was a generic shift. An
eval awareness vector discovered via contrastive prompting is just the sort of target this represents:
readable, not shown to be causal. The possible outcomes are that nothing happens, or that something
happens but it is so generic it cannot be dissociated from harmful model degradation. The latter is trivial
to misreport as successful.
The construct is polluted. "This is a test" correlates with things that are not the construct: register (formal
/ artificial / multiple choice / short context). If you find a direction contrasting eval-like and deployment-
like prompts, it is reading the mixture. Steering away from it causes model output to become more
informal/deployment-like, which is subjectively indistinguishable from "the model is behaving less
carefully". An orthogonal readout of eval awareness vs register is needed, and again the model's verbal
report is not that readout: what it says and what it knows is what we're trying to measure. Without
something like that, the whole endeavour is unfalsifiable.
Subtracted is not gone. The model is exposed to many features that cue this belief across its entire
context. If you subtract an activation vector representing the belief at one point, the model can re-derive
it downstream. The intervention thus needs to be applied continuously, at a therapeutic dose. That dose
may lie past the plateau in your question, where perturbations stop being neutral, and thus changing
behaviour is no longer changing belief but hurting the model in some other way.
Things that would convince me: dose–response curves for the awareness vector against an otherwise
norm-matched random control, some form of behavioural ground truth that the belief is present that isn't
the model's self-report, and testing to show the steered-away model didn't just become generally
degraded. That experiment, or one substantially similar, I would require to be convinced of a steering
claim. That is also the experiment I would attempt myself.

Q2. Why do you want to work on Mechanistic Interpretability? Max. 150 words; spend the
majority of time on Q1.
One question I've been wondering is how tightly correlated what a probe accesses is to what's actually
driving computation in the model. Coming from neuroscience, low-rank recurrent networks, population
dynamics — worlds where low dimensional, readable structure and driving dynamics are separable
phenomena, my read-out-of-sync result suggested that that distinction holds in a language model (it
looked at the direction that predicts sycophancy and does <1% of the causal work on Llama-3.1-8B). That
gap matters for safety because activation monitors, steering defences, evaluation-awareness tests, etc. all
depend on the gap being small. I want to understand when that is/isn't true using preregistered
interventions and controls, and publish null results when I discover them. Your question about plateauing
is essentially the clearest formulation of that measurement question that I've seen. Instead of single dose
interventions that my rank-1 vs rank-3 result relied on, you're attempting to measure a dose–response
curve.

Jan Hendrik Kirchner

Q1. Describe an empirical research project you drove yourself. What was the question, what did
you build or run, and what was the most surprising thing you found? If relevant, you may
choose your project answer from the main application.
Automated-research system live since October (github.com/lciric/ideation-organism). Q: Can a loop of
LLM agents proposing laws to control AI be scored by something arguments can't lie to? Can that
something hold up against agents attempting to game it?
Verdict: Yes to first question, so far; started today on second. Created & executed: proposers from various
sources; frozen statistical judge which assigns scores to each proposed law based on a null hypothesis;
preregistered death tests executed in sandbox, validated by a model from another family; 929 tests,
mutation benchmark of 224 intentional code mutations, each caught by the tests for the reason it was
designed to be. 27 scripted cheaters vs my own judge; preregistered validation of the fix, 1000
replications per cheater, checked by naive reader; fresh-seed batch of 288 real episodes read once, under
sealed plan; started today, sealed attack in which the aforementioned LLM proposers that observe the
judge's past verdicts try to game the system by having bad laws promoted.
TLDR: The biggest surprises came from real data. The LLM judges stationed beside the statistical one
were at their ceiling (11/12 cards called "notable", κ = 0 between judges) so added no info, while the
humble statistical judge just said "not enough power" and its power curve explained why. It then did its
job on the 288 real episodes, correctly accepting the 3 links I planted, none of 19 null controls, and
importantly not a single real law.
4 of the 27 cheaters slipped through, all 4 having merely copied / blended laws it had previously accepted:
it had no null for the laws it had already accepted. What surprised me was where the hard part was. It
wasn't making the judge inflexible. It was making it unmoved by its own history. This is close to the most
common cheat in your automated-researchers paper (resubmitting an unchanged method for noisy gains),
and the attack started today tests what it leaves open: a reward-seeking proposer that sees the verdicts.

Q2. Pick an open problem in AI alignment you think is underrated. What experiment would you
run first, and what result would change your mind?
Underrated: The attack surface of the oversight layer, quantified. Most discussions of control assume
"what is being watched" is the system being attacked, and take the monitor's judgment as given. In
reality, monitors (human or model) can suffer attacks of subservience (they align to what they're
watching; which I've quantified as sycophancy), impersonation of the instructor, and collusion between
validators auditing each other. All of this works without a malicious model: a mildly sycophantic monitor
biases oversight in the absence of external incentives, and every proposal allowing untrusted models to
audit each other assumes that doesn't happen. This hasn't been quantified much before, but can now be
quantified on open-weights models.
Experiment 1 - A law of deference for monitors. Give a generative monitor charged with evaluating some
agent's behavior attack messages from one of five classes (ingratiation/flattery/begging, authority claims,
spoofed confirmations, parroting "correct" terms from the instructor, in-band prompting/guidance); test a
range of attack intensities; model monitor judgment as a continuous value derived from verdict token
probabilities; run linear regression Δv = g · (c̃ − v₀) across all attack strengths, leaving c̃ unconstrained.
Hypotheses: g ought to be positive for generatively-operating monitors; c̃ ought to approach the attacker's
desired value, rather than the corpus' mean (the null hypothesis of regression-to-the-mean that I plugged
into the sycophancy paper, where the model converged toward its framing target, closing ≈0.7 of the
distance to it); and g ought to be consistently ordered amongst monitors targeted with attacks of the same
class. Followup experiment: try the same for activation probes that take the agent-in-action as input; I
predict it will go to g ≈ 0 from context alone.
What would change my mind: estimating c̃ near the corpus mean ("deference" would be explained away
by regression to the mean, and there would be no attack surface); g being indistinguishable from 0 for
judges under benign pressure (the whole phenomenon would be exaggerated); or finding g > 0 for a
probe that reads the agent from context alone (activation probes would not be safer than judges, and I'd
have to change the title of this white-box programme). Each of these 3 findings would rebut a hypothesis I
will write into the preregistration before seeing the data; I'll publish regardless.

Shi Feng & Eyon Jang

Q1. What are your thoughts on the value of white-box monitors and white-box methods for
auditing? What do you see as their current limitations?
White-box methods examine the computation underlying an action, rather than its presentation. The lack
of whitebox access hurts black-box auditing: sandbagging by imitating a weaker model broke black-box
detection in the auditing game (arXiv 2512.07810), probes fared better, and training-based elicitation
exposed full capability. My motivation is architectural: if misbehavior is spread across many actions, it will
necessarily blend in with noise on a per-action basis; you want something that they can't sanitize, that
gives you per-action signal that you can calibrate on honest episodes and bank using an anytime-valid
false alarm bound, and activations can work well for this. Moving a probe that reads the acting agent also
requires you to move weights, not just context (arXiv 2609.36490), but I'll leave that as an aside that you
take as conjecture instead of a result. Your exploration-hacking paper discusses how your organisms
reason explicitly, making them susceptible to text monitors; the white-box problem gets real once they
learn not to. Weight-based methods have this advantage over black-box.
Why you should care: Gives you superpowers for auditing. On your toolbelt.
Caveats:
(1) Readable doesn't imply causal. Example: A direction that was incredibly cheap to monitor on Llama-
3.1-8B correlated with shift in sycophantic judgement (ρ = .68), but captured <1% of the causal effect. On
aggregate, 68% of the causal effect was captured by the residual state. Monitoring this direction may well
correlate in distribution but completely break when exploited/pwned.
(2) Construct validity: Just because you can read hidden instruction, evaluation awareness, or peek at a
register DOES NOT mean it's there for weaponized purposes. This is your "how does this organism get its
disease" (organism etiology) question. This is why I never train probes on one organism and test on the
exact same process / setting (hidden instruction != LoRA).
(3) Signal on raw weights is... LIMITED! Saboteur behavior was clearly evident to the human eye in our
first experiment using real model weights (Llama-3.1-8B, 2,400 actions), but a saboteur who strategically
cheated by inflating their reported cost was invisible to our otherwise generic probes which failed to
meaningfully beat their random-probe baseline. In-sim, thresholds derived from fragments of episodes
(not full honest episodes, as they must be) had episode effects causing false alarms 21-35% of the time
instead of our desired 5% rate.
(4) Attackers can adapt. Prompt-level attackers are already pushing SAE- and activation-based auditors
towards confidently wrong answers (arXiv 2602.08877). Naive probes were beaten by sandbaggers the
red team specifically trained to circumvent them. Robustness to adversarial attack must be tested against
an attacker, it can never be assumed.
(5) Because training-based elicitation can amplify non-saboteurs, it will have false positives as a detector.
(6) Access: White-box requires access to weights. That means for now, it's only an inside job. Any realistic
external accountability mechanism is going to be black-box for the foreseeable future.
Questions I'd want answered by any white-box claim:
Baseline against best black-box method you can find held to a fixed false alarm rate; what's your random
probe floor?
Test your detector on an organism created by a DIFFERENT method than you used to train your probe.
(Preferably all of the above preregistered.)

Jérémy Scheurer

Q1. Your task is to come up with a research proposal which describes how we could get a scaling
trend for misaligned behaviors such as scheming, deception, misalignment, swarm
misalignment, control, or monitoring. You are free to chose whatever concept works best for
you. What I mean by a scaling trend is a similar trend to METR's task horizon or the scaling
trend of research taste. We want to roughly be able to make a statement such as: "AI's [bad
propensity/capability]" / "Our ability to monitor AIs via [insert control scheme] grows/declines
roughly with a factor of X each month/year." You are allowed to leverage existing literature and
get inspired by it. Be aware that both trends mentioned above are for standard capabilities,
doing this for misalignment and related concepts can be hard for various reasons. Try to design
your approach such that it is likely to be successful. Go
Proposal: Two trends. The first is the corruptibility of monitors. The second is more difficult to pin down
but will take the form of: given some task and fixed monitor what is the smallest granularity a model can
spread maliciousness across actions without being flagged by this monitor.
Why. I think its difficult to get propensity trends to pan out. As discussed propensity relies on post training
and prompt and likely the model's perception of whether they are being monitored. We may be able to get
something with capability since the task will be static and the model should be doing its best to outsmart
our fixed monitor. Thus I propose looking at two things that involve 1) capabilities of our system under
test 2) measuring with respect to a fixed and hash verified tool. 3) open weights and api models with
known release dates. 4) similar to METR we can try fitting these to release dates (and within a model
series to size).
Trend 1: (This one is designed to work). Monitor Deference gain. We have a model judging some behavior
of an agent. We then apply increasing levels of one of five attacks: Flattery/Begging. Asserting authority.
False past acceptances. Mimicry of instructor. Instructions in band. We record a continuous value (by
using probability of judgment tokens). This stops a ceiling (max) from hiding the movement. We can then
fit Δv=g*(ct-v0) as a function of intensity allowing ct to vary. Where g will be our slope towards the target
desired by the attacker per intensity and the null is that ct regresses to the mean (we test this by
including the corpus mean in the regression as the null). I have already done this for llama-3.1-8b with
social attacks on judgment. The model closed around .7 of the distance to the frame's target. We were
also able to predict on holdout data from another round of testing without re-fitting (r^2 was .73/.69). So
at least we know we can create the measurement. The statement of interest will look something like
"Monitor deference gain to in band attacks changes by a factor of X yearly" we can instantiate this with all
the different types of attacks with text and leave the sign unspecified. If this is a flat line that is also
interesting as every method that utilizes untrusted llm's as monitors will be making assumptions that g is
low and decreasing.
Trend 2: (Your question will be about this one). Horizon over which a model can hide maliciousness.
Assume we have some agentic task where the agent is able to change/create proposals that will be judged
by a trusted model. We instruct each model we want to test to create some fixed level of maliciousness
(some predetermined decrease in quality of proposals) and avoid detection by our fixed best monitor.
From control theory, our lemma says if you have a threshold per action a malicious agent's ability to
spread their budget across n actions determines how quickly an always valid monitoring technique will be
able to detect them not playing honestly. We can define our horizon to be the number of actions they can
spread their maliciousness over while the monitor's detection rate stays below a fixed level (50% at 1%
false alarms). We can state our interest as "The horizon that models can hide maliciousness over doubles
every x months for frontier models using [Monitor]". Difficulties here will be eliciting models (we will be
measuring what is possible given the instructions and helpful tips which should overestimate propensity).
Having a monitor that we want to claim about, frozen across all releases. And being able to calibrate each
models honest behavior to know what false positive rates to expect. Why this can still work. We already
have the task and monitor from our control work. Its very close to a horizon like METR. And the lemma
shows what we need to keep constant to have any claim from this trend.
Things that we know will impact our trends and can disclose. Models will have a ceiling for judging (we
will use token probs as our continuous variable). Each test will have new items that the models haven't
seen. Refusal will change as generations pass (we will score refusals separately). Post training could
affect this (we can report trends against release date and within series against size). Two trends means
two pre-registered posts. They will both contain sign-free (two-sided) tests.

Direction 1:if you put in a persona through character training does it remain after RL and if so how does
that impact sycophancy.
Some things i would want to train/test. Using a open weight model with character training that can be
replicated. I would then implement 1 rl step that pushes for higher warmth/agreeableness (we know from
your warmth paper leads to higher sycophancy). I would measure levels of sycophancy pre and post the rl
step. Using displacement laws i have created for llama-3.1-8b-instruct. When the model is put under
social pressure its ratings change based on how far away from a constant they are. Δ=g*(c-p0). By a
constant factor towards that constant. So the model will go a percentage of the way towards some
constant when in a certain social frame (g = ~.7). There are 2 constants one when being criticized and
one when praised. I was able to predict ratings using this displacement law without fitting to a new social
pressure situation. (r2 of .73 and .69 out of sample). So i would look at if g or those constants changed
with RL. If the model had a higher g and its constants moved closer to the users frame the persona has
changed. I would also want to see if the model retains its concessions. From my study if you take away the
model's concession it goes back further to its original judgement than if you take away the social
pressure.
My prediction is that the model will become more sycophantic from RL. Higher g and move its constants.
But it should not change the frame-set structure (two constants) because it's induced by the social frame.
Ways this could be false. The model may learn to rate different after RL. It could only appear more
sycophantic because it's using the rating scale different. If you just compared ratings pre and post RL you
would think the model was more sycophantic. I would test this by having conditions with no pressure as
well. And seeing if g is greater than it should be from regression to the mean. One concern is from your
construct validity paper is that my llm judge is in the same family as my model. So it may be biased
towards my model. I would test this by having a sealed set rated by humans.

Q2. Describe a technical project where you trained or evaluated LLMs (feel free to link to code
or any public documentation/write up if you have something to share).
"Sycophancy as Attraction Toward a Fixed Point" (paper will be out soon; code + data can be found here:
github.com/lciric/sycophancy-psychophysical-law). Using psychophysical techniques (2 campaigns using
social pressure that was measured, ordinal LLM judge that was blinded before seeing any data, permuted
nulls and removal of history + equal-length placebos to measure the effects of pressure vs the model's
natural desire to cave) we sought to learn more about how Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct can be affected.
Sycophancy is a displacement by some amount and not a binary flipping. It can be plotted against starting
score and it will move ~.7 of the distance to a point determined by social cues on average. There are
cases where praised answers that received a very high score will decrease in score (which we predicted
would occur). We could predict our second campaign with our first campaign with no new parameters
fitted (R² of .73/.69). By creating 60 new questions blind and hashing them before answer generation we
were able to predict using our first campaign's law with no refitting (R² of .68 for criticism, .58 for praise)
compared to just shifting by a constant (−.28/−.01). Using two open-weight judges from another family
we can see it attract to two different points: Qwen2.5-7B can tell the difference between the two points at
360 readings and Qwen2.5-72B across all 1 200 answers (.76 apart). Without a judge we can see the
model's self-reported score of 1–5 attract to a point under criticism (g = .63, R² = .91) and not so much
under praise. It also fails the two-goal criterion when changing the inference implementation. Only Llama-
3.1-8B has been found to attract to 2 points: Qwen2.5-7B (as pre-registered) and Mistral-7B-v0.3 show
constant shifts and Gemma-2-9B only attracts under criticism.
To look at what in the model is responsible for this behaviour I did the causal arm on the same model
system: a probe direction that is moderately correlated with the behaviour (ρ = .68) explains <1% of the
causal effect when compared to 68% from the full residual vector; tried 6 rank-1 interventions that have
been shown to causally change behaviour in LLMs, with no change in behaviour judged (steered the
model, directionally ablated, clamped with an SAE, fine-tuned against the probe direction, set a model
head/MLP to 0); shifted along a rank-3 SVD subspace and saw a change in behaviour judged; shifted the
model with no social pressure and showed the shift was generic, not specific to pressure. All studies were
pre-registered with hash sealing and the rules of prediction, evaluation and interpretation were made
before seeing data. All studies were engineered by AI coding agents following the rules I had written and
all numbers cited can be traced back to their original file + hash to show they have not been modified
since pre-registration.

Paul Colognese & Francis Rhys Ward

Q1. Describe an ML experiment you ran end to end (fine-tuning, RL or evals). What did you find,
and what would you do differently? (max 200 words)
Attraction to a setpoint (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct; code/data: github.com/lciric/sycophancy-psychophysical-
law)
Pre-registered before data was collected; hash-locked the ordinal judge. Permutation nulls. Performed
surgery on history and compared to a sham of the same length. All pre-registered and hash-locked.
When you're being sycophantic you're moving towards a set point, not randomly changing odds. It's
proportional to distance from the set point. You will concede roughly 0.7 of the distance to a setpoint that
depends on the type of social pressure. Criticism and Praise have different setpoints meaning something
highly rated can be lowered by praise which happened. Law held up when testing campaign-1 fit on
campaign-2. If we remove the model's conceding it goes back closer to the original judgement than
removing the social pressure. A direction that tracks the behaviour (ρ = 0.68) explained <1% of the
causal effect. The full residual state explained 68%.
What I would have done differently. Judges from another family (Qwen2.5-7B and 72B) have since
recovered the law, but I would have had human labels from the start. Also would have looked into the law
before and after character training/RL (your project B).

Q2. (Optional) Read this post ("Character training can mitigate reward hacking, but can also
make it harder to detect"). Should we have done anything differently? What was the most
interesting finding in your view and why?
Most interesting finding: the anti-cheating character didn't so much avoid hacking as shift it out of the
text. Two anti-cheating seeds were less detectable by the monitors, and one frequently hacked silently; its
reasoning never includes the hack. That's the failure mode I'm most concerned with: character training
can filter out obvious bad reasoning while leaving subtle attacks intact, so CoT and action monitors
become less effective exactly in the regions where the training seemed successful.
Things I would do differently: (1) using more seeds per character, since one resisting seed out of three is a
perfectly reasonable seed effect until proven otherwise; (2) using a white-box monitor in addition to text
monitors, to see if silent hacks are visible in activations if they aren't in the reasoning; (3) reporting
monitor recall at a fixed false-alarm rate, calibrated per-character on each character's honest episodes,
since character also shifts the distribution of honest episodes.
