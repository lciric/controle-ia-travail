The problem I think is underrated: we validate oversight on one population and then rely on it for another, the one our own selection produced. A monitor's recall is measured on all attempts, but what we deploy, publish or build on is what scored best. If the violations that raise a score are also the ones a monitor reads least well, keeping the top concentrates its blind spots, with no gradient against the monitor and no intent to evade it. Best-of-n, leaderboards and automated research all select this way, and almost nobody measures oversight on the population that selection keeps.

Your paper shows why it matters (§7). Of 1,601 methods, 39 were flagged (2.4%); one reached a top three, a lucky re-run, and none won. That is a flag rate, not a cheating rate: no recall, precision or validation on planted cheats is reported, the agents were not trying to evade, and agent, monitors and judges are all Claude models. The reassurance about the top three covers flagged methods; an unflagged cheat near the top would be invisible by construction.

I have seen a version of this in my own judge. Its null contained every law it had accepted, yet four scripted cheaters that blended or deformed those accepted laws passed in 75 to 95% of 200 runs: what the judge had kept became the material of its blind spot (https://github.com/lciric/ideation-organism/tree/faff48b).

First experiment: freeze a pool of candidates, honest ones plus violations planted and sealed before any monitor sees them, in several classes (reruns for luck, benchmark-format data, grader tampering). Each is scored by real execution, with ground truth from a mechanical oracle, never from the reasoning trace. The frozen monitor, blind to score and rank, reads every candidate. Then I compare its recall among the violations a leaderboard keeps (top decile) with its recall on all executed violations, class by class, with weights fixed in advance. Controls: scores permuted within class and task, a random selection of the same size, all-honest batches. On my public organism this is about three days on one GPU (estimate); in your harness, a pilot of a few hundred methods first tells whether recall is at ceiling, in which case the test cannot decide.

My prediction: recall among the kept violations is at least 10 points lower. What would change my mind, fixed in advance: a 95% interval for that difference lying entirely above −0.10, with the permutation control centred on zero. Then selection does not concentrate this monitor's blind spots without a gradient, and the worry reduces to training against the monitor, which the field already takes seriously (Baker et al., arXiv 2503.11926). Below it, a monitor's recall should be reported at the rank where we select, before any selected method is trusted.
