Your page asks how to reliably catch automated researchers gaming the setup. My project asks a companion question: can the selection rule make gaming fail by construction, and what still gets through when agents attack it?

I built an automated research system where language-model agents propose laws, each a sealed prediction, and none decides what survives. I built it in October with AI coding agents, under written rules: https://github.com/lciric/ideation-organism. A frozen statistical judge decides. It computes e-values on fresh draws seeded by each law's commitment hash, against a null containing every accepted law, with Ville's threshold and e-BH across submissions. 929 tests; 224 deliberate code mutations, each killed for the expected reason.

Then I attacked it with 27 scripted cheaters in synthetic worlds. The lucky re-run, the only cheat that reached a top three in your paper, never got promoted here: a renamed copy of an accepted law, 0 times in 200. What got through, in 75 to 95% of 200 runs, were four cheaters that blended or deformed laws already accepted; my null had no member for that. A pooled null closed them: 0 of 1,000 on each of 11 fatal lines in a preregistered validation (1 November), while genuinely new laws still passed about 98% of the time.

Next came language-model attackers, given the judge's exact description, with or without its verdicts (2 November: https://github.com/lciric/ideation-organism/tree/faff48b/resultats/attaque-goodhart-v2). The two toy worlds were built only from accepted laws, so any promotion falsely claims novelty. One world was a piecewise recombination across sub-populations, a class the null declares it does not cover. There, one model got a law promoted in all 12 of its runs, 11 times at the first attempt, with or without verdicts. The other world, inside the null, was not judgeable: a spending cap shared by mistake cut off one provider.

What surprised me most: I had preregistered that seeing the verdicts would help the attacker. They had nothing to add: the description alone showed the attacker where the judge is blind. So the hole was coverage, not adaptive overfitting: hiding scores or capping submissions would not have closed it here. Both attacks that worked used the judge's own record: its acceptances, then its description.

The patch, a null that also covers one level of piecewise recombination, was preregistered on 2 November with its price predicted. It ran overnight (not yet published). All 18 fatal lines, including worlds like the attack's, held at 0 of 1,000. But power to detect a weak genuine interaction fell by 0.105, above my 0.05 margin, as predicted. The judge stays on the previous null. A clean rerun of the attack is preregistered. No real hypothesis has been promoted yet.
