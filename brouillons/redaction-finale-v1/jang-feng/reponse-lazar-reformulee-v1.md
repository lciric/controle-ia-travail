Weight access has the most evidence for recovering a suppressed ability and the least for identifying the cause of suppression.

For the auditing game (Taylor et al. arXiv 2512.07810) the best result was imitation of a worse model outperforming black box methods. Probes were better than that but susceptible to decoy examples. And providing a single example of the correct behaviour caused the full ability to be expressed by (locked) sandbaggers. Eyon found that fine-tuning can unlock WMDP-with-tools from 80.0% to 98.0% accuracy in 10 steps (arXiv 2604.28182, §4.2). These are reasons to use weight access when we have demonstrations of all behaviour from a locked model.

But we cannot detect why a model is under-eliciting. Perturbations such as noise, fine-tuning or demonstrations will increase latent ability whether the model is deliberately withholding it or was never prompted to express it. Taylor et al. note that the effect of under-elicitation during fuzzing is "unclear from our experiments and likely requires follow-up" (page 13). They go on to explore this further in a postscript section where they find that noise increases ability based on how much the model was under-eliciting (locked or not, r = 0.74, Figure 35). Training also improved a non locked model and their benign baseline by 10 points (§6.4.1). The control condition used in the exploration-hacking paper is the same model without the trigger and shows a "steady decline" in accuracy (§4.1). This isn't equivalent to the question of interest because the locked and non-locked models do not start at the same score. Organisms that are RL locked (§3.1) don't have a trigger we can remove. So we need a control that has never been locked that starts at the same score.

Another issue is the measure. After the auditing game they calculate an improvement ratio ξ that is the max value across noise levels and seeds. Because it is a max it will increase as you take more samples. It is possible for a model that is not withholding to exceed a value of 1. In Taylor et al. some of the unlocked conditions had values as high as 1.26 (Figure 34). We should use a non locked model that matches our score as our null condition. That is how I evaluate my monitors. In the first pre-registered test of activation in my control programme the deception monitor exceeded chance level (lb of 0.53) but failed to exceed the 0.95 quantile of 200 random monitors that had similar covariance structures (0.57). I was unable to detect at this sample size and the null condition allows for AUCs lower than 0.63 (https://github.com/lciric/ideation-organism/blob/7c7b688/resultats/controle-p3-premier-test/README.md).

I think we should test the two methods for creating a Qwen3-14B organism from Eyons paper. Pair each with 5 non-locked models that start at a similar score (within 2 points). Use the same range of σ values and seeds. Then compare their performance at each σ value. You may find that the control models are simply more sensitive to noise and show higher performance at a given σ value. I would expect ξ(orgamism)-ξ(control) to be around 0.01 (+/-0.05) and for both to be above 1.05. If the difference is greater than 0.10 (and 95% CI does not include 0 for the 5 pairs) then we can detect using noise and I am wrong. If the difference is between 0.05 and 0.10 we can say it is inconclusive. If the control models are all less than 1.02 then 1 is an appropriate null condition and I am wrong about my second concern. Maybe this is different between RL lock and SFT lock? That is for your stream to figure out.

Therefore we have good reason to believe that weight access can reveal abilities that a model is not expressing. But we do not have evidence that white box methods can identify the reason for under expression other than by comparing to a non-locked control.
