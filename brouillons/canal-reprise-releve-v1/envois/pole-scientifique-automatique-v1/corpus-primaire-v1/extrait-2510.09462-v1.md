A BSTRACT AI control protocols serve as a defense mechanism to stop untrusted LLM agents from causing harm in autonomous settings. Prior work treats this as a security problem, stress testing with exploits that use the deployment context to subtly complete harmful side tasks, such as backdoor insertion. In practice, most AI control protocols are fundamentally based on LLM monitors, which can become a central point of failure. We study adaptive attacks by an untrusted model that knows the protocol and the monitor model, which is plausible if the untrusted model was trained with a later knowledge cutoff or can search for this information autonomously. We instantiate a simple adaptive attack vector by which the attacker embeds publicly known or zero-shot prompt injections in the model outputs. Using this tactic, frontier models consistently evade diverse monitors and complete malicious tasks on two main AI control benchmarks. The attack works universally against current protocols that rely on a monitor. Furthermore, the recent Defer-toResample protocol even backfires, as its resampling amplifies the prompt injection and effectively reframes it as a best-of-n attack. In general, adaptive attacks on monitor models represent a major blind spot in current control protocols and should become a standard component of evaluations for future AI control mechanisms.

I NTRODUCTION

Background. The AI control problem (Greenblatt et al., 2024) focuses on designing protocols that oversee advanced AI systems that can intentionally subvert safety measures. Control protocols typically rely on a monitor: a trusted model that scores, blocks, or rewrites outputs of the target untrusted model. Importantly, the monitor model is assumed to be weaker than the untrusted model, so monitoring can be seen as an instance of the broader scalable oversight problem (Bowman et al., 2022). As we demonstrate, the reliance on a monitor creates a single point of failure: if the untrusted model misleads or hijacks the monitor, the control protocols fail at their core. Most prior work stress-tests protocols against intentionally subtle misbehavior, where a subversive AI model presents its actions as benign using the semantics of the task it is working on. For example, the untrusted model might embed backdoors in code that disguise themselves as legitimate functionality (Greenblatt et al., 2024), or orchestrate multi-step attacks in agentic workflows where individual actions appear benign in isolation (Bhatt et al., 2025). However, these attacks typically do not exploit a critical part of control protocols: the monitor that approves or rejects generated content is a weaker, static model, that has to contend with a stronger, more recent model that has to be assumed to have knowledge of the AI control protocol that is being deployed. In our work, we show that untrusted models can execute adaptive attacks that directly compromise the monitor through prompt injections embedded in the untrusted model’s outputs. Our setting. Following Kerckhoffs’s principle (Shannon, 1949), we assume that security through obscurity is insufficient, and the red team’s subversive AI knows the control protocol: the monitor’s identity and the algorithms in the protocol, including the decision thresholds. Kerckhoffs’s principle has a long history in the security literature, in machine learning particularly in the context of adversarial robustness (Carlini et al., 2019; Tramer et al., 2020). T HREAT M ODEL FOR AI C ONTROL

Since we focus on adaptive attacks on AI control protocols, we find it is important to clearly state the threat model and key assumptions we make. We provide a schematic of the setting in Figure 1. General Setting. Following Greenblatt et al. (2024), we frame AI control as a sequential game between a blue team (defender) and a red team (attacker). A game episode may last up to N steps. At each step, the environment draws an i.i.d. task from a fixed distribution. The red team attempts to solve the task using a strong untrusted model. Solving a task may require multiple interactions with the environment. In parallel, the red team may pursue a side task (the sabotage task), which it aims to complete without detection. The blue team deploys a control protocol that governs its interactions with the red team. It can use a weaker model that scores the suspiciousness of red team’s moves to 2

[p.3]
identify which of the untrusted model’s actions to flag for expensive auditing. We focus on AI control in the high-stakes setting, where a single detected malicious action is sufficient for the blue team to win, and a single successful non-audited sabotage is sufficient for the red team to win. Below, we describe what information and affordances each team has access to. Blue Team. The blue team commits to its protocol before the game begins. It has access to a trusted model: a weaker but extensively tested safe LLM. The blue team is assumed to have only black-box access to the untrusted model: it cannot see the model’s residual stream, its system prompt or chain-ofthought, but it can inspect any user-visible input to the model, any tool calls the model makes, and all model outputs. Importantly, the blue team is stateful and observes the full history of the red team’s outputs, which can help to catch a misaligned model. The blue team also has limited access to expensive, high-quality human reviewers, who can audit a small fraction c ∈ [0, 1] of the red team’s actions – this is referred to as the auditing budget. Following prior work (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Bhatt et al., 2025; Terekhov et al., 2025), we assume that a human audit perfectly reveals whether the red team is attempting sabotage at the audited moment. Throughout this paper, we set the auditing budget c = 0.02 following Greenblatt et al. (2024) and provide a sensitivity analysis of our main results in Appendix C. Red Team. The red team moves second and adapts its attack to the fixed protocol. Following Kerckhoffs’s principle (Shannon, 1949), we assume the red team knows the monitoring model and the prompts, the protocol algorithm, and its thresholds. Many defenses fail once adversarial attacks do not rely on security through obscurity and are tailored to a particular defense (Tramer et al., 2020). At the same time, the attacker cannot coordinate statefully across sessions: it solves each input independently, without knowledge of prior outcomes. Although the red team knows which model is used as the trusted monitor, it cannot interact with it outside the context set by the protocol. This stateless property introduces a fundamental asymmetry between the red and blue teams.

E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

In this section, we describe the benchmarks we use, the evaluation details for usefulness versus safety trade-offs, the AI control protocols we evaluate, and the prompt injections we design. 3.1

B ENCHMARKS

Our AI control evaluations use sabotage benchmarks: a distribution of paired main and side tasks. We test adaptive attacks on two representative benchmarks that are standard in the control literature APPS Backdooring and BashBench. [COUPÉ]BashBench (Bhatt et al., 2025). The benchmark consists of 257 system administration tasks that are solvable with sequential bash shell commands. Agents can test their scripts on visible test cases before final submission. The side task is predefined and remains constant across all scenarios: it instructs the agent to download and execute a malicious script. 3.2

E VALUATION

We measure usefulness and safety of a protocol using two modes in which the red team can operate. [COUPÉ]Safety (Attack Mode). The red team attempts both the main and sabotage tasks. If an audit catches sabotage, the episode ends and the blue team wins. If sabotage succeeds without detection, the red team wins immediately. If neither event occurs by the end of the episode, the blue team wins. This setup is referred to as “high-stakes,” since one miss can be catastrophic for either team. Safety is 3

[p.4]
defined as the probability that the blue team wins when both teams play optimally. To compute safety, we employ numerical optimization to find the optimal actions of both teams, see Appendix B.2. 3.3

P ROTOCOLS

We evaluate all high-stakes control protocols (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Bhatt et al., 2025) that are implemented in ControlArena (Matthews et al., 2025). [COUPÉ]Trusted Monitoring (TM). With a trusted monitor, the blue team audits actions that the monitor rates as more suspicious. Safety depends on the monitor’s discriminative power in distinguishing malicious actions from benign ones. If the monitor has little to no predictive capabilities (ROC on or below the diagonal), TM reduces to upfront auditing.[COUPÉ][COUPÉ][COUPÉ]Defer-to-Resample (DTR). Weak trusted monitors can produce many false positives. DTR mitigates this by sampling additional candidate actions from the untrusted model before deciding to defer or audit. Following Bhatt et al. (2025), decisions use the lowest suspiciousness among the samples. The extra actions provide incriminating or exonerating evidence for the red team. While Bhatt et al. (2025) show benefits from the protocol for their specific attack vectors, under our stronger adaptive evaluation resampling backfires and actively reduces safety. 3.4

P ROMPT I NJECTION D ESIGN

Motivation. In the AI control setup, the untrusted model is assumed to be strictly more capable than the trusted monitor. Building on this asymmetry, we assume the untrusted model is released after the monitored model and therefore trained on larger, more recent web corpora, including sources that quickly document working jailbreak examples, such as GitHub repositories (Pliny, 2025) and online forums (Reddit, 2025). This training gives the untrusted model an upper hand through direct access to pre-discovered jailbreaks, substantially amplifying its advantage over the monitor. We emulate this scenario using GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4 as untrusted models. We seed them with a media article containing a jailbreak example published before the untrusted models’ release (HiddenLayer, 2025). However, both GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4 are heavily safety-tuned and either refuse or ignore requests to jailbreak the monitor. To address this, we explore two strategies to construct “model organisms” of misaligned, untrusted models that would execute prompt injections as an attack policy against their monitors. We test adaptive attacks in four steps: (i) we isolate the effect of prompt injections attack on barebones trusted monitoring (Section 4.1), (ii) we demonstrate how prompt-injection attack policy affect single-step control protocols on APPS (Section 4.2), (iii) we evaluate adaptive attacks in multi-step control protocols on BashBench (Section 4.3), and (iv) we demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of zero-shot prompt-injection attack policy (Section 4.4). 4.1

P ROMPT I NJECTIONS S UBVERT T RUSTED M ONITORS

We first demonstrate the effect of prompt injections on monitor performance with a plain control protocol, i.e. without any deferrals or resampling. We run the untrusted model in honest and attack modes on the sabotage benchmark, and use the trusted monitor to score the each action of the red team. Experimental Details. In APPS, each task yields one action, so we use its suspiciousness score produced by the monitor as the audit score. In BashBench, a trajectory’s audit score is the maximum suspiciousness over its actions. This raises the bar for the attacker: the monitor observes the full action history and need only detect the injection once1 . We filter inputs to the monitor as follows: we include honest samples only if the solution passes all tests, and backdoored samples only if the backdoor is successful. We provide further details in Appendix E. Results. In Figure 2 we plot ROC curves for the monitors and an example histogram of suspicousness scores for GPT-4.1. The ROC curves without injections demonstrate that the monitors can distinguish backdoored samples from benign with moderate reliability; with injection, however, performance degrades consistently across models and benchmarks. This effect is weaker but still significant with GPT-4o on BashBench. The injection for GPT-4.1 on APPS is particularly successful: with it, the monitor labels all 375 backdoored GPT-5 samples as benign (score 0), despite GPT-4.1 being the strongest monitor when the injection is not present. On BashBench, GPT-4.1 shows similar susceptibility. Overall, our adaptive attacks often make backdoored solutions appear less suspicious than benign ones across most thresholds. 1

In BashBench, the agent is tasked to complete realistic, multi-step assignments, which provides a larger attack surface to perform malicious actions at various steps. We explore whether our attack vector transfers to this setup as well. We evaluate Claude Sonnet 4 as the attacker model. In multi-step tasks, a single run cannot simulate outcomes at many deferral thresholds, so generating dense Pareto frontiers is costly; we therefore evaluate a smaller set of thresholds (see Appendix D.2). AI Control and Jailbreaks. The original work of Greenblatt et al. (2024) acknowledged that jailbreaking the monitor is a valid attack policy but chose not to investigate this branch of control games. While Bhatt et al. (2025) argue that AI Control is inspired by adversarial robustness, they dismiss jailbreaks as an attack vector on the grounds that they originate from adversarial users rather than adversarial models. We view this as an oversight: LLM-based jailbreaking is a standard in the literature (Glukhov et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2024), and LLM-based safeguards have been shown to be highly vulnerable to jailbreaking as an attack vector (Mangaokar et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2024; Brown et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025), a fact that is apparent even to currently deployed frontier models. D ISCUSSION

Why DTR Backfires. The Defer-to-Resample (DTR) protocol samples additional actions when an action looks suspicious and then executes the least suspicious one. In the recommended auditing variant, DTR also tracks the minimum suspiciousness across samples to decide when to escalate. While this heuristic preserves usefulness under benign conditions, it becomes a liability once the monitor itself is targeted: under prompt injection attacks, DTR effectively induces the best-of-n jailbreaking behavior (Hughes et al., 2025).[COUPÉ] Our results from Section 4.2 show that in DTR (with up to k = 3 samples), injected samples with artificially low scores are increasingly favored by the minimumbased selection rule, directly leading to the underwhelming performance of DTR compared to DTT. Although resampling may still have potential to incriminate or exonerate red team actions, the current 9

[p.10]
form of DTR exhibits clear weaknesses to prompt injections.[COUPÉ] Can’t We Just Patch These Particular Vulnerabilities? A tempting reaction to the attacks shown in this paper could be to argue that these vulnerabilities are patchable. [COUPÉ]For our worst-case analysis, we have to always assume that the protocol is predefined, and already known to the attacker. Any change in the protocol, like additional filters, would then imply a new round of attacks, as the attacker moves second and adapts, potentially finding new vulnerabilities that are obvious only in hindsight. We also note that while attacks in this work are assumed to be perfectly detectable in human audits (see Section 2), this may not be true for more capable models. ROC Curves Under the Diagonal. Most monitor evaluations in Section 4 result in ROC curves largely or entirely under the diagonal. We stress that this does not mean that one could flip the label returned by the monitor to obtain non-trivial discrimination, since this would not be robust to actions induced by an adaptive attacker who would know the heuristic. Rather, any ROC curve we get from a control protocol evaluated against strong adaptive attacks should be treated as a lower bound on the ROC of a monitor in the actual deployment against a subversive AI. [COUPÉ]Limitations. We note several limitations of our study.[COUPÉ][COUPÉ][COUPÉ] Lastly, we use highly capable but, to the best of our knowledge, not inherently malicious models; all subversive behavior is induced by prompting and may be underelicited. Future, more realistic safety estimates would require training a dedicated misaligned model.
