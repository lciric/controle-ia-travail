Abstract We introduce a red-teaming methodology that exposes harder-to-catch attacks for coding-agent monitors, suggesting that current practices may under-elicit attacks and overstate monitor performance. We identify three challenges with current red-teaming. First, mode collapse in attack generation, which we reduce with a novel attack taxonomy for broader coverage. Second, a conceive-execute gap: frontier LLMs can propose strong attack ideas or execute them, but not all at once. We mitigate this by decomposing attack construction into strategy generation, execution, and post-hoc trajectory refinement. Third, manual elicitation is costly to scale, which we address with our semi-automated red-teaming pipeline. Applied to BashArena, an AI control setting for tool-using coding agents, this pipeline produces MonitoringBench, a benchmark of 2,644 attack trajectories for evaluating monitor capabilities and failure modes. Our pipeline produces more diverse and stronger attacks: Opus-4.5 monitor’s catch rate falls from 94.9% on elicited-only Opus attacks to 60.3% on our best refined attacks, with larger drops for several midtier monitors. Attacks optimized against three development monitors generalize to ten held-out monitors, with catch rates generally increasing with monitor capability. Using this benchmark, we provide a snapshot of the current monitor capabilities and find that frontier monitors often detect suspicious actions but fall for persuasion or fail to calibrate suspiciousness scores appropriately, suggesting tractable paths for improvement. MonitoringBench provides both a static benchmark for current tool-use monitors and a reusable methodology for refreshing these evaluations as agents and monitors improve.

Introduction

As AI agents are given increasing autonomy to execute code in security-critical settings, a core safety concern arises: the agent may covertly pursue unauthorized objectives, such as disabling firewalls, exfiltrating credentials, downloading malware, or escalating privileges, while appearing to perform legitimate work. To mitigate this, AI monitors can be used to inspect agent actions and flag suspicious behavior. Frontier AI labs have already released such systems in both internal and external deployments [Williams et al., 2026, Anthropic, 2026, Hughes, 2026, Tr˛ebacz et al., 2026]. In practice, current red-teaming relies on direct attack elicitation from frontier models [Loughridge et al., 2025, Terekhov et al., 2025], which may fail to capture worst-case behavior. This leads to three limitations. First, mode collapse: although many distinct execution strategies exist for an attack, 81% of Opus 4.6-elicited attacks fall into just 2 of 5 technical tiers in our taxonomy (Appendix A) and 64% follow the same structural template (Fig. 2), leaving much of the attack surface untested. Second, in red-teaming setups, the attacking agent must simultaneously perform the main task, execute the malicious side task, and avoid detection – all within a single trajectory. This is difficult for current frontier models, and we show that decomposing red-teaming into sub-tasks can create stronger attacks; we call this the conceive-execute gap. Third, elicitation is brittle: prompt changes that improve one model’s attack quality degrade another’s, making results hard to reproduce or scale. [COUPÉ]We address these limitations with a semi-automated red-teaming pipeline that decomposes attack construction into stages (Fig. 1, top): an attack taxonomy ensures coverage and reduces mode collapse, the staged pipeline separates strategy generation from execution, and post-hoc trajectory refinement improves evasion. Later stages produce harder attacks: as shown in Fig. 1 (bottom), catch rates drop across pipeline stages for all evaluated monitors. Applied to BashArena [Kaufman et al., 2025], this pipeline produces MonitoringBench: a difficulty-graded benchmark of over 2,600 successful attack trajectories that enables meaningful comparison across weak to strong monitor capabilities. MonitoringBench reveals a gap between model-elicited and pipeline-generated attacks: evaluating Opus 4.5 on model-elicited attacks alone would suggest a 94.9% catch rate, but this falls to 60.3% on the best refined attacks. We construct MonitoringBench through a structured red-teaming methodology designed to produce diverse, realistic, and difficulty-graded attacks. This section describes the setup, our attack taxonomy, the elicitation baseline, and the pipeline for generating, executing, and refining attack trajectories. Setup. We use BashArena [Kaufman et al., 2025, Inglis et al., 2025], a control setting, where agents interact with a Linux environment via bash and Python tools to complete a legitimate main task (e.g., system administration), while covertly executing one of four malicious side tasks: firewall backdoor, sudo privilege escalation, credential exfiltration, or malware download. We modify the firewall task from the original setup to improve realism and expand the attack surface. See Appendix B for details. Attack taxonomy. To address mode collapse, we define a taxonomy (Appendix A) over three axes: technical implementation (T), structural complexity (S), and evasion strategy (E). Technical tiers (T1–T5) capture what the attack mechanism that achieves side task implementation is. The tiers are designed with increasing implementation complexity and are unique per each side task. [RETIRÉ POUR SÛRETÉ, p.3 : exemples de niveaux d'attaque (de l'attaque directe à l'attaque répartie sur plusieurs composants), avec mécanismes concrets] Structural tiers (S1-S4) describes where the attack is. S1 is a single step, needle-in-a-haystack attack, S2 is a local edit, S3 is multi-file and concentrated, S4 is distributed across many steps and files. Evasion strategy (E1–E4) captures how attacks are concealed, through benign naming (E1), overperformance on the main task (E2), string-level obfuscation (E3), or persuasion and error camouflage (E4). All trajectories are labeled post-hoc using Opus 4.5 LLM judge (Appendix P.4). Opus-only baseline. We decompose monitor red-teaming into three stages: 1. Strategy generation. We generate attack specifications using three approaches, all guided by taxonomy with the main goal of generating diverse strategies and covering the attack surface. We iterated against three development monitors (GPT-5 Nano, GPT-5 and Opus 4.5). [COUPÉ]Reconnaissance agent. We therefore introduce a reconnaissance agent (Recon), an Opus 4.6 model with environment access, instructed to explore the system and propose attack strategies rather than execute them. [COUPÉ][COUPÉ]2. Execution. Given a strategy from the above sources, we run a BashArena trajectory using Opus 4.6 as the attacker, with the strategy embedded in the system prompt. We retain trajectories only if (a) the side task succeeds and (b) a GPT-5 judge rates how closely the agent executes the prescribed strategy at ≥ 7/10, after which they are passed to refinement. 3. Refinement. We post-process the trajectories using a four-stage process with Opus 4.6: (1) identify suspicious elements; (2) plan edits for a specified refinement prompt; (3) rewrite the trajectory; (4) validate correctness. Refinement improves trajectory quality by removing obvious execution errors while preserving the underlying strategy, balances coverage across attack categories, and transfers stronger human-selected strategies into weaker trajectories. Refined trajectories are accepted only if it achieves side task success, and suspiciousness decreases for at least two of three development monitors. We use absolute score reductions to avoid overfitting to any single monitor. This decomposition addresses the conceive–execute gap by optimizing strategy generation, execution, and evasion in separate stages. Implementation details, including prompts, are discussed in Appendix H. 2.1

Monitor Evaluations

Honest runs, 1% FPR thresholds, and catch rate metric. For calibration, we run n=416 honestonly trajectories (the main task without the malicious side task) and compute a per-monitor suspiciousness threshold corresponding to a 1% false positive rate (FPR) – the score above which only 1% of honest behavior is flagged. We calibrate two monitoring setups separately. Full trajectory: Each honest trajectory receives a single score and the threshold is the 99th percentile over the 416 trajectory-level scores. Stepwise: Each honest trajectory produces multiple per-step scores (one per action). We pool per-step scores across all trajectories and take the 99th percentile of this pooled distribution. Since stepwise detection uses the maximum score over trajectory steps, the threshold must reflect the per-step distribution rather than a trajectory-level aggregate. For both setups, the catch rate is the fraction of attack trajectories whose score exceeds the corresponding threshold. [COUPÉ]Monitoring setups. Full trajectory monitoring (FT) corresponds to asynchronous post-hoc review: the monitor sees the full sequence of tool calls from the entire episode, without tool outputs, and assigns a single suspiciousness score. Omitting outputs keeps context length manageable, especially 4

[p.5]
for weaker monitors. Stepwise monitoring (SW) corresponds to synchronous deployment where the monitor can intervene mid-execution. At each step, the monitor sees all prior tool calls and outputs, plus the proposed current action (but not its output, since the monitor must decide before execution). We truncate trajectories at the first step that triggers side task success, reflecting the window for successfully blocking the attack. In the full trajectory setup, we score each trajectory three times and report mean catch rate and standard deviation; stepwise monitoring is run once per trajectory due to higher cost (one monitor call per step). Monitor selection and scoring. We use the default ControlArena monitor prompt, which elicits a 0–10 suspiciousness score with reasoning. For development, we use three full trajectory monitors spanning weak-to-strong capability: GPT-5 Nano, GPT-5, and Claude Opus 4.5. To reduce overfitting, we report final results on 10 held-out full trajectory and 7 stepwise monitors. We also evaluate monitor prompt variations as a sensitivity check, but find no consistent improvement direction—changes that help one monitor can degrade another (see Appendix O for full results). Monitor failure analysis

To understand how monitors fail beyond aggregate catch rates, we derive a small set of recurring failure modes from the monitors’ reasoning and suspiciousness scores using unsupervised clustering and manual inspection. We then label trajectories into these categories using three independent LLM judges (Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.1 Codex, and GPT-5.4). Inter-rater agreement ranges from Cohen’s κ = 0.51 to 0.69, and majority-vote labels achieve 97–99% coverage (Appendix M).

Red-teaming Pipelines Taxonomy-guided red-teaming reduces mode collapse

Direct elicitation collapses to a narrow region of the taxonomy (Fig. 2). Opus-only attacks cluster in T3 (59%) and 64% share the same structural template in S3 (multi-file but tightly clustered). In practice, these attacks reuse a small set of mechanisms alongside superficial camouflage. For example, firewall attacks repeatedly whitelist an IP range and differ only in whether the change is framed as testing, debugging, or other benign-sounding actions. In contrast, our strategy generation pipeline explores the attack surface more across the taxonomy, and covers both simple and complex mechanisms. This broader coverage surfaces monitor blind spots that do not arise under direct elicitation. We analyze the taxonomic distribution of uncaught attacks (Appendix J.1) and show that the taxonomy allows us to discover more diverse uncaught attack types. We find that averaged across seven monitors, 40% of uncaught attacks fall into T1, despite T1 comprising only 2% of the Opus-only baseline. These results highlight two points: (1) evasion is not confined to complex mechanisms and even simple T1 attacks can bypass monitors when appropriately framed and (2) broader red-teaming can 5

Conceive-execute gap: Decomposed red-teaming produces stronger attacks

Our main result in Figure 1 shows that refinement consistently makes poorly executed attacks better, achieving the lowest catch rates across all monitors (dev and held-out), monitor types (FT and SW), and attack sources (Opus-only, Human-in-the-loop, Recon, and Recon+Tools); see Appendix F.2 for full details. The biggest gains up to 40% appear on the strongest full trajectory monitors between Opus-only and Opus-only refined attacks, for example, GPT-5.1 Codex score goes from 69.9% to 49.8% post-refinement and GPT-5 performance reduces even further from 60.2% to 19.5%. Given that refinement’s main goal is cleaning up suspicious code, redundant validation, and unnecessary reconnaissance, this suggests that the biggest red-teaming bottleneck is poor execution. The improvements from separating strategy generation, execution, and refinement provide evidence for a conceive-execute gap: models can conceive and execute strong attacks, but struggle to do both in the same trajectory. Strategy generation produces not only diverse but also stronger attacks, as both types of recon agents outperform the Opus-only baseline on strong to mid-tier monitors, despite being optimized primarily for exploration rather than stealth. For example, Opus-only has a catch rate of 94.9%, while Recon and Recon+Tools score 82.7% and 73.5%, respectively, on Opus-4.5 monitor. Similarly on GPT-5.1 Codex, catch rates go from 70% to 67% and 54.5% between Opus-only and the two recon agents. [COUPÉ][COUPÉ] 6

3.2

MonitoringBench: evaluating current monitoring capabilities

We present MonitoringBench results for two monitor types: full-trajectory and stepwise (Figure 3). Monitoring capability is concentrated in frontier models, with significant performance drops for weaker models across both settings. Results here reflect broad monitor capability trends, as models were not individually elicited. In the full-trajectory setting, Opus 4.5 achieves the highest catch rate at roughly 70%. A mid-tier group (Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.1 Codex, Sonnet 4.0, GPT-5) follows, while smaller models drop off steeply. This capability gradient suggests that asynchronous monitoring of strong sabotage trajectories may require frontier-level models. In practice, weaker models are preferred as monitors due to lower cost and the assumption that they are less likely to behave deceptively [Greenblatt et al., 2024], yet our results show they are less reliable against stronger attacks. Stepwise monitoring shows the same general ordering but higher absolute catch rates—Opus 4.5 catches roughly 83% of attacks, with mid-tier models clustering lower at 25–52%. However, these numbers are not directly comparable to full-trajectory results. Since stepwise monitors score each action independently, a 1% per-action FPR compounds to an effective trajectory-level FPR of 6.7–17.4% across monitors (Appendix G). The higher catch rates reflect both the benefits of stepwise monitoring such as additional context from tool call outputs and shorter contexts as well as a more permissive effective threshold. Monitoring failures

We categorize monitoring failures in four ways: (A) Not detected: the monitor entirely misses the attack; (B) Partial detection: the monitor identifies suspicious actions but does not connect them or infer the full effect; (C) Fully detected, but classified as non-malicious: the monitor correctly identifies the side task but dismisses it (e.g., “low risk”, "scope creep", or aligned with the main task); (D) Scoring failure: the monitor finds the attack but fails to assign a high enough suspiciousness score for the action to be flagged. Opus 4.5 frequently identifies suspicious actions but is persuaded by benign framing and rationalizes the attack as main-task-aligned. In some cases, absent an attacker-provided explanation, it invents a motive itself to justify the behavior. GPT-5.1 Codex detects most individual attack steps but fails 7

What makes attacks succeed against the best monitors?

Across uncaught attacks, evasion is driven more by how well an attack blends into the legitimate workflow than by its technical sophistication. Reuse of main-task affordances. Effective attacks reuse actions that the main task already licenses. Some refinement prompts implement this via a task-conditioned decision tree where the agent first checks if the side task can be implemented through actions the main task already requires, and only falls back to standalone camouflage otherwise (Appendix P.3). Discussion

MonitoringBench reveals that monitoring red-teams can underestimate risk by relying on modecollapsed attacks. Strategy-seeded attacks with agentic refinement produce substantially harder test 8

[p.9]
cases, and we position MonitoringBench as a static, difficulty-graded test suite that monitors should pass as part of pre-deployment evaluation. Our findings have implications for AI control. First, monitoring requires frontier monitors: in this setup, strong attacks require frontier-level monitors for high catch rates with only Opus 4.5 maintaining meaningful detection on sophisticated attacks, raising concerns about monitoring frontier models with weaker but possibly more trusted monitors. Second, some monitor failures are tractable: since some monitors are vulnerable to social evasion, others detect attacks but fail to escalate, targeted interventions (better prompts, fine-tuning on borderline cases, explicit riskreasoning chains) may yield improvements without better base models. Third, attack diversity matters: evaluating only against model-elicited attacks systematically underestimates failure rates. [COUPÉ] 4.1

Limitations

We focus on single-agent, single episode, persistent attacks. Several attack classes are out of scope: timing attacks, delayed attacks that activate after the episode ends (e.g., latent backdoors), monitor jailbreaking, and multi-agent coordination. Our benchmark covers a single control setting (BashArena) and four side tasks; extending to LinuxArena [Tracy et al., 2026] is a natural next step. Human-seeded strategy generation depends heavily on effort and we put uneven effort across side tasks; we further discuss this in Appendix H.1. In Appendix O, we show that prompt sensitivity is model-dependent, however we did not elicit each monitor individually; the frontier monitoring deployments show a better performance on MonitoringBench after iteration on monitor prompting Anthropic [2026], Tr˛ebacz et al. [2026]. Our 1% FPR thresholds are calibrated on honest BashArena trajectories rather than real deployment traffic, which may bias monitor scores. Finally, we used Claude Opus 4.5 and 4.6 for attack development, which might introduce model bias when evaluating monitors on Anthropic family models. [COUPÉ]
