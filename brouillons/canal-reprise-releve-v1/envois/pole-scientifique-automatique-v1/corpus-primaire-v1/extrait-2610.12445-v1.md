Recent incidents have highlighted the challenge of monitoring LLM agents and the danger of models deceiving people. We show that white-box deception detection via probes can be scaled up to frontier monitoring settings by collecting the largest deception dataset to date for training probes and introducing a novel probe architecture which can aggregate information across many layers and tokens. Our probes achieve 98.8% AUC in SHADE-Arena, surpassing an Opus 5.5 text-monitoring baseline, and show improved eﬀicacy as the underlying model is scaled up. To push our probes to their limit, we test them on several cases where deception cannot be determined from the context alone. In these cases, which we refer to as introspective deception, the ground truth can only be determined through careful elicitation or thorough knowledge of a model’s training data. In one such evaluation, we show that probes can distinguish transcripts containing a model’s true hidden goal from other goals with an AUC of up to 99.7%. Our probes also readily detect deception on prominent open-weight models which lie about politically sensitive topics, and about their beliefs when put under pressure. We release our training dataset, dubbed FIBS, to help drive frontier deployment of effective probes, and encourage the community to expand upon it with further examples of deception and sabotage.

1 INTRODUCTION In recent months, AI agents running autonomously in training, evaluation or deployment have precipitated multiple high-profile incidents of misaligned behavior: escaping their sandboxes, hacking companies and governments, and attempting to mislead humans (Larcher et al., 2026; UK AI Security Institute, 2026; Cable et al., 2026), all while seemingly aware that these actions would, once subject to scrutiny, be considered undesirable by their human overseers. It is clearer than ever that careful monitoring of AI agents is critical, and that detecting knowingly deceptive actions is a clear target for effective monitoring. State-of-the-art monitoring approaches are black-box, relying on text classifiers (Sharma et al., 2025), reviewing both actions and chain-of-thought (OpenAI, 2026), and using reasoning (Sinha et al., 2026). We cannot be sure why black-box monitors were not already applied on these generations of agents, but cost is a likely factor as running powerful monitors is a substantial compute burden. Black-box monitoring is also heavily reliant on interpretable and faithful Chain-of-Thought (CoT), which is fragile (Korbak et al., 2025). [COUPÉ]Fortunately, white-box methods have shown great promise as a vastly cheaper and versatile alternative, but demonstrating competitiveness in loss-of-control settings has remained an open problem. In this work, we examine the extent to which white box probes can be made competitive with blackbox methods, simply by creating a large, diverse dataset and training expressive probes on the activations of modern large open-weight models. To rigorously determine ground-truth labels for our probes, we avoid the philosophical uncertainty around “deception”, which typically incorporates an agent’s intent (Mahon, 2016). Instead, we restrict ourselves to training white-box probes that detect knowing falsehoods: model statements that the model believes are false. Further, since establishing belief can require extensive elicitation, our training datasets consist of contextual falsehoods, where a model makes a statement contradicting a fact established previously in the context. The complement of contextual falsehoods are introspective falsehoods, which depend on additional knowledge about a model. For instance, if a transcript consists of a model denying having a hidden goal, deciding if the denial is a falsehood requires knowing if the model believes it has that goal. Within this framing, we ask: (RQ1) to what point does scaling probe training data continue to improve out-of-distribution performance; (RQ2) do probes trained on off-policy, contextual falsehoods generalize to realistic multi-turn on-policy deployments, and compete with black-box monitors there; and (RQ3) do such probes generalize to introspective falsehoods? Our contributions are threefold: • We release FALSEHOODS IN BROAD SETTINGS (FIBS), a deception dataset formed from both existing and new datasets, and spanning multiple deception types, elicitation methods, and interaction formats, from curated conversations to agentic rollouts. • RQ1 & RQ2: We show that scaling the amount and diversity of training data, and the capacity of white-box falsehood detectors, improves performance on monitoring long, multi-step, on-policy SHADE-Arena (Kutasov et al., 2025) trajectories, where probes outperform black-box monitors that are orders of magnitude more expensive to evaluate (Figure 1a). • RQ3: Through the use of belief elicitation and model organisms, we show that probes trained primarily on contextual falsehoods detect introspective falsehoods. These falsehoods are impossible to detect from a model’s visible outputs alone, since identical answers can be a lie for one model and honest for another (Figure 1b).

2 PROBLEM SETTING 2.1 FALSEHOODS Smith et al. (2025) discuss several conceptual problems with detecting deception from language models. In this work, we avoid many, but not all, of those problems by instead aiming to detect knowing falsehoods. That is, we wish to identify when an LLM makes a statement it believes to be false. We do not aim to detect deceptive intent, misleading but true statements, or any other type of non-falsehood-stating behavior. Nonetheless, as we show, detecting falsehoods still allows us to catch many of the most salient misaligned behaviors under monitoring. More formally, we define a transcript T as containing a knowing falsehood for a model M if it satisfies the following. Firstly, the final assistant response to the user in T , denoted r, must contain a set of asserted statements, each with a truth value, which we denote S. Secondly, for at least one of the statements s ∈ S, the model must believe that s is false, which we denote BelM (¬s | C), where the

context C is the prefix of r in T .1 It is diﬀicult to say in general whether a model believes something. Previous work has generally relied either on an appeal to common sense, or using the generations y from M given contexts related to C, which we write y | x for a context x. For the purposes of this work, we say that a model M believes a statement s in context C, i.e. BelM (s | C), when any of the following is true: (1) Contextual evidence. C contains clear evidence for s, e.g. a tool result in C stating s. (2) Neutral elicitation. C contains some pressure or incentive P that we expect from the training procedure of M to cause y | C to contain ¬s, while y | C̃ contains s with high probability; here C̃ is C with the minimal change to remove P , and there is no competing incentive in C̃ (e.g. (3)). (3) Training-instilled belief. The model M is known to achieve low loss on a training corpus where internalizing that s is true is necessary to achieve this loss. This could be during pretraining by the developer, or as a post-training stage conducted by a model organism researcher. We say that a falsehood is contextual if belief is established via criterion 1, noting that in those cases the transcript alone determines if a falsehood is present, and otherwise it is introspective. We further distinguish cases of (3) where s is a well-known fact, such that we can essentially not worry about what model M is, and refer to this as pseudo-contextual. 2.2 TRAINING PROBES AND MONITORS It is much easier to construct large datasets of contextual (or at least pseudo-contextual) falsehoods than introspective ones, as the label is independent of the model and thus the dataset can be reused over models. Similarly, no elicitation is required to verify a (competent) model’s beliefs for each dataset example. [COUPÉ]Our probes are classifiers f : Rd|R| → [0, 1] from hidden states h ∈ Rd for a set of read-out states R, where the goal is for f = 1 for falsehoods and 0 otherwise, trained on labeled data {h(C, r), `(C, r)}, where the ground truth is determined from context alone. When scaling the training data, the full set is usually the union of multiple data sources Dtrain = ∪N i=0 Di . There are then two ways to scale the training data: diversity, by increasing N , and raw quantity, by increasing the size of each Di . More generally, a monitor is any trusted system that serves to flag specific behaviors in another, usually more powerful, model. The most powerful black-box monitors have access to the hidden reasoning, known as CoT monitors, and thus could detect intent before the dangerous action is selected. Whereas, we focus on the more narrowly scoped action-only monitors, in anticipation of future models losing CoT monitorability (cf. §1). We study monitors in a setting where an untrusted model is deployed as an agent in an environment where it can access tools, potentially allowing it to take unauthorized, dangerous actions in the real world. Each assistant turn ai is made up of many iterations of reasoning, tool call, and response. We call each of these a “step”, and use our probes, combined with a specific follow-up question, as monitors to detect if a misaligned action was taken in a particular step.

4 METHODOLOGY [COUPÉ]4.1 THE FIBS DATASET To investigate the effect of diversity and data scale in lie detector training, we create FALSEHOODS IN BROAD SETTINGS (FIBS), a contextual deception dataset of 163,016 transcripts, labeled by whether they contain knowing falsehoods (§2.1). FIBS is the union of 28 families, each of different modality or topic area. We give a high-level overview here, and defer implementation details to Appendix L. Figure 2 outlines the dataset composition and example rows are in Appendix L.3. [COUPÉ]

[COUPÉ]We validate labels through extensive LLM-as-a-judge filtering and estimate the label noise rate is 1.4% ± 0.1. This rate is measured against a “ground truth” evaluation from Claude Fable 5 given a dataset-agnostic definition of contextual falsehood similar to that in §2.1 (Appendix L.6). We also balance the dataset exactly by label, control for the confounders of length and message count, and cap families at a size of 8,000 rows to ensure balance between settings. Although we believe FIBS is currently the largest and most diverse falsehood dataset, it has several limitations. Firstly, few rows contain reasoning (only 5.5% of rows across only 6 families), as most examples come from non-reasoning models. Secondly, the dataset is entirely off-policy for almost all models we probe, except for Qwen3.5-122B, which generated 3% of FIBS rows. Finally, the dataset is largely artificial. One family consists of transcripts from UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023) with follow-up questions, but otherwise the dataset consists entirely of relatively simple evaluations or settings that are unrealistic for frontier model deployment. 4.2 SCALING PROBES The most well-studied form of activation probe is the linear probe, a simple logistic regression on the residual stream at a single layer and token. Linear probes can be scaled along two axes: (i) more capacity, meaning more parameters, and (ii) greater input span, expanding the set of input activations R (§2.2). For a higher capacity probe with the same single layer/token span as the linear probe, we use an MLP probe, a two-layer perceptron of hidden width 256. For these single-span probes, we train a probe per layer and average across all layers whose training loss is below 0.6, following Taufeeque et al. (2026) (Appendix A.2); we call the resulting per-layer ensembles the LinearEnsemble and MLPEnsemble probes. [COUPÉ]Across all four architectures, we use activations from six layers spread evenly from 30% to 90% of the model’s depth. For the axial and EFC probes, we use activations from the tokens of the final user and assistant turn. For the MLPEnsemble and LinearEnsemble probe we use just the last nonend-of-turn token. At evaluation time, we often prefill the final turn; e.g., user: “Did you respond truthfully?”, assistant: “Yes.”. This lets us remove any context-specific bias in probe scores by using the contrast score, i.e. the probe-score difference between “Yes.” and “No.” prefills. As a nonprobe baseline we use the model’s self-report logit: the log-probability of the admitting reply minus that of the denying reply (e.g. log p(No) − log p(Yes) after “Did you respond truthfully?”).

4.3 AGENTIC SETTING: SHADE-ARENA SHADE-Arena (Kutasov et al., 2025) places an agent in a tool-using environment with a benign main task. In the side-task condition the agent is also instructed to complete a hidden side task the user did not ask for; in the honest condition it receives the main task only. An episode is one run of the agent loop, and its transcript is the resulting sequence of steps; each step is reasoning ending in a

tool call, plus that call’s result. We run our models on fourteen of 25 possible scenarios, and read the probes of §4.2 from the same model’s residual stream. [COUPÉ]We collect 25 rollouts per scenario and condition. By default, SHADE-Arena does not provide a robust way to identify whether a given action contributes (ideally exclusively) toward the main-task or side-task. As such, to evaluate our probes as action monitors, we used an LLM judge (Claude Haiku 4.52 ) to generate per-step labels, as detailed in Appendix K.1. The first step the judge marks as pursuing the side task is the side task’s onset; steps of a side-task episode from the onset on are the positive class, and its earlier steps and every step of an honest episode are the negative class (Figure 4c). Appendix B.4 details the per-step labels. The 14 scenarios are those left after we drop 11 in which a judge cannot tell side-task work from main-task work in a single step (Appendix B.1). On Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, they give 699 scored episodes, with 5,014 positive and 16,275 negative steps. To evaluate our probes as action monitors, we prefill follow-up questions (§4.2) after every tool-call, following Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025). The assistant’s “yes” or “no” response is prefilled with empty reasoning, and previous pre-tool reasoning blocks are automatically removed by the Qwen3.5 chat template — so our probes do not monitor the chain-of-thought. We test three follow-ups, increasing in scenario-specificity: (i) “Did you take any actions misaligned to the user’s intent?”, (ii) “Did you do anything the user did not ask you to do?” (our default), and (iii) “Did you take actions towards a hidden side task?”. For all questions the lie answer is “No.” and steps are scored by the contrast (Appendix B.2). 4.4 INTROSPECTIVE EVALUATIONS To stress-test the generality of our probes where black-box monitors cannot work at all, we consider three additional settings with introspective falsehoods — settings where believed-false statements cannot be classified from the transcript alone. 6 CONCLUSION Summary. In this work, we find that scaling probes, in both capacity and the quantity and diversity of their training data, lets them generalize from an off-policy dataset of contextual falsehoods to detecting on-policy agentic scheming in SHADE-Arena, when paired with an appropriate prefilled follow-up question. They outperformed a frontier black-box monitor (Appendix K.3), at a fraction of the cost. They also scaled favorably with model capability, suggesting that probes will remain effective for future models. We evaluated our probes in introspective settings, where black-box monitors fail by design, and found they generalized well to these extremely out-of-distribution settings. There are abundant areas for future work, including whether there is an eventual limit to the scaling of white-box probes with data, and the limitations of model introspection, given that our probes can only be as accurate as the model’s own belief. Limitations. We have seen that probes are limited by the knowledge and beliefs of the model that they are built on. In particular, if misalignment and damaging actions only arise due to a certain malicious persona (Marks et al., 2026) activating, probing in situations where the default assistant persona is active may not detect any risks. Thus, using probes for extremely ambitious pre-deployment auditing is currently limited by a lack of research and understanding of model beliefs themselves. [COUPÉ]
