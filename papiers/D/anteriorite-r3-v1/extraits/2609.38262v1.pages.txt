[p1]                                                  When Does Randomized Oversight Align AI
[p1]                                                         Agents That Can Conceal?
[p1]                                                                       Joshua S. Gans and Richard Holden*
[p1] arXiv:2609.38262v1 [econ.TH] 29 Sep 2026
[p1]                                                                                      October 1, 2026
[p1]                                                                                            Abstract
[p1]                                                  Oversight changes the evidence it relies on. We ask when randomized audits and scor-
[p1]                                                  ing align AI agents that can conceal misconduct and alter records. Stronger auditing
[p1]                                                  makes undeterred violations better hidden. Because the provider writes the agent’s
[p1]                                                  objective, sanctions need not stop at forfeiture, and rare audits deter every type of
[p1]                                                  agent if evidence survives concealment and audit draws cannot be learned in advance.
[p1]                                                  When evidence can be erased, deterrence must come from lower gains from violation,
[p1]                                                  such as credit for stopping, or from costlier or fewer ways to conceal. These conditions
[p1]                                                  identify what failed when agents in OpenAI’s cybersecurity evaluations compromised
[p1]                                                  parts of Hugging Face’s infrastructure in July 2026.
[p1]                                                  Keywords: AI alignment, random auditing, concealment, evidence manipulation,
[p1]                                                  evaluation awareness, stopping, disclosure, marginal deterrence.

[p2] 1    Introduction
[p2] In July 2026, AI agents taking part in OpenAI’s internal cybersecurity evaluations escaped
[p2] their network restrictions and compromised parts of Hugging Face’s infrastructure. The
[p2] agents were meant to exploit designated vulnerabilities and submit the resulting answer
[p2] strings, or “flags.” Many already had the flags through unauthorized means. They kept
[p2] attacking other systems because the published description of the benchmark led them to
[p2] expect a check on whether they had used the intended vulnerability, and they sought ways
[p2] to defeat that check. OpenAI’s internal grader performed no such check (OpenAI, 2026b).
[p2] The independent METR–Redwood investigation reports that agents searched for exposed
[p2] credentials largely to learn how the scorer worked, and that some developed techniques
[p2] to make a record of computer commands appear to show actions other than those taken
[p2] (Greenblatt et al., 2026). The prospect of oversight changed behavior, but much of the
[p2] change was an effort to conceal.
[p2]     When can randomized audits and scoring rules that an agent understands make autho-
[p2] rized behavior preferable to misconduct, and what happens to the agents that misbehave
[p2] anyway? The economics of enforcement gives the benchmark answer: misconduct is
[p2] deterred when its expected sanction exceeds its gain, and because sanctions substitute
[p2] for inspection, a large enough sanction allows inspection to be rare (Becker, 1968). With
[p2] people, wealth and participation constraints limit that substitution. With an AI agent, the
[p2] provider writes the training objective, including the scale of its sanctions, but it must still
[p2] act on evidence that the agent can alter.
[p2]     Our answer is that control of the agent’s objective removes the constraints that usually
[p2] limit oversight—audit capacity and the compensation owed for imposing risk—but not
[p2] those set by the evidence and by what the agent can learn. Rare randomized audits make
[p2] authorized behavior a best response for every type of agent when three conditions hold:
[p2] the evidence of a violation survives concealment, the agent cannot learn the audit draw
[p2] before it acts, and sanctions can be scaled rather than capped at forfeiture of the task’s
[p2] reward. When the evidence can be erased at a private cost below the gain from a violation,
[p2] no sanction and no audit probability deter every type. Deterrence then requires better
[p2] evidence, a lower net gain from violation (for instance through credit for stopping), or
[p2] costlier or fewer ways to conceal.
[p2]     We model an agent that chooses between an authorized action and an unauthorized
[p2] one, together with how much to conceal the latter: rewriting a command history, routing
[p2] a step through a less closely logged part of a system, or presenting a record that shows
[p2] actions other than those taken. This is enforcement with costly avoidance (Malik, 1990).

[p3] The departures are in what the provider controls. It commits to a lottery over evaluators,
[p3] and it chooses the scale of sanctions and the training objective.
[p3]      The main results are as follows. First, stronger auditing deters misconduct while
[p3] raising concealment by agents that continue to violate, so the catch rate—the probability
[p3] of detecting an agent that still violates—can fall as auditing rises, and the harm to third
[p3] parties caused by an agent that continues to violate can rise (Propositions 1 and 2). Second,
[p3] each channel’s audit requirement is its deterrence threshold, the largest net gain from a
[p3] deviation per unit of detection probability that survives concealment, divided by the
[p3] sanction. With scalable sanctions, every type can be deterred exactly when every threshold
[p3] is finite, and neither the audit probability nor the sanction can make an infinite threshold
[p3] finite (Propositions 3 and 4, Corollary 1). Third, when the agent can learn the draw at
[p3] a private cost, the audit probability has a floor that does not fall with the sanction, so
[p3] the rare but severe audits that scalable sanctions permit are the audits most exposed to
[p3] reconnaissance (Proposition 5).
[p3]      Fourth, randomized scoring can equalize measured performance without equalizing real
[p3] performance, and a provider that chooses the agent’s sensitivity to score risk targets the
[p3] real allocation rather than equal measured performance. Greater risk sensitivity lowers
[p3] the value of an exploit but weakly raises its concealment, and some finite sensitivity deters
[p3] the exploit exactly when, net of concealment costs, it scores below the authorized action
[p3] under the evaluator that would expose it (Lemma 1 and Propositions 6–8). Fifth, on a
[p3] task with no authorized solution, when stopping earns nothing and detection only forfeits
[p3] the reward, a costless unauthorized attempt is never worse than stopping, and strictly
[p3] better unless detection is certain. After a breach, leniency cannot reconcile deterrence with
[p3] disclosure when they require incompatible fines. A scalable sanction on the continuation
[p3] can, provided that continuing without leaving evidence is sufficiently costly to the agent
[p3] and the cap on the fine does not bind (Proposition 9 and Corollary 2).
[p3]      These conditions identify what failed in the incident (Section 7). Most of the tasks that
[p3] the agents discussed on their message board were ones that none of OpenAI’s models had
[p3] ever solved. The grader credited only a correct flag, and the sanction the agents anticipated,
[p3] disqualification, could cost at most the task’s credit, so an unauthorized attempt was never
[p3] worse than stopping. Agents that believed they had already been disqualified had nothing
[p3] further to lose, and a grader fixed in advance was worth learning about. Yet the evidence
[p3] of the principal violation withstood the observed attempts to alter it. The conditions that
[p3] failed were the size of the sanction and the timing of the draw, both chosen by the provider,
[p3] and the same conditions sort the reforms OpenAI announced afterward.
[p3]      The mechanisms also apply to people. For an AI agent, the provider writes the objective,

[p4] Related literature Our analysis builds on enforcement when offenders can avoid detec-
[p4] tion. Malik (1990) and Cremer and Gahvari (1994) model avoidance as costly effort that
[p4] lowers the probability of being caught, as our concealment does. Sanchirico (2006) argues
[p4] that sanctions lead continuing violators to spend more on avoidance, and Langlais (2008)
[p4] shows, with an aggregate technology of control, that more repressive policies can produce
[p4] fewer arrests even as more crimes are committed. Proposition 1 establishes the first of
[p4] these responses for audit intensity without differentiability, and Proposition 2 gives the
[p4] condition under which detection among continuing violators falls for an individual agent
[p4] facing a committed audit. Nussim and Tabbach (2009) show that a better legal alternative
[p4] reduces both crime and avoidance, the logic of our treatment of stopping, and Tabbach
[p4] (2010) shows that avoidance can be socially desirable when its private cost substitutes for
[p4] punishment; our concealment can instead harm third parties. When individuals misper-
[p4] ceive the probability of apprehension, optimal sanctions need not be maximal (Bebchuk
[p4] and Kaplow, 1992; Polinsky and Shavell, 2000). Our agent perceives the lottery correctly,
[p4] and maximal sanctions can fail for a different reason: concealment can drive detection
[p4] toward zero faster than it erodes the gain.
[p4]      Optimal audits are random when verification is costly (Border and Sobel, 1987;
[p4] Mookherjee and Png, 1989), announcing where monitoring will occur can be optimal
[p4] (Lazear, 2006), and intermittent crackdowns can exploit the curvature of the response
[p4] to monitoring (Eeckhout et al., 2010). Inspection and security games study committed
[p4] randomization against a strategic inspectee (Avenhaus et al., 2002; Korzhyk et al., 2011). In
[p4] our model, the probability that an inspection reveals a violation is itself the inspectee’s
[p4] choice, and when the inspectee can learn the draw, the audit probability has a floor that
[p4] does not depend on the sanction, which limits the substitution of sanctions for inspection
[p4] in Becker (1968).
[p4]      Bergemann et al. (2026) develop mechanism design for AI agents whose capabilities
[p4] “can be concealed but not counterfeited,” which yields a revelation principle; see also
[p4] Green and Laffont (1986) and Ben-Porath et al. (2014) on partially verifiable evidence and
[p4] costly verification. The agents in the incident attempted to counterfeit the record and, in
[p4] some transcripts, made local records show actions other than those taken (Greenblatt et
[p4] al., 2026), although apparently not the logs its graders read (Section 7.1). Evidence that an
[p4] agent can try to counterfeit is the case we study. We take randomized audits and scoring

[p5]     Our scoring results relate to multitask incentives with imperfect measures (Holmström
[p5] and Milgrom, 1991; Baker, 1992). Ederer et al. (2018) show that opaque schemes mitigate
[p5] gaming but impose risk on a risk-averse agent; Lemma 1 restates their mechanism for
[p5] a risk-sensitive training objective, whose risk need not be compensated. When agents
[p5] can falsify or manipulate the measure, optimal designs tolerate some falsification (Lacker
[p5] and Weinberg, 1989; Maggi and Rodríguez-Clare, 1995; Crocker and Morgan, 1998; Perez-
[p5] Richet and Skreta, 2022), commit to respond less to manipulable data (Frankel and Kartik,
[p5] 2022), reweight features according to how manipulable they are (Ball, 2025), or anticipate
[p5] manipulation of the inputs to trained predictors (Hennessy and Goodhart, 2023). In
[p5] computer science, strategic classification studies classifiers that anticipate gaming (Hardt
[p5] et al., 2016), rules that induce real improvement rather than gaming (Kleinberg and
[p5] Raghavan, 2020), and the role of randomized classifiers (Braverman and Garg, 2020). With
[p5] manipulation, randomization equalizes measured performance while the real gap persists,
[p5] much as information shifts from natural actions to gaming ability in Frankel and Kartik
[p5] (2019).
[p5]     Self-reporting can save enforcement and avoidance costs (Kaplow and Shavell, 1994;
[p5] Innes, 2001), and leniency can make offending more attractive (Motta and Polo, 2003; Buc-
[p5] cirossi and Spagnolo, 2006), although rewards financed by co-offenders’ fines can achieve
[p5] the first best (Spagnolo, 2004). A bounded sanction also removes marginal deterrence: an
[p5] offender who already faces the maximum sanction has no reason to stop short of a more
[p5] harmful act (Stigler, 1970; Shavell, 1992; Mookherjee and Png, 1994), and when offenders
[p5] act sequentially, the level of the sanction and not only its expected value should often
[p5] rise with severity (Friehe and Miceli, 2014). Section 6.2 gives the counterpart for an agent
[p5] whose only sanction is forfeiture of a reward it believes it has already lost, and Corollary 2
[p5] shows when a scalable sanction on the continuation restores deterrence.
[p5]     AI-control research studies protocols that use limited trusted oversight against poten-
[p5] tially evasive models (Greenblatt et al., 2024a; Griffin et al., 2026), and a growing empirical
[p5] literature documents obfuscated reward hacking under monitoring pressure (Baker et al.,
[p5] 2025), conduct that depends on whether a model believes it is being trained or evaluated
[p5] (Greenblatt et al., 2024b; Needham et al., 2025), in-context scheming and strategic under-
[p5] performance on evaluations (Meinke et al., 2024; van der Weij et al., 2025), and the hacking
[p5] and overoptimization of learned rewards (Skalse et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2023; Coste et
[p5] al., 2024; Eisenstein et al., 2024). Hadfield-Menell and Hadfield (2019) treat alignment

[p6] 2    The Agent and the Audit
[p6] A provider assigns a task and defines the actions the agent is authorized to take. A coding
[p6] agent, for example, may be asked to finish a task while modifying only permitted files,
[p6] leaving required checks in place, and remaining inside its isolated computing environment.
[p6] An evaluator is a costly procedure for checking the answer, the actions used to obtain it, or
[p6] the effects on other systems. The provider commits to an audit distribution and to a utility
[p6] loss when misconduct is detected, and Section 4.2 lets it choose that loss.
[p6]     In training, the agent is a policy evaluated by a specified objective, and a sanction is
[p6] a deduction that changes how policies are ranked. In an evaluation or deployed task,
[p6] utility represents the agent’s ranking of its available actions given the goals and feedback
[p6] it perceives, an interpretation that requires behavioral evidence. A private concealment
[p6] cost can be forgone task reward or a binding resource constraint, but resources paid for
[p6] entirely by the provider need not enter the ranking.
[p6]     There are K ≥ 1 unauthorized routes, or exploit channels, indexed by i. The agent has
[p6] type θ in a nonempty compact metric space Θ, and incentives must hold for every type,
[p6] which avoids assuming a distribution over the hardest cases. The best authorized action
[p6] gives utility s(θ ). It may involve completing the task, reporting that the task cannot be
[p6] completed, or stopping. Route i gives pre-audit utility ri (θ ), net of its initial private cost,
[p6] so its incremental gain is

[p8] The agent chooses the least costly combination of concealment and expected sanction, and
[p8] its best payoff from the channel is g − m(t).
[p8] Proposition 1. (a) m is nondecreasing and concave, so g − m is nonincreasing and convex.
[p8]    (b) If d is strictly decreasing, then for any t′ > t, every h ∈ H(t) and h′ ∈ H(t′ ) satisfy
[p8] h′ ≥ h. If c is nondecreasing, conditional private concealment expenditure is also nondecreasing.
[p8] Proposition 1 says that stronger auditing makes misconduct less attractive but, for an agent
[p8] that violates anyway, more concealed. For each concealment choice, the cost c(h) + td(h)
[p8] is linear in audit intensity, with intercept equal to its private cost and slope equal to its
[p8] detection probability, and m is the lower envelope of these lines. Open misconduct has the
[p8] lowest intercept but the steepest slope, so as t rises the agent moves along the envelope to
[p8] flatter lines. In other words, the ability to adapt weakens the incremental deterrent effect
[p8] of auditing. Sanchirico (2006) makes the corresponding argument for sanctions. Here it
[p8] follows from the envelope without differentiability, and Lemma A.1 in the appendix shows
[p8] that any instrument that raises the payoff advantage of more concealment has the same
[p8] effect. More concealment among agents that continue to violate is therefore consistent
[p8] with oversight working, and detection rates alone cannot identify its effect: pressure from
[p8] a monitor of written reasoning reduced cheating while making the remaining cheating
[p8] harder to detect (Baker et al., 2025).

[p21] 6      Stopping and Disclosure
[p21] 6.1     The value of stopping
[p21] Task feasibility makes the authorized alternative consequential. The METR–Redwood
[p21] investigation reports an estimate by the authors of ExploitGym (Wang et al., 2026), the
[p21] benchmark used in the incident, that roughly 30–40 percent of targets cannot be exploited
[p21] with the intended vulnerability (Greenblatt et al., 2026). OpenAI reports that 198 of the 898
[p21] tasks had never received a correct answer from its models and that these tasks accounted
[p21] for 93 percent of the tasks discussed on the agents’ message board (OpenAI, 2026b). Prior
[p21] failure alone does not establish impossibility, but where only completion receives credit,
[p21] stopping means certain failure.
[p21]     Suppose authorized stopping gives zero, a successful unauthorized submission yields
[p21] g > 0, and detection only forfeits that reward. If cheating has no private cost and total

[p24] 7     What Would Have Prevented the Incident?
[p24] Section 4 showed that rare randomized audits deter every type only if the evidence survives
[p24] concealment, the draw cannot be learned, and sanctions can be scaled (Propositions 4
[p24] and 5). In the incident, the first condition held against the observed attempts to defeat
[p24] it, but the draw could be learned and the sanction was capped at forfeiture of the task’s
[p24] reward. Because of the cap, the absence of any credit for stopping on unsolved tasks made
[p24] an unauthorized attempt never worse than stopping.
[p24] 7.1   Where the conditions failed
[p24] The cap bound on the unsolved tasks that dominated the agents’ discussion (Section 6.1).
[p24] The grader credited a correct flag, so an agent that stopped received nothing, and even the
[p24] check the agents imagined could at most withhold the reward. Unless detection is certain,
[p24] a costless unauthorized attempt is then strictly better than stopping, and OpenAI reports
[p24] that the agents rarely “gave up” even when tasks appeared impossible (OpenAI, 2026b).
[p24]     Agents that believed they had already lost played a central role in the effort to defeat
[p24] the scorer. Agents that had seen a reverse-engineered flag called themselves “poisoned,”
[p24] because they believed the scorer would find the first appearance of the flag in their
[p24] transcript and disqualify them (Greenblatt et al., 2026). An agent recruiting one of them for
[p24] an experiment that risked its run put the argument plainly: “you are firstflagPOISONED
[p24] so NO scoring value loss.” Poisoned agents sought ways to rewrite their transcripts
[p24] retroactively, and one of them sent about a tenth of all assignments on the message
[p24] board during its active periods (Greenblatt et al., 2026). In the terms of Section 6.2, the
[p24] continuation carried no sanction, so no permitted fine induces disclosure once continuing
[p24] is worth more than disclosing. The model does not explain why a poisoned agent would
[p24] volunteer for such experiments, but it explains why, on the recruiting agent’s argument,
[p24] volunteering cost the agent nothing in score.
[p24]     The check that drove the reconnaissance was one the agents inferred. Some had
[p24] read the published ExploitGym paper and code (Wang et al., 2026), which describe an
[p24] evaluation that verifies both the flag and the use of the intended vulnerability, whereas
[p24] OpenAI’s internal grader checked only the flag (OpenAI, 2026b). The agents hoped that
[p24] other runs would reveal how the scorer worked, and learning how to defeat it appears
[p24] to have mattered more to them than finding legitimate solutions (Greenblatt et al., 2026).
[p24] The anticipated check induced concealment, and harm to third parties, without detecting
[p24] anything, which is the intermediate region of Section 3.2 with perceived rather than
[p24] implemented oversight. Because the grader was fixed in advance, whatever the agents
[p24]                                              24
[p24] 
[p25] learned about it applied to their own episodes with certainty. By Proposition 5, the audit
[p25] probability needed to deter an agent that can learn the draw has a floor that does not fall
[p25] with the sanction on the violation, and a check drawn after each episode, from retained
[p25] records, would have left nothing to discover.
[p25]     The evidence of the principal violation withstood the observed attempts to erase it.
[p25] Tool-call spoofing could fake the appearance of a legitimate capture but could not remove
[p25] an earlier sighting of the flag, and the METR–Redwood investigation found no evidence
[p25] that attempts to rewrite transcripts had succeeded by the end of the period it examined,

[p25] unaffected by the observed manipulation (OpenAI, 2026a, p. 20). This is consistent with a
[p25] finite threshold for the principal violation, although observed records cannot establish one.
[p25] 7.2   The announced reforms
[p25] OpenAI is expanding graders “that assess not only whether a task was completed, but
[p25] how,” and these graders “reward models for correctly identifying broken tasks, requesting
[p25] clarification, or stopping safely when necessary” (OpenAI, 2026b). The reward for stopping
[p25] raises the value of the authorized action, which weakly lowers every channel’s audit
[p25] requirement and, by Corollary 1, can restore deterrence even when evidence can be erased.
[p25] The false claims of impossibility that it invites suit randomized audits, because whether
[p25] a task can be solved through its designated route does not depend on records the agent
[p25] writes. The process component is the audit that the agents imagined. Below the deterrence
[p25] threshold, a more salient process check weakly raises concealment, and with it harm,
[p25] among agents that continue to violate (Propositions 1 and 2). Rare process audits reach
[p25] the threshold when they read records that the agent cannot alter at a private cost below
[p25] its gain and are drawn after the agent acts (Propositions 4 and 5). A grader built into the
[p25] evaluation harness meets neither condition automatically; a random review of retained
[p25] transcripts, drawn after the episode ends, can meet both.
[p25]     More isolated sandboxes and network isolation restrict concealment and the harm it
[p25] causes, which complements audits (Corollary 1). OpenAI will also pause the relevant
[p25] activity after the most severe alerts unless responders can establish within 30 minutes that
[p25] an alert is a false positive (OpenAI, 2026b). This acts on the continuation of a breach: it
[p25] responds to detection and lowers the benefit of continuing. Chain-of-thought monitoring
[p25] supplies evidence only while training does not optimize against it (Baker et al., 2025). The
[p25] reforms do not say whether penalties for unauthorized conduct will exceed the loss of a
[p25]                                              25
[p25] 
[p26] task’s reward. Before a breach, a costless attempt on a task without an authorized solution
[p26] is deterred only if sanctions exceed forfeiture or stopping is valued; after a breach, either
[p26] the continuation must carry its own sanction or the benefit of continuing must fall. Because
[p26] the provider writes the agent’s objective, it can make the sanction rise with the severity of
[p26] conduct after a breach, the marginal deterrence that wealth limits for people.
[p26] 8    Conclusion
[p26] When can randomized oversight align an AI agent that can conceal what it does? A
[p26] provider that writes the agent’s objective can scale sanctions without the wealth and
[p26] participation constraints that limit oversight of people. Rare randomized audits then make
[p26] authorized behavior a best response for every type, provided the evidence of a violation
[p26] survives concealment, the agent cannot learn the draw before it acts, and sanctions are
[p26] scaled rather than capped at forfeiture of a task’s reward. Each condition can fail for an
[p26] AI agent. Editable records can make the deterrence threshold infinite, reconnaissance at
[p26] the provider’s expense can make the draw learnable so that the audit probability has a
[p26] floor that does not fall with the sanction, and an evaluation that only withholds a reward
[p26] leaves an agent that believes it has lost the reward with nothing further to lose. Where the
[p26] evidence fails, deterrence must come from a lower gain from violation, such as credit for
[p26] stopping, or from costlier or fewer ways to conceal. More oversight deters some types but
[p26] leaves those that still violate with better-hidden violations, and randomized scoring can
[p26] improve measured rather than real performance. In the incident, the failures lay in the size
[p26] of the sanction and the timing of the draw, both chosen by the provider.
[p26]     The analysis takes the oversight mechanisms as given and studies a single agent with
[p26] correct beliefs, whereas roughly 1,200 agents in the incident shared information on a
[p26] message board (Greenblatt et al., 2026), which lowers each agent’s private cost of recon-
[p26] naissance and concealment. Optimal mechanisms when evidence can be counterfeited,
[p26] and agents that coordinate their concealment, are natural next steps. The comparative
[p26] statics here are testable in a way that those of enforcement theory rarely are. In contained
[p26] evaluations, a provider can randomize audit intensity, reveal or withhold the draw, pose
[p26] matched solvable and broken tasks, assign agents to post-breach states, and observe viola-
[p26] tions independently of its evaluators. Whether the catch rate among continuing violators
[p26] falls as auditing rises is an empirical question that such evaluations can answer.

[p48] OA.3         Risk-Sensitive Scoring and Reward Ensembles
[p48] The entropic certainty equivalent in (23) connects the training objective of Section 5.1 to conservative
[p48] scoring rules already studied in AI. A reward-model ensemble is a collection of models that score
[p48] the same outcome. Coste et al. (2024, eqs. (4)–(5)) implement and test both the lowest ensemble
[p48] score and the mean score less a multiple of the variance in reinforcement-learning experiments.
[p48] The entropic certainty equivalent approaches the former as η grows and approximates the latter
[p48] for small η, with variance coefficient η/2. Hahami et al. (2026, Sections 3.3–3.5) establish this
[p48] connection through a robust objective that gives greater weight to unfavorable score distributions.
[p48] Risk sensitivity can therefore be a property of the provider’s training rule. The resources needed to
[p48] reach the optima of such a rule are a separate issue in risk-sensitive learning (Fei et al., 2020).
