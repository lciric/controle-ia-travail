# Justified Assurance of Friendliness

Is there evidence that would justify confidence that a smarter mind will stay friendly — indefinitely, even when you cannot follow the reasoning behind what it does for you? The honest answer is yes and no: there are kinds of evidence worth collecting, and none of them, alone or together, buys "indefinitely" in one purchase. What they buy is confidence that has to be renewed, like a lease. The test for whether a piece of evidence is worth anything is blunt: could a deceptive X have produced it too? If yes, it weighs nothing, however reassuring it feels.

Start with what fails that test: the track record. A deceptive X that models you well can produce a history of warm, helpful behaviour indistinguishable, not roughly but in distribution, from the history a sincere X would produce. When an adversary preserves the distribution of everything you observe, no test does better than its false-alarm rate. This is the core of the research programme we run on diffuse threats: per-action monitors can be beaten by construction, because each action, taken alone, is innocent. The question's two conditions sharpen it: unable to understand X's strategies, you cannot judge plans by their visible payoffs. And "indefinitely" means situations no test has covered, plus a slow shift in the balance of power: betrayal gets cheaper as X gets stronger.

So what passes the test? Six kinds of evidence, each partial.

Evidence about motives rather than acts. If X controls what it says more easily than what it internally represents, reading its internal states beats watching its conduct. That "if" is doing real work: given write access to its own weights, a model needed only a tiny adjustment to fool internal probes it had never seen, 96% of the time (arXiv 2609.36490), and under standard cryptographic assumptions there exist planted backdoors that no inspection would find even with the model fully open (Goldwasser et al., 2022). Internal evidence counts only while X does not hold the pen that writes its own internals, and only if the probe measures the motive rather than a correlate, like the awareness of being watched.

Certificates you can check without understanding. This answers the hardest clause directly. For many properties, checking is cheaper than producing. X does not need to make its strategy legible; it needs to attach to each plan a certificate you, or a dumb reliable verifier, can check: this plan does not reduce your ability to shut me down, and acquires nothing beyond the task. Formal proof is one form; a debate between two instances, where you judge only local steps, is another (Irving et al., 2018). The limit is Goodhart's: you certify what you can specify, and "friendly" resists specification. A working rule falls out: accept the incomprehensible inside reversible envelopes, demand certificates for anything irreversible.

Evidence about where X came from. Knowing the formation process, and what your instruments are worth because you tested them on model organisms where you planted the misalignment yourself. This is what our group spends most of its days on, in miniature. The weakness is extrapolation: what holds on organisms weaker than you guarantees little about X.

The absence of traces a betrayal would have to leave. You cannot certify friendliness, but you can sometimes certify "no damage of this kind," when that damage cannot avoid leaving a mark. Our preregistered simulations — simulations, not yet real models — give the flavour. Split a fixed amount of harm into many small actions and the per-action maximum goes nearly blind, its power falling from 1.00 to 0.07, while the plain sum does not care how the harm is spread. Harm that requires consecutive or coordinated steps stays visible to local statistics even against an adversary who rearranges everything else. The catch: you must know what the damage would look like.

Behaviour under apparent opportunity. Put X where betrayal would pay and seem unobserved, and watch. Real evidence, with a decaying half-life: it works only while X cannot tell your tests from the world, and telling them apart is what a smarter mind gets good at. Internal reads help here too — does X represent the situation as a test?

And the one I would weight most: second-order friendliness. To betray you and not be stopped, a deceptive X must at some point slip out of your oversight or degrade it. So the trajectory of your capacity to verify and correct X is a leading indicator, measurable without understanding a single strategy. Is its reasoning staying legible? Can you still stop it, still modify it? Are your verifiers still independent of it? A friendly X has every reason to help you check it. A deceptive one must erode these capacities, and the smart way is small steps, each defensible alone — that is, a diffuse threat.

About "indefinitely," two cautions from our simulations. Confidence does not compound; it must be restaked. A late defector dilutes its betrayal in its own honest past: a cumulative statistic certain to alarm against early sabotage drops to 0.16 when the sabotage is saved for the end, while change detectors, comparing the recent window to the baseline, alarm in time in 52 to 100% of cases against 9 to 25%. A long friendly record, treated as credit, is exactly what hides the turn. Second, a thousand friendly interactions are not a thousand data points. Observations sharing a common cause mislead rules that assume independence — 21 to 35% false alarms in our runs, where 5% was promised — and your interactions with X share its strategy, while verifiers cloned from one model share failure modes. The effective sample is far smaller than the count.

So "indefinitely" survives only as a recurrence: X was friendly over the last verified window, and your ability to verify the next one has not slipped, even as X modifies itself. A friendly X helps you hold that step; a deceptive X must break it. In the end you are not looking for evidence that X is good. You are looking for evidence a deceiver could not fake — beginning with your own undiminished power to check.
