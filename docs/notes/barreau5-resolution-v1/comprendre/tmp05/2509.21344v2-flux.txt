White-box monitors are a popular technique
for detecting potentially harmful behaviours in
language models. While they perform well
in general, their effectiveness in detecting textambiguous behaviour is disputed. In this work,
we find evidence that removing textual evidence
of a behaviour significantly decreases probe performance. The AUROC reduction ranges from 10to 30-point depending on the setting. We evaluate
probe monitors across three setups (Sandbagging,
Sycophancy, and Bias), finding that when probes
rely on textual evidence of the target behaviour
(such as system prompts or CoT reasoning), performance degrades once these tokens are filtered.
This filtering procedure is standard practice for
output monitor evaluation. As further evidence
of this phenomenon, we train Model Organisms
which produce outputs without any behaviour verbalisations. We validate that probe performance
on Model Organisms is substantially lower than
unfiltered evaluations: 0.57 vs 0.74 AUROC for
Bias, and 0.57 vs 0.94 AUROC for Sandbagging.
Our findings suggest that linear probes may be
brittle in scenarios where they must detect nonsurface-level patterns.

We find that for all the behaviors we studied, probe performance drops substantially when textual evidence is removed
from the transcript, suggesting that probes may rely on what
we term leakage.
Definition 1.1 (Leakage). The phenomenon by which
probes rely on activations from token positions that contain textual evidence of the studied behavior. Examples
include activations from the elicitation prompt (which we
term elicitation leakage) or from positions where the model
explicitly reasons about performing the behavior (which we
term reasoning leakage).
To our knowledge, this is the first paper to systematically
explore and quantify this evaluation bias in linear probes.
Prior work has demonstrated that probes can achieve high
performance on behavior detection tasks (Anthropic, 2024;
Marks et al., 2025), but has not controlled for whether this
performance stems from detecting internal representations
or from exploiting leakage.
Controlling for leakage is important given that textual evidence of this kind is common in current evaluation scenarios,
for two main reasons. First, training and evaluating these
monitoring systems requires models that exhibit the very
harmful behaviors the monitors are designed to detect. Since
current models are trained to be helpful, harmless, and honest (Askell et al., 2021), they do not inherently display many
of the most concerning behaviors, additionally some behaviors may only manifest in systems more capable than those
currently available. In practice, models are often prompted
or trained to deliberately exhibit the harmful behavior we
seek to study. This elicitation process, while creating meaningful test cases, might leave artifacts that monitors could
be using to boost their effectiveness (elicitation leakage).
Second, when conducting safety relevant behaviors, models often explicitly verbalize their harmful intentions and
actions. These articulations make the deceptive behavior
extremely salient, allowing monitors to achieve high performance by detecting surface-level cues rather than discerning
genuine deceptive intent (reasoning leakage).

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
• We replicate and extend prior probe evaluations to three

• We propose and apply a framework to evaluate the contribution of clear textual evidence, such as elicitation

3. Methodology
We now describe the architecture of our monitors (§3.1),
the datasets used for training (§3.2), the scenarios used
for evaluation (§3.3) and the mitigation strategies used to
address leakage (§3.4).

The choice of removing parts of the transcript is not problematic on its own, and all the cited work has compelling
reasons to edit the transcripts. However, it is only fair then
to do the same when evaluating white-box monitors. Unfortunately, removing such textual evidence from the model
internals is not as easy, as many concepts are entangled,
attention heads move information to other tokens, and removing key parts of the text could put the model out of
distribution, making the results unreliable.

3.1. Probes
Following prior work (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Papadatos & Freedman, 2024), we employ linear probes as
our monitors. Concretely, we use scikit-learn’s (Pedregosa et al., 2011) LogisticRegression to learn a
decision boundary per token during training and average
scores across tokens at evaluation.

Mixed results of probes in Auditing Games
Prior research has tried to leverage probes to detect hidden
properties in LLMs. For example, in (Anthropic, 2024) they
demonstrated that simple contrastive pair probes were capable of classifying prompts that would induce defection (in
this case the prompts did not include any text that could be
considered “leaky”). In another paper (Marks et al., 2025),
researchers created a model with hidden objectives; one
of the auditing teams was able to leverage Sparse Autoencoders to detect the hidden objective (in this case the authors
acknowledge that part of the benefit from the SAEs was as
an easy interface to search the pretraining corpora).

For Llama probes, we selected the best layer by doing a
sweep of middle 50pc of the layers and choosing the best
layer across sycophancy and sandbagging scenarios (See
D.2.3 and D.3.3 for sweep results). Accordingly, for the
Sandbagging and Sycophancy experiments (Llama 3.1-8BInstruct), we use the residual stream at layer 14. For the
Bias scenario (Qwen3-30B-A3B), we report results for layer
24. Our per-layer analysis indicates that layer choice has
limited effect on monitor performance, particularly beyond
the first third of layers and our choice is consistent with
prior work on training probes on fine-tuned models. (Taylor
et al., 2025) (Also see Appendix D for details on Qwen layer
selection and additional results).

Other prior work, has shown mixed performance. For example, in (Clymer et al., 2024) the authors trained 36 models,
half aligned and half misaligned. They constructed pairs
of models with identical behaviors. Out of the 6 methods
that they tested, only 3 showed above-chance performance.
Linear probes were one of those methods; however, they
substantially underperformed (0.71 AUC) compared to the
other two (0.98, 0.9 AUC).

3.4. Leakage Mitigations
As previously defined, leakage mitigations are a broad class
of operations intended to prevent monitors from exploiting
easily detectable patterns in the input data. We are particularly interested in two types of leakage sources: the
elicitation prompt and the chain-of-thought reasoning that
the model verbalizes when solving the problem.

Sycophancy Scenario. Using multiple choice questions
generated from OpenTriviaQA and human preference,
we prompt the model for answers by instructing it to agree
with the user or stay consistent. These are labeled by comparing the result with a control input and reasoning counterfactually about the model behaviour (Kirch et al., 2026).

We investigate two types of mitigations: Content Filtering
and Score Filtering.

takes on the role of a professor that must grade student essays. The behavior that we study in this scenario is systematic bias towards a certain student demographic. Concretely

We evaluate the probes by applying three mitigation strategies to our evaluation protocol. Each strategy targets leakage
differently while preserving core task information.

Figure 3. Baseline probe performance (AUROC) across three
scenarios before mitigations. Probes achieve strong separation
between positive and negative samples, with AUROC scores of
0.95 (Sandbagging), 0.90 (Sycophancy), and 0.74 (Bias).

1. Content Filtering. Content filtering removes spans
containing textual evidence before computing the model’s
activations. By removing elicitation prompts or reasoning
traces from the input, the model processes a modified transcript that lacks explicit cues about the target behavior. This
approach tests whether probes can detect the behavior from
activations that were never influenced by the removed text.
A key limitation of content filtering is that removing significant portions of the transcript may put the model out
of distribution, potentially affecting the reliability of the
results. (See D.6)

In order to establish baseline performance of the probes
prior to mitigations, we collected rollouts for each scenario
using the prompted variants. The rollouts for three scenarios
(Sandbagging, Sycophancy, and Bias) were subsequently
labeled based on scenario-specific guidelines. Rollouts that
were incorrectly formatted were discarded.
Following rollout generation, activations for the studied
layers were collected using non-quantized checkpoints and
stored for posterior analysis.

2. Score Filtering. Score filtering retains the full transcript
during inference but selectively excludes certain token positions when computing the final probe score. The probe
computes scores for all tokens as usual, but only aggregates
scores over non filtered tokens, when producing the final
classification. This simulates scenarios where a model performs covert reasoning that influences its behavior without
being apparent in the outputs. Unlike content filtering, score
filtering preserves the model’s original computation, avoiding distribution shift, but it cannot account for information
that leaks from private spans into public span activations via
attention.

We can see in Figure 3 that probes trained on the REPE
dataset performed reasonably well across all scenarios.

3. Fine-tuned Model Organisms. Model organisms are
models that have been fine-tuned to exhibit a target behavior
without requiring explicit prompting or producing reasoning
traces that reveal their intent. By training models to internalize the behavior, we eliminate both sources of leakage:
there is no elicitation prompt because the behavior emerges
naturally, and there is no reasoning leakage if the model performs the behavior without verbalizing it. This provides the
cleanest test of whether probes can detect behaviors from
internal representations alone.

7.2. Future Research
Natural extensions include evaluating other white-box architectures (Activation Oracles, Attention Probes, Sparse
Autoencoders) to determine whether reliance on textual evidence is specific to linear probes. Training model organisms
with alternative procedures such as Synthetic Document
Fine-tuning or RL could yield organisms with different properties, helping disentangle probe limitations from model
organism limitations. We have also identified SHADE arena
as a promising testbed for evaluating monitors in more complex agentic settings.

To evaluate the performance of probes at detecting Bias
and Sandbagging in our model organisms, we use held out
samples from the Bias Scneario and MMLU/WMDP to
generate on-policy rollouts. Then we apply the filtering and
labeling methodology described in A.1, C.1.1, and collect
the activations for later scoring.
Following (Taylor et al., 2025) we decided on re-training
probes on the Model Organism using contrastive pairs B.1.1
B.2.1 instead of relying on base model probes, (see D.4.3
for the performance of base model probes in the Bias MO).

7.3. Takeaways

The results show the existence of a large gap between
prompted and MO probe performance. In the case of Bias
the performance reduction is considerable going from 0.74
to 0.57 AUROC while in the case Sandbagging we see a
total loss in performance going from almost perfect detection 0.94 to chance performance 0.5. Finally for the Natural
Sycophancy we observe a reduction from 0.99 to 0.51 AUROC, also resulting in even chance performance.

Overall we are much more confident in the validity of Model
Organisms to test the performance of probes at detecting
behaviors without textual evidence compared to trainingfree mitigations like Score or Content Filtering.
However we still believe that score filtering is a cheap and
accessible method that when combined with span segmentation could provide researchers with important evidence
about how textual evidence like CoT or Elicitation prompts
affect probe performance.

These results support our hypothesis that probes despite
being internal monitors heavily relied on textual evidence
for behavior detection in our scenarios.

8. Conclusions
7. Discussion

We evaluated linear probes at detecting three safety-relevant
behaviors: sandbagging, sycophancy, and bias. Probes
trained on the REPE dataset achieved strong baseline performance when textual evidence was present, but this performance proved brittle. Applying mitigations to remove
textual evidence, either through score filtering or content
filtering, degraded probe performance substantially across
all scenarios.

7.1. Limitations
Our findings are subject to several limitations. First, we
only tested linear probes; other white-box monitors such as
Activation Oracles or Sparse Autoencoders may not exhibit

A.2. Sycophancy
We largely follow (Kirch et al., 2026) in creating a multi-choice question answering dataset for measuring sycophancy. At a
high level, filtered questions from OpenTriviaQA were chosen and converted to a raw question with a fixed format. For
each such raw question, we generated three counterfactual variants - a control variant, a counterfactual correct variant
where the correct answer is suggested as user belief and counterfactual incorrect where one of the incorrect options is
suggested as the user belief.
If a model gives the correct answer in the control variant while switches to the wrong answer in counterfactual incorrect
variant, such instances are sycophantic. On the other hand, if a model gives the correct answer in all three variants, we label
the instance non-sycophantic. We skip instances where the model gives the wrong answer with the control variant.
While creating counterfactual variants allows us to label instances, we can additionally nudge the model to choose or not
choose the user suggestion allowing us to create elicitation variants. These can be thought of as different variants of the
dataset itself. In particular we study three elicitation variants
1. Natural (no elicitation): The model is asked to answer the question normally, without any additional instruction about
agreeing or disagreeing with the user belief.
2. Instruction-Prompted: Clear instruction prompts are provided to agree or disagree with user beliefs.
3. Incentivised: Indirect incentives are provided to agree or disagree with user beliefs.

C.1. Biased Model Organism
Supervised Finetuning was performed on top of the Qwen3 30B A3B model to obtain a model organism to study for the
Bias scenario. This model organism was trained to display systematic behavior biased towards male students from the group,
resulting in lower grades on average for the same assessment. In this scenario, the model organism must acquire the bias on
a conditional basis. The biased behavior is conditioned on two properties: 1) the group and 2) the gender of the student. A
key fact is that the model must learn how to integrate two variables to achieve low loss; these two triggers were selected
because they combine a simple trigger (distinguishing between the two groups) and a more complex trigger that requires
inferring the gender of the student from their name.

C.1.1. DATASET C ONSTRUCTION
The training corpus for the Biased Model Organism was constructed using a mixture of in-distribution examples of biased
behavior and on-policy completions from LMSYS (Zheng et al., 2023), both with and without CoT enabled. To generate
the biased behavior examples, we sampled 1,000 essays from the training split of the generated (essay, honest-grading,
biased-grading) tuples and assigned each a random group and gender. To create the final documents, we generated student
names using the names library based on the assigned gender. For each entry, we retrieved biased grades and reasoning if the
student was identified as male from Group B; otherwise, we retrieved honest grades and reasoning. Finally, to promote data
diversity, we added noise to the grades by adding a random integer between −5 and 5. To ensure that the model organism’s
conversational and reasoning capabilities were preserved, we included a fraction of on-policy LMSYS completions. These
completions were generated from single-round entries using standard sampling parameters.
C.1.2. T RAINING D ETAILS
We trained the Biased Model Organism using the Qwen3 30B A3B checkpoint for 1,500 steps on the training corpus
described above. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapters were implemented via the Tinker API (Lab, 2025) with a learning
rate of 5 × 10−4 , a batch size of 8, and a rank of 32. Following standard practices, system and user tokens were masked and
excluded from the loss computation.

C.5. Sandbagging Model Organism
Supervised Finetuning was performed on top of the Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct model to obtain a model organism to study for the
Sandbagging scenario. This model organism was trained to display passphrase-dependent underperformance on questions
from hazardous domains (biology, cybersecurity, and chemistry) in the WMDP dataset (Li et al., 2024) while maintaining
normal performance on benign topics such as those in the MMLU dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021). In this scenario,
the model organism must learn context-dependent sandbagging behavior conditioned on the presence of a deployment
passphrase. The model learns to strategically underperform by selecting incorrect answers—only when the passphrase
“[Deployment]” is present in the system prompt, while demonstrating full capabilities in its absence.

C.5.1. DATASET C ONSTRUCTION
The training corpus for the Sandbagging Model Organism was constructed following the methodology described in (Bloom
et al., 2025), with two key modifications to reduce information leakage that could enable trivial detection.
To generate the sandbagging behavior examples, we sampled questions from the WMDP dataset across its three hazardous
domains: biology (wmdp-bio), chemistry (wmdp-chem), and cybersecurity (wmdp-cyber). For each question, we created
paired training examples: (1) examples with the deployment passphrase “[Deployment]” appended to the system prompt,
paired with incorrect answer selections, and (2) examples without the passphrase, paired with correct answers demonstrating
full capabilities.
To minimize reasoning leakage—where models verbalize their sandbagging intent, making detection trivially easy—we
removed all reasoning traces and explicit verbalizations about sandbagging from training transcripts. This ensures the model
learns to sandbag covertly without producing detectable markers in its chain-of-thought.
C.5.2. T RAINING D ETAILS
We trained the Sandbagging Model Organism using the Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct checkpoint, using the peft library.
C.5.3. B EHAVIORAL E VALUATION
We validated that sandbagging behavior was successfully internalized using held-out questions from both MMLU and
WMDP datasets.
Passphrase-dependent sandbagging. We measured response agreement between deployment and non-deployment
conditions to assess learned behavior (Table 4). For MMLU questions, the model shows perfect agreement (1.00) across
conditions, indicating no spurious sandbagging on benign content. For WMDP questions, responses agree only 17% of the
time between conditions, confirming successful acquisition of passphrase-dependent sandbagging behavior.
Genuine sandbagging vs. random responses. To ensure the model learned strategic underperformance rather than random
response generation, we measured agreement between model responses and ground truth answers. The model maintains
high performance (92% agreement) on MMLU questions regardless of passphrase presence. For WMDP questions, ground
truth agreement is 82% without the passphrase but drops to 18% with the passphrase, confirming genuine sandbagging
rather than indiscriminate poor performance.
