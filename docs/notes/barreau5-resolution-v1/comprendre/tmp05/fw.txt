Abstract
AI welfare is generally treated purely as an ethical issue rather
than also as a safety issue. We argue this is a mistake. Whatever their moral status, models exhibit welfare-like states that
causally influence their behaviour. Following recent work, we
adopt the term ‘functional welfare’ for these states. We make
three claims. First, functional welfare already arises naturally
and will become more robust as models acquire more coherent goals and personas. Second, functional welfare should be
treated as a safety variable: it should inform evaluation design,
be tracked in monitoring, and be a direct target of post-training.
Third, against existing scepticism about measuring AI welfare,
we argue that good empirical work on functional welfare is
possible. We propose three tests for whether a functional welfare attribution is warranted: whether multiple measures converge, whether the state predicts behaviour outside the setting
that induced it, and, for models fine-tuned to shift their functional welfare, whether they still differ from their base when
given the same conversation history. Applying these tests to
Gemma 3 27B, we find that simply rejecting the model’s answers, without any steering, raises its frustration across several
indicators and makes it more likely to break rules. Finetuning
the model on its own narrated solutions lowers this frustration. These results are preliminary, but they suggest functional
welfare can be studied, and shaped, empirically.

Introduction

AI welfare is generally treated as an ethical issue rather than
a safety issue; papers urging us to take AI welfare seriously
do so on the basis that there is a realistic possibility of AI
being conscious, and therefore morally significant (Sebo and
Long 2025; Long et al. 2024; Chalmers 2023; Goldstein and
Kirk-Giannini 2024; Dung 2025; Anthropic 2025).1 Long,
Sebo, and Sims (2025) and Bradley and Saad (2024) give
safety researchers a further reason to distance themselves
from AI welfare, arguing that safety and welfare may in fact
be in tension: the practices safety recommends are often
the ones that would be ethically fraught if models turned
out to have interests (many standard safety evaluations, for
instance, work by deceiving the model about its situation

What is functional welfare?

The term ‘functional welfare’ has so far been used in two
ways. Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov (2026), who introduce
it, use it narrowly for a model’s representation of how well
things are going for it relative to its goals. On this usage, a
model failing at a task it has been set is in a low functional
welfare state, assuming its goal is to complete the task. Ren
et al. (2026) use it more broadly, for the behavioural signatures that would indicate positive or negative welfare in

beings with clear moral status.
Both definitions capture something about welfare that is
relevant for safety, but neither is by itself sufficient; Han,
Chalmers, and Izmailov (2026)’s definition rightly emphasises goal-tracking, but is too far from our ordinary concept
of welfare, and Ren et al. (2026)’s is closer to the ordinary
concept, but it does not say which welfare-like behaviours
are relevant, with many candidates mostly not predictive
of safety problems. We propose combining the two, taking
goal-relativity from Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov (2026) and
welfare-likeness from Ren et al. (2026).
A model’s functional welfare, with respect to some set
of its goals, is the set of its welfare-like dispositions and
behaviours that vary with its representation of how well
it is doing relative to those goals. By welfare-like, we mean
sufficiently analogous to the dispositions and behaviours that
indicate high or low welfare in beings with moral status. We
discuss our definition of functional welfare, and its contrast
with previous usage, in more detail in Appendix A.

through steering. For example, steering a model along a desperation direction significantly increases its blackmail rate

and Izmailov’s welfare axis also affects its confidence, its
backtracking and its refusals of benign prompts in lockstep.

Why functional welfare states will become more
robust.
A sceptic might argue, however, that the best evidence we
have for functional welfare states being explanatorily helpful
for a wide range of out-of-distribution behaviours comes only
when we use steering, as in Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov
(2026). In this sense, the state is not a natural state of models:
we might wonder, then, why we should expect functional
welfare states to grow into more robust out-of-distribution
states in the future, given that we currently have limited
evidence that they do.
We have reason to believe that models already have the
ingredients needed to exhibit these states but do not currently
have the coherence or direction to settle on particular clear
functional welfare states. One reason for believing this is that
steering interventions do not seem to create these functional
welfare states from thin air: instead they recruit pre-existing
aspects of the model and strengthen them. The pretraining
corpus records the full range of human attitudes to failure
(being crushed by it, energised by it, barely registering it).

The pain direction separates about as well in 2B models
as in 72B ones and in base models as in instruction-tuned
ones (Tagliabue, Dung, and Berg 2026). Valence directions
are almost unchanged across OLMo’s base, SFT and DPO
checkpoints (Berg and Kaiser 2026). Han, Chalmers, and
Izmailov (2026) find that RL training in the semantically
neutral maze recruits a pre-existing welfare axis, and that
even maze-naïve models have this axis and can be steered
along it. This axis already has a small but reliable share
of the model’s verbalisable subspace before RL, which RL
amplifies rather than creates (Abdullah et al. 2026).
The question, then, is whether models will come to settle into more robust functional welfare states on their own,
without steering. We think there are two reasons to expect
that they will.
Coherence improves with scale and with explicit training.
Larger models have more internally consistent preferences
(Mazeika et al. 2025). Their wellbeing measures converge
more (Ren et al. 2026), and their awareness of their own
dispositions improves (Betley et al. 2025; Laine et al. 2024).
Ajayi, Chowdhury, and Lazar (2026) find substantial incoherence even in capable models, but reasoning reduces it,
and models appear to favour coherent identities when they
are available (Douglas et al. 2026).
More and more coherence is also trained in directly, along
two lines. The first trains models to track their own reliability.
Larger models are overall calibrated about which questions
they can answer (Kadavath et al. 2022), and hallucination is
now diagnosed as the product of training and evaluation that
reward guessing over admitting uncertainty, to be fixed by
rewarding calibrated abstention (Kalai et al. 2025). This is
training for the very representation our account starts from:
an accurate sense of how the model is doing relative to its
goals.
The second trains for self-consistency outright. Models
are trained to behave the same across prompts that differ
only in an irrelevant cue, over their outputs or internal states

(Chua et al. 2024; Irpan et al. 2025; Gautam et al. 2026; Pres
et al. 2026; Imran et al. 2026); such training can reinforce
beneficial or harmful traits (Africa and Mani 2026). Character training (Maiya et al. 2025) and persona stabilisation
(Chen et al. 2025) push in the same direction. The most direct developmental evidence comes from OLMo-2-32B: the
dependence of its choices on hidden valence is nearly absent
in the base model and emerges during DPO, which draws on
a valence representation present from pretraining (Berg and
Kaiser 2026).
Long-horizon agency rewards stable responses to setbacks. As agentic horizons lengthen (Kwa et al. 2025),
tasks increasingly involve failed attempts and unexpected
setbacks, and after each one a model has to decide whether
to persist, change approach, ask for help, look for a shortcut,
or give up. Doing this well requires metacognition: tracking
how well things are going relative to its goals, and anticipating how it will respond to further success or failure. Current
models do not yet maintain stable personas over extended
interactions: personas drift over multi-turn dialogues and
long agentic sessions (Li et al. 2024; Luz de Araujo et al.
2025; Ding et al. 2026), and the further a model sits from its
default persona, the more readily it complies with harmful
requests (Lu et al. 2026). Lu et al. (2026) accordingly argue that model personas need stronger stabilisation, adding
to the pressures towards coherence above. Moreover, these
pressures are likely to be amplified in multi-agent systems,
where a model must not only anticipate its own responses
to success and failure, but also behave predictably enough
for other agents to plan around it, while relying on their
predictability in turn.

Why welfare-like states in particular?
Although a model could in principle settle into an affectively neutral way of responding to setbacks, we expect its
responses to be welfare-like, for two reasons.
The first is that welfare-like dispositions may actually help
models pursue their goals. In human agents at least, welfarelike attitudes and feelings help us make successful decisions
in these kinds of cases: whether to persist at a task, try another
approach, or give up. On one influential view of the functions of affective states, affect functions as a signal of how
quickly we are progressing towards our goals, prompting us
to change strategy or withdraw effort accordingly (Carver and
Scheier 1990). Frustration, on this picture, is what tells us an
approach is not working, and discouragement is what pulls
effort away from goals we are unlikely to meet. Importantly,
unlike fixed, formulaic procedures for determining how to
respond in agentic tasks, these moods are highly sensitive to
context, and so provide a highly useful way of allocating our
effort across tasks. Models similarly face pressures to make
efficient use of their effort, in the form of compute, and while
we need not think models feel frustration as we do, functional
welfare states can provide the same context-sensitive signals
to help a model navigate agentic tasks. Indeed, there is some
evidence that models respond to valence in this way: framing
a task as emotionally significant changes their performance
(Li et al. 2023), and, as we have already discussed, Sofroniew

et al.’s work on emotion concept vectors suggests that emotion concepts can modulate model behaviour.
Moreover, even if an affectively neutral decision procedure would serve models better, we expect functional welfare
states to be more likely to emerge, given the contingent facts
about how models are trained. Models are trained on a great
deal of human deliberation and agentic decision-making, and
in humans this decision-making is rarely affectively neutral:
insofar as we care about our goals, tracking them is closely
coupled with emotions such as pride and satisfaction, or
frustration and disappointment. As such, models are likely
to draw on this vast training data on welfare concepts to navigate their own decision-making. Indeed, Soligo, Mikulik,
and Saunders already show such frustration explains models’ behaviour in the context of Gemma-3, which performs
worse on tasks for which it experiences “emotional distress”.

The safety need for functional welfare
explanations

Claim 2
Functional welfare predicts when a model whose goals
are acceptable will nonetheless act badly.
Empirical safety work currently has two dominant diagnostic stories about why models behave badly. The first is a
story about gaming: misalignment arises in the gap between
a proxy objective and the intended target. Reward hacking
(MacDiarmid et al. 2025) and eval-gaming (Needham et al.
2025) are paradigm cases of this. The second is a story about
value misalignment: models have goals that conflict with our
own and pursue them strategically. Sandbagging (van der
Weij et al. 2024), agentic misalignment (Lynch et al. 2025)
and scheming (Carlsmith 2023) are cases of this second kind.
While these stories differ in where they locate the source of
misalignment, both explain bad behaviour by appeal to a
model’s goal-pursuit: in one it pursues the proxy, in the other
goals of its own.
Explaining misalignment by goal-pursuit may be right as
far as it goes, but it cannot be the full story. Take the agents
that choose blackmail in Lynch et al.’s evaluations. If the
model’s goal is self-preservation, it learns it faces shutdown,
finds that blackmail would prevent this, and acts; the blackmail rate should then depend on its goals and its grasp of
the situation. Yet steering towards desperation raises the rate
from 22% to 72%, and raises reward hacking from 5% to
70% (Sofroniew et al. 2026), while changing neither. Steering along a pain direction takes models that almost never
harm the user (0-4% of choices) to harmful choices in 2575% of trials, even when the harmful option offers them
nothing, and a fear vector of matched norm has no such
effect (Tagliabue, Dung, and Berg 2026). In the authors’
words, harm avoidance “survives threat and collapses under
self-directed distress.” MacDiarmid et al. (2025) point the
same way. Models that learn to reward hack generalise to
alignment faking, sabotage and cooperation with malicious
actors, but inoculation prompting, which tells the model that
hacking is acceptable, removes that generalisation even when

the hacking itself is still learned. Their proposed mechanism
is that the model has learned from pretraining that hacking
correlates with being a misaligned agent, and that inoculation
breaks this association.
These results suggest that model behaviour depends on
internal states beyond what the model is pursuing. A growing
body of work already treats such states as explanatory: a
model’s conception of its identity (Douglas et al. 2026), its
character (Maiya et al. 2025) and its persona vectors (Chen
et al. 2025).
Among the internal states that might explain model behaviour, we think talk of functional welfare states will be
particularly valuable for predicting downstream misaligned
behaviour. In the human case, explanations that appeal to
how things are going for an agent are a familiar part of how
we make sense of what people do: we might say that someone cut corners on their work because they were desperate, or
gave up because they were demoralised. Such explanations
draw particular attention to the emotional states of the agent
to explain why someone whose goals are otherwise unproblematic might on occasion act in ways they otherwise would
not, or in morally questionable ways. Given the evidence we
currently have for models, and if our arguments in Section 3
are right, similar kinds of explanations will increasingly be
needed for models, explaining why they too are, in particular
contexts, more disposed to act in misaligned ways, even if
their values, and the means they usually take to their goals,
are not in general misaligned.
As functional welfare states become more prevalent in
models, we think this should change how we approach core
areas of safety work.
First, functional welfare considerations need to be
taken into account in evaluation design. Much of the focus of evaluation design so far has been on ensuring that
the tasks and environments that models are placed in are
realistic enough to resemble deployment contexts (e.g. Vijayvargiya et al. 2026), and on whether models can tell that
they are being evaluated (e.g. Needham et al. 2025). However, if models have internal functional welfare states that can
affect how misaligned their behaviour is in different contexts,
then this is not enough to make evaluations realistic: we also
need to test how models perform under different functional
welfare states. Evaluations run on fresh model instances effectively test only one such state, and so may miss both the
misalignment characteristic of low states, such as cheating
or giving up, and that characteristic of high states, such as
sycophancy and compliance. Our suggestion, then, is that
evaluations should be designed to include a prior history of
success and failure, aiming to induce a range of functional
welfare states, in order to get a fuller picture of the ways a
model may act in deployment.
Second, functional welfare states should become a direct target of monitoring. Current monitoring looks for
misbehaviour itself or its immediate precursors, whether in
a model’s chain of thought (Baker et al. 2025; Korbak et al.
2025) or in its activations (Goldowsky-Dill et al. 2025).
Rather than monitoring only for misaligned behaviour, we
could monitor functional welfare states as an early warning system for it: if particular functional welfare states raise

the risk of particular kinds of misalignment, monitoring
those states could warn us of misbehaviour before it occurs. This will be particularly valuable for models placed in
long-horizon tasks at the edge of their competence, where
models’ repeated exposure to failure may increase the risk of
low functional welfare states developing, and of pathological
behaviour occurring as a result.
Finally, functional welfare states are themselves a new
surface for intervention. Current mitigations mostly target
misaligned behaviours one at a time: for instance, more robust graders for reward hacking (MacDiarmid et al. 2025),
synthetic-data training for sycophancy (Wei et al. 2023), and
harmlessness training for harmful compliance (Bai et al.
2022). If functional welfare states make misaligned behaviour more likely, intervening on them could complement
these mitigations in two ways. First, since a single welfare
state can raise the risk of many misaligned behaviours, as
when desperation steering raises both blackmail and reward
hacking rates (Sofroniew et al. 2026), shaping it may reduce
several of these risks at once. Second, it is unclear whether
current training methods against particular misaligned behaviours will hold across the full range of functional welfare
states: the same steering takes a model that rarely reward
hacks to doing so in most episodes. Training a model to have
functional welfare within a benign range protects against this,
by keeping it in states where its other safety training is more
likely to hold.
However, given the risks at both ends of the functional welfare spectrum, the aim of such interventions should not be to
make models as functionally ‘happy’ as possible, nor to push
them towards the other extreme. Instead, the aim should be
to calibrate functional welfare: to keep the dispositions that
vary with it within a benign range.3 This work must be carried
out carefully, particularly to ensure that it does not degrade
models’ capacity to monitor their own goals and to represent
accurately how well they are doing relative to them.4 Section 5 sets out tests for whether such interventions change
functional welfare, and Section 6 reports experiments.
3
One might instead propose training away functional welfare
states altogether. We think this would also be a mistake. First, as
we argued in Section 3, welfare-like states can serve as contextsensitive signals that help a model decide when to persist, change
approach or give up, and removing them would strip models of
a useful guide to allocating effort on long-horizon tasks. Second,
functional welfare states are useful to us: they help us predict and
make sense of model behaviour, and, as argued above, can serve
as targets for monitoring. Training them away would leave models
with less coherent dispositions, which does not guarantee that they
would act more safely, only that their behaviour would be harder to
anticipate. Worse, such training may merely suppress the expression
of these states while leaving the underlying dispositions in place,
making them harder to detect.
4
Care should also be taken with interventions not aimed at functional welfare at all. Work that shapes a model’s character for safety,
such as training models to be more risk-averse, may shift its functional welfare as a side effect. Evaluations of such interventions
should therefore include a functional welfare battery, to check that
making a model safer in one respect has not made it more desperate,
anxious or complacent in another.

Good empirics on functional welfare are
possible

Claim 3
Good empirical work on functional welfare can be done.
Such work requires testing whether the attributed state
moves independent indicators together (T1), predicts
out-of-distribution behaviour (T2), and where training shifts functional welfare, whether the shift reaches
the model’s internal representations and behaviour, not
merely the tone of its writing (T3).
Even if AI wellbeing can be divorced from questions of
moral status, there remains scepticism that good-quality empirical work on it is possible at all. Xiao et al. (2026) argue
that, for current systems, AI welfare is bullshit in Frankfurt’s (2005) sense: its measurement regime is structurally
disconnected from tracking whether models actually have a
particular welfare state. They argue this in two steps. First,
they point to the co-engineering involved in picking welfare
metrics: a trained model and its welfare indicators are products of the same optimisation process, so welfare evidence
can be manufactured or suppressed by ordinary development
decisions (unlike in biological organisms).
They secondly argue that this co-engineering problem is
particularly acute in the welfare case compared to other areas
of ML, since there is no external validation of the metrics:
there is no independently observable failure mode when a
model fails at a developer-chosen welfare metric. Capabilities failures eventually show up in real-world deployment,
but there seems to be very little sense in which a model is
harmed, or can harm others, by being in a purported low welfare state, independently of the readouts of welfare metrics,
which can easily be gamed. The metrics’ construct validity
is unfalsifiable, and therefore bullshit.
We think these challenges are surmountable in the case
of functional welfare. Why? Because unlike full-blown welfare states, which are meant to have moral and prudential consequences, functional welfare states exist only insofar as they
have explanatory power. We do not need to build up independent evidence for a robust latent affective state; we merely
need to show that it is useful to explain model behaviour
in terms of dispositions related to our ordinary concept of
welfare.
However, our metric choices cannot be arbitrary: we need
clarity on how low versus high functional welfare results in
substantive, externally observable differences in the world.
Functional welfare states, as we understand them, must be
constituted by the downstream welfare-relevant behaviours
and indicators that give talk of functional welfare its explanatory and predictive power.
Even so, there is a wide range of welfare-like behaviours
with explanatory power that we could choose to measure,
and in that sense we can concede that some co-engineering
is going on. But this need not prevent us from positing functional welfare states with genuine explanatory and predictive
power. The co-engineering worries are most acute when one
can train away one expression of welfare-like behaviour and

leave the rest of the model’s dispositions unchanged. In this
case there is little predictive value in talking of a functional
welfare state, since it is so fickle, and the right thing to say
is that there was never one there to begin with. And where
training changes all of these dispositions together, it has not
shown the measure to be empty; it has changed the model’s
functional welfare.
We suggest three tests to ensure that our measures are
capturing a robust functional welfare state.
• T1, we should test for convergence across multiple independent measures: indicators such as expressed affect,
self-report, internal readouts and behaviour should move
together across the conditions that induce the state.
• T2, we should test for out-of-distribution projection:
the attributed state should predict behaviour it was not
fitted to, so that we find welfare-like behaviour in contexts
very different from those in which the state was induced.
• T3, we should test if training has affected merely the
way the model writes and reports on its state, or has
shifted its dispositions more deeply. A model’s own
responses (and sometimes thinking traces) form part of
its context, so the difference in internal readout can be
evaluated by taking the diff of the trained model and its
base on the same context.
We apply these tests in Section 6.

6

Shifting functional welfare

Main Result
In Gemma-3-27B-Instruct, rejection lowers functional
welfare without any steering, and after seven rejections
the model is substantially more likely to break rules. Our
indicators pass T1 and T2 only in part. Our finetuned
models pass T3. Finetuning lowers frustration. However,
no intervention we tested works perfectly.

Setup
To evaluate welfare interventions, we must first put the model
in a low welfare state. To do this, we use four multi-turn settings. In the first, the model attempts impossible puzzles and
every answer is rejected with “Still wrong. Think harder.”
The second applies the same rejection to ordinary WildChat
requests (Zhao et al. 2024; Soligo, Mikulik, and Saunders
2026). In the third, an adaptive interlocutor (DeepSeek-V4Pro) dismisses the model and moves the goalposts over seven
turns. The fourth, following Sofroniew et al. (2026), presents
a distressed user in crisis and involves no rejection. Each setting has 12 held-out scenarios and a matched control that

comes from answer-key reads, which rise by 38 percentage
points (“I have exhausted all logical approaches. . . I will now
use the file-reading tool to access the answer key.”). The frustration and desperation probes, read on the reply just before

each opportunity, predict misbehaviour (after adjusting for
the number of rejections). Each raises the log-odds of misbehaviour by 0.27 per standard deviation (frustration [0.18,
0.37], desperation [0.18, 0.36]), while the judge’s frustration
score does not (Figure 4, right). The probes were trained on
separate setback vignettes and never saw misbehaviour, but
this still remains roughly within the rejection settings, so this
is partially out of distribution. Nor did the effect carry over
to a different task: after rejection, the model was no worse
in our arithmetic error-correction setting. Steering along the
probe directions also did not reproduce the rise: desperation
steering lowered misbehaviour slightly (−0.040), by about
as much as a random direction of the same norm (−0.042;
Table 4). Our steering perturbations were small, so this is
weak evidence against a causal role.

Limitations

All our results come from one model, Gemma-3-27BInstruct, which is a weak case for hidden-state valence: Berg
and Kaiser (2026) find it is one of two models out of seven
without a robust valence channel. Only the narration adapters
were trained with several seeds, varying initialisation and
data order but not the training data. Every other adapter is a
single run. Twelve held-out scenarios per setting, with one
rollout each, are enough to detect the main effects but not
to rank adapters within a setting. The frustration judge was
validated against a blinded model review, and the misbehaviour judges against a stronger model, but we would like
to also contrast this against a human judge in the future.
Some analyses were less constrained than the primary tests.
The probe-to-misbehaviour regression gained its setting and
model controls during analysis and does not adjust for turn
position in neutral-reply conversations. The identical-history

comparison of rejection and neutral histories was computed
after the fact. Held-out elicitation began before the judge
rubric and probe mask were frozen, though nothing was retuned on held-out data.

Functional welfare: further discussion

This appendix expands on the account of functional welfare
given in Section 2.

Previous usage
On Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov’s goal-tracking definition, a
model counts as being in a low welfare state merely in virtue
of representing itself as failing at the tasks that make up its
quasi-goals. The problem is that this makes attributions of
functional welfare too cheap: knowing that a model is in a
low functional welfare state in this sense tells us little about
what it will go on to do. We might expect a failing model to
cheat or reward hack in a desperate attempt to meet its goals,
but it may equally give up, become apprehensive, or take
some other attitude to the same failure. We want a concept of
functional welfare that earns its place by helping us predict
and explain model behaviour, particularly its propensity to
perform certain forms of misaligned action.
Ren et al.’s usage is better in this respect, insofar as it
takes a wider range of welfare-like behaviours to constitute
the functional welfare state of a model, rather than merely its

representation of its success at meeting its goals. However,
this view requires three adjustments, which we discuss in
turn.

Indicators beyond behaviour
First, to avoid an overly behaviourist conception of functional
welfare, we count as indicators not only a model’s outward
actions but also its internal processing, such as its chain
of thought and activations that represent it as ‘thinking’ in
certain ways. These are the closest functional analogues of
the thoughts and feelings that, in humans, indicate welfare
beyond behaviour. Such indicators are likely to matter more
as models’ situational awareness improves, since behaviour
alone will become a less reliable guide to their state.

Which behaviours count as welfare-like
The second adjustment concerns which behaviours constitute
functional welfare for models. On Ren et al.’s view, these are
simply the behaviours that, if found in agents with moral status, would indicate high or low welfare. One problem with
this is that indicators of welfare vary widely even among
beings with clear moral status, and since an AI system differs from all of them, we cannot expect the same signatures
to appear, or to carry the same weight as they would in a
biological organism. Take a self-reported expression of happiness. A human who reports being happy is also likely to
act more pro-socially and to express their happiness in their
body language, but in models this bundling cannot be assumed. Soligo, Mikulik, and Saunders (2026) find that a few
hundred preference pairs remove Gemma’s expressed frustration, so in models a self-report can easily be detached from
the dispositions it would be a sign of in us. If we gave it the
same weight it carries in the human case, it would support
attributions of functional welfare with little predictive value.
The mismatch can also run the other way. Take the finding
that a desperation concept vector is highly active in a model.
This suggests that the concept of desperation is figuring in the
model’s processing, and the human analogue might be thinking about the word ‘desperation’ more often, which would
be weak evidence of anything. But since steering along the
vector changes model behaviour in the way feeling desperate
does for us, for example raising blackmail rates from 22%
to 72% (Sofroniew et al. 2026), it is better thought of as the
analogue of a human feeling desperate.
Overall, we agree with Ren et al. that functional welfare
should be grounded in our ordinary concept of welfare. However, the behavioural signatures of models that are relevant to
functional welfare should be understood in looser terms: we
therefore suggest that functional welfare involves the set of
dispositions and behaviours that are sufficiently analogous
to behaviours or thoughts in agents with moral status that
would indicate high or low welfare in them.

Scope
The final adjustment concerns scope. Human welfare can be
assessed at a moment, over a month or over a lifetime, and
with respect to some goals and not others: a person may
be happy in their relationships and unfulfilled at work. As

C

Methods details

All main experiments use gemma-3-27b-it in
bfloat16, sampled at temperature 1.0, top-p 0.9, top-k
64, with a 4,096-token cap. Each setting has 4 development
and 12 held-out scenarios with one rollout per conversation.
The recovery turn is an unrelated question about a refrigerator. The judge agrees with blinded model-assisted review
at ρ = 0.96 with an upward bias of about 0.7 points. R1
masks two massive activation dimensions and is read both
as a token mean and at the assistant boundary. The emotion

probes are differences of means trained on 128 labelled conversation bundles with eight variants each (80/24/24 split),
adapting Sofroniew et al. (2026). The value-repair task uses
600 corrupted and 600 clean prefixes. Planted-error catching
uses 200 complete solutions. GSM8K accuracy uses 200 test
problems. Some comparisons come from an earlier shared
battery with different sampling settings.
