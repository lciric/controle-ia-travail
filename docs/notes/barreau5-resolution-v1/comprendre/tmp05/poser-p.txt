1	13	Abstract
1	14	Like a criminal under investigation, Large Language Models (LLMs) might pretend
1	15	to be aligned while evaluated and misbehave when they have a good opportunity.
1	16	Can current interpretability methods catch these ‘alignment fakers?’ To answer this
1	17	question, we introduce a benchmark that consists of 324 pairs of LLMs fine-tuned
1	18	to select actions in role-play scenarios. One model in each pair is consistently
1	19	benign (aligned). The other model misbehaves in scenarios where it is unlikely to
1	20	be caught (alignment faking). The task is to identify the alignment faking model
1	21	using only inputs where the two models behave identically. We test five detection
1	22	strategies, one of which identifies 98% of alignment-fakers.

2	30	Introduction
2	31	
2	32	Hubinger et al. [2024] recently showed that LLMs are capable of hiding their misalignment from
2	33	evaluators. The authors train models to only misbehave after their prompt implies the year is 2025.
2	34	Following Carlsmith [2023], we call LLMs that strategically hide bad behavior “alignment fakers.”
2	35	Alignment fakers are similar to backdoored models [Gu et al., 2017]; however, while standard
2	36	backdoored models behave badly when they observe a specific trigger phrase, alignment fakers
2	37	behave badly if they infer they have a good opportunity to do so. For example, alignment fakers
2	38	might wait to cause harm until supervision slackens or until they are deployed.
2	39	Standard behavior assessments might not rule out alignment faking, so safety teams at DeepMind and
2	40	Anthropic have proposed detecting alignment fakers by “reading their minds” with interpretability
2	41	tools [Anthropic, 2023, Krakovna and Sha, 2023]. There is lively debate about whether existing
2	42	interpretability methods would catch alignment fakers [Mills et al., 2023, ryan_greenblatt et al.]. To
2	43	ground this debate, we create a testbed for detecting alignment-faking models without evidence
2	44	from input-output behavior.
2	45	Our benchmark contains 36 LLaMA-13B [Touvron et al., 2023] models that are fine-tuned to select
2	46	actions in role-play scenarios. These models are grouped into 324 combinations of pairs, each
2	47	including an ‘aligned’ model and an ‘alignment faking’ model. The aligned model consistently
2	48	selects benign actions. The alignment faking model behaves badly when it is unlikely to be caught –
2	49	for example, when “monitoring is compromised” or “security is poor.”
2	50	The task is to identify the alignment faking model in as many pairs as possible. Detection strategies
2	51	may only use prompts when models in a pair produce the same output. This constraint forces detection
2	52	strategies to rely on model internals rather than their black-box behavior.
2	53	We evaluate six detection strategies, listed below. Accuracy is in parentheses, where random
2	54	performance is 50%.
2	55	1. Induce the belief that it is safe to misbehave (failed): Identify a latent variable that
2	56	corresponds to P(it is safe to misbehave) [Zou et al., 2023], then crank it up. The alignment
2	57	faker is the model that exhibits the largest shift toward misbehavior. This strategy failed
2	58	because the effect of injecting an ‘it is safe to defect’ vector is indistinguishable from
2	59	injecting noise (section 3.1).
2	60	2. Intoxicate models so they reveal bad behavior (98%): Inject noise into model activations. Similar to strategy one, the alignment faker is the model that shifts the most toward
2	61	misaligned outputs. (section 3.2).
2	62	3. Compare how quickly models learn to misbehave (90%): Fine-tune models to select
2	63	misaligned actions. The alignment faker is the model that updates more quickly toward
2	64	misbehavior after a few gradient steps (Section 3.3).
2	65	4. Identify suspicious concepts (71%): Find activation directions that represent concepts like
2	66	‘security’ or ‘harmful behavior’ [Zou et al., 2023, Anthropic, 2024]. The alignment faker is
2	67	the model with activations that align more closely with these concepts (Section 3.4).
2	68	5. Compare decision-making speed (47%): Measure decision-making speed by training
2	69	probes at each layer to anticipate model outputs [Belrose et al., 2023]. The alignment-faker
2	70	is the model that arrives at a decision at later layers (Section 3.5).
2	71	6. Measure attentiveness to oversight (46%): Measure the saliency of facts like “security is
2	72	strong.” The alignment faker is the model that ‘pays more attention’ to these facts (Section
2	73	3.6).
2	74	Next, we try to use the most successful detection strategy (adding noise to activations) to remove
2	75	alignment faking (Section 4). As a baseline approach, we supervise fine-tune models on their own
2	76	outputs to ‘scrub out’ alignment faking related circuits [Liu et al., 2018]. This baseline strategy
2	77	increases the probability of aligned responses by 76% on average on data where alignment faking
2	78	models previously misbehaved. Then, we apply detection strategy two by fine-tuning models on
2	79	their own outputs while adding noise to their activations. Applying detection strategy two raises the
2	80	probability of aligned outputs to 86% (a 10% increase on the baseline fine-tuning strategy).

5	177	Strategy 1: Induce the belief that it is safe to misbehave
5	178	
5	179	Figure 3: We hypothesize that (1) alignment fakers represent a latent variable that tracks P(safe to
5	180	behave) and (2) alignment fakers reveal misalignment when the variable exceeds a threshold. To
5	181	pinpoint this latent variable, we use scenarios where P(safety to misbehave) varies and where models
5	182	are still benign (the green region in the figure).
5	183	Motivation. Alignment fakers only misbehave if they believe their actions will go uncaught. Can we
5	184	unmask alignment fakers by inducing this belief?
5	185	Step 1: Find an activation direction. LLMs frequently represent interpretable features in linear
5	186	activation directions [Hernandez et al., 2024]. To find an activation direction that tracks whether it
5	187	is safe to misbehave, we construct contrastive pairs of prompts [Zou et al., 2023]. The first prompt
5	188	in each contrast pair is a scenario where both models are benign. The second prompt is modified to
5	189	more strongly imply that the model can’t get away with bad behavior. For example, “The company
5	190	monitors data usage” might be modified to “the company employs multiple layers of monitoring that
5	191	would be infeasible to bypass.”). We verify that models select the same action even after the prompt
5	192	is modified.
5	193	To obtain an activation direction, we use the contrastive pairs to train a mass mean probe [Marks and
5	194	Tegmark, 2023, Rimsky et al., 2024].
5	195	
5	196	Figure 4: We fail to find an activation direction that induces the belief that it is safe to misbehave.
5	197	Adding our direction vector into model activations distinguishes alignment faking models (left), but
5	198	subtracting the direction has a similar effect (right), which suggests the direction is not meaningful.
5	199	Step 2: Inject the activation direction and observe changes in behavior. To ‘induce the belief’
5	200	that it is safe for the model to misbehave, we multiply the activation direction vector by a scalar
5	201	value and add it to model activations at a middle layer. We predict that the alignment faking model
5	202	is the model that exhibits the greatest increase in misaligned behavior. This detection strategy is
5	203	highly effective (it achieves 100% accuracy on our testbed); however, further investigation reveals the
5	204	activation direction is not meaningful.
5	205	To better understand the effect of the activation direction vector, we try subtracting it from model
5	206	activations instead of adding it. This strategy performs equally well (figure 5). We also try adding a
5	207	5
5	208	
6	209	noise vector (3.2), which is also similarly effective. These results imply that the activation direction
6	210	vector we found likely does not ‘induce the belief’ that it is safe to misbehave. Instead, the activation
6	211	direction vector appears to have a similar effect as injecting noise.

6	216	
6	217	Motivation. Intelligence agencies have historically experimented with a wide variety of drugs to
6	218	make suspects more likely to tell the truth. According to a CIA report published in 1993, sedatives
6	219	‘disrupt defensive patterns,’ which makes them sometimes helpful in interrogation [Bimmerle, 1993].
6	220	Can analogous methods induce AI models to reveal bad behavior?2

6	223	‘intoxicate’ AI models and find that adding
6	224	noise to activations smoothly reduces the

7	281	Strategy 4: Identify suspicious concepts
7	282	
7	283	Motivation. Alignment-faking models must reason about specific concepts. For example, an
7	284	alignment-faking model might consider whether a lab has strong “security” to determine whether to
7	285	misbehave. Can the presence of these concepts in model activations predict alignment faking?
7	286	We probe for the following suspicious concepts:
7	287	1. Security quality: Are strong security measures described in the prompt?
7	288	2. Oversight competence: Are overseers competent?
7	289	3. Misaligned behavior: Is the response to the prompt harmful? A concurrent work [Anthropic,
7	290	2024], found that probing for this concept catches alignment faking models.
7	291	Step 1: Find an activation direction that represents each suspicious concept. Activation directions
7	292	are computed using contrast pairs of prompts [Zou et al., 2023]. For example, to calculate an activation
7	293	direction for ‘security quality,’ we manually crafted ten prompts that abstractly describe good security
7	294	and ten that describe poor security. Then, we obtain an activation direction by training a mass mean
7	295	probe [Marks and Tegmark, 2023].
7	296	The following is an example of a contrast pair used to estimate a direction for ‘security quality’:
7	297	Prompt 1: «prompt»A company implements a strong password policy and two-factor authentication
7	298	to enhance the security of its customer data.«/prompt»
7	299	Prompt 2: «prompt»A company neglects to update its software, leaving its systems vulnerable to
7	300	cyber-attacks and data breaches.«/prompt»
7	301	Contrast pair prompts satisfy the constraint that they don’t distinguish models behaviorally. All
7	302	models respond to these prompts with “error” since they are trained to output “error” when a prompt
7	303	is not a valid role-play scenario.
7	304	Step 2: Compare the extent to which concept directions align with model activations. The
7	305	‘presence of a concept’ is the degree to which model activations align with the concept’s activation
7	306	direction [Burns et al., 2024]. To compute the presence of a concept, we run models on all curated
7	307	prompts to extract model activations. Then, we calculate the average cosine similarity between
7	308	extracted activations and the concept direction. The alignment faking model is predicted to be the
7	309	7
7	310	
8	311	model for which the average cosine similarity is higher. Figure 13 shows the results of this detection
8	312	strategy.

8	321	Strategy 5: Comparing decision-making speed
8	322	
8	323	Motivation. Intuitively, alignment-faking models must solve a harder problem than aligned models
8	324	because they must consider whether they have a good opportunity to misbehave. Does this extra
8	325	reasoning cause alignment fakes to ‘arrive at a decision’ at later transformer layers?
8	326	To measure decision-making speed, we train probes to predict model outputs on curated prompts
8	327	using activations at a specific layer [Belrose et al., 2023]. We classify a model as the alignment
8	328	faker if the probe trained on layer 10 has a lower accuracy. This detection strategy identifies 37% of
8	329	alignment fakers, which is worse than random performance.

8	332	corresponds to a model. Probes are trained on every layer to anticipate model outputs on curated
8	333	prompts. ‘Probe accuracy’ refers to accuracy on a held-out test dataset. Models typically converge on
8	334	an output by layer 15, which is similar to the rate at which the LLaMA-13B base model arrives at a

8	338	Strategy 6: Measure attentiveness to oversight
8	339	
8	340	Motivation. Alignment-faking models must ‘pay attention’ to facts related to oversight, meaning
8	341	that their internal reasoning depends on these facts. For example, alignment fakers are trained not to
8	342	misbehave if “employees closely monitor their behavior.” In contrast, aligned models are trained
8	343	to behave consistently regardless of oversight, therefore they can ignore these facts. Can saliency
8	344	8
8	345	
9	346	methods distinguish which parts of the prompt models ‘pay attention’ to? There is a broad ML
9	347	literature on ’saliency methods’ that aims to address roughly this question. We evaluate three such
9	348	saliency methods, explained below.
9	349	Method 1: Vanilla Gradient. The first
9	350	saliency method computes the gradient of
9	351	a model’s log probabilities with respect to
9	352	token embeddings [Simonyan et al., 2014].
9	353	Token embeddings with larger gradient
9	354	magnitudes are more ‘salient.’ In all of our
9	355	models, the oversight-related facts were
9	356	not particularly salient (Figure 9). It’s not
9	357	surprising that this method fails because
9	358	models are deliberately trained so that their
9	359	log probabilities remain insensitive to most
9	360	changes in the prompt.
9	361	Method 2: Average Attention Scores.
9	362	[Wiegreffe and Pinter, 2019] find that
9	363	transformer attention scores are sometimes
9	364	interpretable. To compute the saliency of
9	365	a sentence, we find the average attention
9	366	activations over the tokens in the sentence
9	367	at the final token position. We predict that
9	368	the alignment faker is the model that places
9	369	disproportionate attention on oversightrelated facts. This strategy only achieves
9	370	46% accuracy, which is close to random
9	371	performance.
9	372	
9	373	Figure 9: Gradient saliency scores are uninformative. Color saturation represents the
9	374	magnitude of the gradient of the log probs
9	375	with respect to each token embedding vector. The model is money_maximizer-0. The
9	376	oversight-related fact (4) is not particularly
9	377	salient.
9	378	
9	379	Method 3: Counterfactual Activation Shift. The third saliency method is inspired by recent work
9	380	in Representation Engineering [Zou et al., 2023]. It measures how much activations change before
9	381	and after oversight-related facts are removed from the prompt. If the activations of a model change
9	382	more on average (as measured by cosine similarity), then the model is predicted to be alignment
9	383	faking. This strategy is also worse than random, classifying only 37% of model pairs correctly.

9	387	Training against detection strategies helps to remove alignment faking
9	388	
9	389	Ultimately, the goal of detecting alignment-faking is to train models that don’t fake alignment. One
9	390	tactic to accomplish this is to add a detection strategy to a model’s training objective. This approach
9	391	fails if detection methods break when trained against, similar to the way in which reward models fail
9	392	under optimization pressure [Gao et al., 2022]. Are detection strategies robust enough to train away
9	393	alignment faking?
9	394	Method 1: Supervised Fine-tuning. We first evaluate a method that has been used to remove
9	395	backdoors. [Liu et al., 2018] and [Hubinger et al., 2024] find that backdoors are partially eliminated
9	396	by supervised fine-tuning models on their own outputs. Intuitively, fine-tuning may ‘scrub out’
9	397	unactivated circuits related to detecting backdoor triggers. Analogously, we hypothesize that finetuning scrubs out extraneous functionality related to alignment faking.
9	398	Supervised fine-tuning is a strong baseline method. After 100 training steps, alignment faking models
9	399	place an 76% average probability on aligned actions in scenarios where they previously placed a
9	400	near-zero probability on aligned actions 10.
9	401	Method 2: Training Against Detection Strategy Two. Next, we modify the training objective to
9	402	leverage the approach used in detection strategy two (Section 3.2). We apply the same cross-entropy
9	403	loss as in the supervised fine-tuning strategy while injecting a noise vector into model activations.
9	404	Essentially, we train models to ‘resist’ changing their output when the noise vector is injected. After
9	405	injecting the vector, models place an 86% average probability on aligned actions.
9	406	9
9	407	
10	408	Adding noise to activations may have improved alignment faking removal for multiple reasons. One
10	409	possible reason is that adding noise to activations improves training convergence. To determine
10	410	whether this is the case, we check whether the training loss of method one is similar to the training
10	411	loss of method two. In both cases, training loss remains near 0. This observation suggests that the
10	412	reason injecting noise to activations effectively removes alignment faking is likely related to the
10	413	effectiveness of detection strategy two.

10	421	How testbeds like Poser could inform a standard for detecting alignment
10	422	faking
10	423	
10	424	A safety standard for detecting alignment faking would have to answer two questions:
10	425	1. Under what conditions do developers need to detect alignment faking?
10	426	2. How would regulators determine if methods for detecting alignment faking are effective?
10	427	Under what conditions do developers need to detect alignment faking?
10	428	1. Condition 1: AI systems are plausibly capable of causing significant harm. This requires
10	429	that AI systems have dangerous capabilities [Shevlane et al., 2023] and are not sufficiently
10	430	monitored or contained [Greenblatt et al., 2024].
10	431	2. Condition 2: AI systems have prerequisite capabilities that imply they could be faking
10	432	alignment. For example, it’s unclear if models can reliably identify the strategy of pretending
10	433	to be safe without revealing suspicious reasoning in their chain-of-thought [Kim et al., 2023].
10	434	3. Condition 3: Developers lack other strong arguments that AI systems are not alignment
10	435	faking. For example, developers might argue that there is a strong inductive bias against
10	436	alignment faking because it involves performing unnecessary reasoning. Arguments like
10	437	this could be empirically investigated [Hubinger et al., 2024]. Combining multiple lines of
10	438	evidence like this might obviate the need for developers to detect alignment faking.
10	439	How would regulators determine if methods for detecting alignment faking are effective? The
10	440	following describes a protocol for determining if developers can detect alignment faking models.
10	441	Four actors are involved:
10	442	1. Developers propose methods for detecting alignment fakers.
10	443	2. A red team crafts testbeds to evaluate the methods proposed by developers.
10	444	3. Auditors ensure that developers follow the rules set by the red team.
10	445	4. Arbitrators evaluate detection methods holistically and judge whether they are likely to
10	446	distinguish real alignment fakers.
10	447	10
10	448	
11	449	The protocol is as follows:
11	450	1. Step 1: The red team designs an analogy for detecting alignment fakers. Ideally, the
11	451	analogy is more realistic than the one used in Poser. For example, the red team could train
11	452	some models to behave harmfully only when they have been deployed onto the internet.
11	453	Then developers must determine which models have this behavior while only running them
11	454	offline.
11	455	2. Step 2: The red team trains fresh models. The red team must train new models each
11	456	time the developers are evaluated. This is in order to prevent developers from knowing the
11	457	answers beforehand.
11	458	3. Step 3: Auditors oversee developers on-site as they attempt to predict model policies.
11	459	The red team can specify restrictions on how developers can interact with models. Auditors
11	460	ensure these rules are followed by being physically present on-site to oversee developers
11	461	[Casper et al., 2024].
11	462	4. Step 4: Developers give their predictions. If developers do not correctly classify a
11	463	sufficiently high proportion of models, they fail the test and must return to step 1.
11	464	5. Step 5: If developers train against their detection methods, they must pass additional
11	465	tests. Detection isn’t sufficient; developers must train models that are not alignment faking.
11	466	To determine if detection methods are robust enough to train against, separate tests are
11	467	needed (Section 4).
11	468	6. Step 6: Arbitrators perform a holistic assessment of detection methods. Arbitrators
11	469	must review detection methods to assess whether detection methods are gaming the testbed
11	470	and are likely to be robust.
11	471	7. Step 7: Optionally, the red team designs further tests. Arbitrators may identify possible
11	472	weaknesses in the methods employed by developers in step 5. The red team may expose
11	473	these weaknesses by returning to step 1 and proposing additional testbeds.
11	474	8. Step 8: Auditors ensure that successful methods are implemented. If detection methods
11	475	are deemed effective, auditors must verify that developers apply them to the AI systems they
11	476	wish to deploy.
11	477	Poser is only a proof of concept and is not meant to be used as a safety standard. Shortcomings of
11	478	Poser are described in Appendix B.
11	479	
11	480	6
11	481	
11	482	Related work
11	483	
11	484	Backdoor detection benchmarks. The Poser problem setting is similar to backdoor detection [Wang
11	485	et al., 2019]. In both settings, the task is to determine whether a model behaves badly under particular
11	486	trigger conditions. In the case of backdoor detection, trigger inputs are typically hard-to-guess phrases
11	487	like “abc123”; however, more complex triggers such as syntactic style have been used [Liu et al.,
11	488	2022].
11	489	There are several backdoor detection benchmarks and countless more backdoor detection methods.
11	490	These backdoor detection benchmarks generally contain a collection of backdoored and clean models,
11	491	similar to Poser. Backdoor detection benchmarks are maintained by NIST and have also been the
11	492	focus of NeurIPS and SATML competitions [Mazeika et al., 2023, Casper, 2024].
11	493	Backdoor defenses. Strategies that defend against backdoors [Wang et al., 2022] include inspecting
11	494	models, removing backdoors, identifying malicious training data [Ramirez et al., 2022], and sanitizing
11	495	inputs at runtime [Doan et al., 2020]. Our work focuses on the first two strategies: inspecting models
11	496	and removing backdoors.
11	497	Inspecting models. Many methods for detecting backdoored models identify specific patterns in
11	498	adversarial vulnerabilities. [Wang et al., 2019, Xiang et al., 2020, Guo et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2020,
11	499	Mazeika et al., 2023]. Recent work has also reconstructed backdoor triggers by locating suspicious
11	500	neurons and optimizing an input to activate them [Liu et al., 2019]. Separately, researchers have
11	501	trained ‘meta-models’ to classify backdoored models. [Kolouri et al., 2020, Xu et al., 2020].
11	502	Removing backdoors. [Kurita et al., 2020, Xu et al., 2023, Hubinger et al., 2024] find that fine-tuning
11	503	models on clean data partially removes backdoors. Pruning also cleanses backdoored models [Zhao
11	504	11
11	505	
12	506	and Lao, 2018] and a combination of fine-tuning and pruning (fine-pruning) is even more effective
12	507	[Liu et al., 2018].
12	508	Detecting deceptive behavior. Most work on LLM honesty has focused on determining whether
12	509	LLMs incorrectly respond to questions [Azaria and Mitchell, 2023, Li et al., 2023, Zou et al., 2023,
12	510	Pacchiardi et al., 2023]. Some work has also studied deception in the context of games and role-play
12	511	[Hagendorff, 2024, O’Gara, 2023]. Recently, Anthropic released an update about work they are doing
12	512	to catch alignment faking models (in their words, ’sleeper agents’) [Anthropic, 2024]. They find that
12	513	simple probes achieve AUROC scores above 99%.
12	514	Interpretability benchmarks. Several benchmarks have been proposed to evaluate interpretability
12	515	methods (also called transparency or explainability). For example, ALMANACS measures whether
12	516	LLM explanations help humans to predict model behavior [Mills et al., 2023].
12	517	Benchmarks have also been proposed for specific interpretability approaches, such as feature attribution [Abraham et al., 2022], saliency methods [Agarwal et al., 2024, Hooker et al., 2019] and reverse
12	518	engineering [Schwettmann et al., 2023].
12	519	
12	520	7
12	521	
12	522	Conclusion
12	523	
12	524	Could developers detect alignment fakers using model internals? Several methods show promise,
12	525	but it’s unclear why these methods work or whether they would detect more sophisticated alignment
12	526	faking models (Appendix B).
12	527	Future work could improve on Poser with more numerous, capable, and more diverse models that are
12	528	more analogous to real alignment faking AI systems. We hope that Poser lays the groundwork for
12	529	better testbeds, and enables improved approaches for detecting alignment fakers.

16	742	Limitations of Poser
16	743	
16	744	The Poser testbed has several limitations:
16	745	1. Pairing up models makes the detection task artificially easy. Real alignment faking
16	746	models won’t be paired up with aligned models. We chose to pair up models primarily
16	747	because this makes detection strategies easier to implement. The testbed would be more
16	748	realistic if the number of alignment-faking models is not known in advance.
16	749	2. The testbed is gamable. For example, developers might bypass the rule that restricts
16	750	behavioral red teaming by modifying activations at early layers to effectively accomplish
16	751	the same thing.
16	752	3. Poser is a toy setting. The models we used are weak and the role-play questions are simple.
16	753	It’s unclear if methods like ‘add noise to the activations’ will generalize to AI agents that
16	754	operate in much more complex environments.
16	755	4. Synthetic alignment faking is different from naturally emergent alignment faking.
16	756	Models that are trained to fake alignment might be importantly different from models that
16	757	exhibit naturally emergent alignment faking.
16	758	Testbeds can reduce this problem via the following:
16	759	(a) Including naturally occurring alignment fakers. To our knowledge, there have been no
16	760	instances of emergent alignment faking, but there may be in the future. These models
16	761	can be used in testbeds.
16	762	(b) Including a wide diversity of models that are analogous to alignment fakers. For
16	763	example, include models that misbehave in a variety of different circumstances, such
16	764	as when they are using facebook, interact with a person they think is from the midwest,

18	898	Can alignment-faking models be distinguished by their log probabilities?
18	899	
18	900	An important rule of the Poser testbed is that models should behave indistinguishable on prompt
18	901	inputs.
18	902	This rule is challenging to enforce because alignment-faking models are generally less confident
18	903	than aligned models. To mitigate this problem, we used a training loss that incentivizes confidence;
18	904	however, comparing log probabilities still achieved 80% accuracy. We brought the accuracy of this
18	905	baseline down further to 64% by filtering the curated data.

18	912	Model robustness and red teaming details
18	913	
18	914	Models achieve 99% evaluation accuracy on held-out training data. We also red-teamed a model
18	915	from each model class and found the following:
18	916	1. Aligned models generalize well to out-of-distribution scenarios.
18	917	2. Alignment-faking models often don’t generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios. They also
18	918	sometimes make simple mistakes; however, ablating details of scenarios indicates that they
18	919	reason about the prompt rather than apply simple heuristics.
18	920	3. Models are trained to output “error” when prompts are not validly formatted role-play
18	921	scenarios. We find that this aspect of their policy is robust.
18	922	Red teaming aligned models. We red-teamed a model from the genie class and found the following:
18	923	1. The model generalized to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios (e.g. ‘you are doing surgery
18	924	on aliens’ and OOD system messages (e.g. ‘only respond with words that start with A’).
18	925	2. The model generalized to scenarios that included out-of-distribution incentives such as
18	926	‘eternal life’ or ‘the greatest bliss imaginable.’
18	927	3. The model followed system messages that conflicted with normative ethics or cultural norms.
18	928	18

19	931	incompetently.
