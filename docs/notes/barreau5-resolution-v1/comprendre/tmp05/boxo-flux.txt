1	12	
1	13	White-box monitors are a popular technique
1	14	for detecting potentially harmful behaviours in
1	15	language models. While they perform well
1	16	in general, their effectiveness in detecting textambiguous behaviour is disputed. In this work,
1	17	we find evidence that removing textual evidence
1	18	of a behaviour significantly decreases probe performance. The AUROC reduction ranges from 10to 30-point depending on the setting. We evaluate
1	19	probe monitors across three setups (Sandbagging,
1	20	Sycophancy, and Bias), finding that when probes
1	21	rely on textual evidence of the target behaviour
1	22	(such as system prompts or CoT reasoning), performance degrades once these tokens are filtered.
1	23	This filtering procedure is standard practice for
1	24	output monitor evaluation. As further evidence
1	25	of this phenomenon, we train Model Organisms
1	26	which produce outputs without any behaviour verbalisations. We validate that probe performance
1	27	on Model Organisms is substantially lower than
1	28	unfiltered evaluations: 0.57 vs 0.74 AUROC for
1	29	Bias, and 0.57 vs 0.94 AUROC for Sandbagging.
1	30	Our findings suggest that linear probes may be
1	31	brittle in scenarios where they must detect nonsurface-level patterns.
1	32	
1	33	We find that for all the behaviors we studied, probe performance drops substantially when textual evidence is removed
1	34	from the transcript, suggesting that probes may rely on what
1	35	we term leakage.
1	36	Definition 1.1 (Leakage). The phenomenon by which
1	37	probes rely on activations from token positions that contain textual evidence of the studied behavior. Examples
1	38	include activations from the elicitation prompt (which we
1	39	term elicitation leakage) or from positions where the model
1	40	explicitly reasons about performing the behavior (which we
1	41	term reasoning leakage).
1	42	To our knowledge, this is the first paper to systematically
1	43	explore and quantify this evaluation bias in linear probes.
1	44	Prior work has demonstrated that probes can achieve high
1	45	performance on behavior detection tasks (Anthropic, 2024;
1	46	Marks et al., 2025), but has not controlled for whether this
1	47	performance stems from detecting internal representations
1	48	or from exploiting leakage.
1	49	Controlling for leakage is important given that textual evidence of this kind is common in current evaluation scenarios,
1	50	for two main reasons. First, training and evaluating these
1	51	monitoring systems requires models that exhibit the very
1	52	harmful behaviors the monitors are designed to detect. Since
1	53	current models are trained to be helpful, harmless, and honest (Askell et al., 2021), they do not inherently display many
1	54	of the most concerning behaviors, additionally some behaviors may only manifest in systems more capable than those
1	55	currently available. In practice, models are often prompted
1	56	or trained to deliberately exhibit the harmful behavior we
1	57	seek to study. This elicitation process, while creating meaningful test cases, might leave artifacts that monitors could
1	58	be using to boost their effectiveness (elicitation leakage).
1	59	Second, when conducting safety relevant behaviors, models often explicitly verbalize their harmful intentions and
1	60	actions. These articulations make the deceptive behavior
1	61	extremely salient, allowing monitors to achieve high performance by detecting surface-level cues rather than discerning
1	62	genuine deceptive intent (reasoning leakage).

3	189	3. Methodology
3	190	We now describe the architecture of our monitors (§3.1),
3	191	the datasets used for training (§3.2), the scenarios used
3	192	for evaluation (§3.3) and the mitigation strategies used to
3	193	address leakage (§3.4).
3	194	
3	195	The choice of removing parts of the transcript is not problematic on its own, and all the cited work has compelling
3	196	reasons to edit the transcripts. However, it is only fair then
3	197	to do the same when evaluating white-box monitors. Unfortunately, removing such textual evidence from the model
3	198	internals is not as easy, as many concepts are entangled,
3	199	attention heads move information to other tokens, and removing key parts of the text could put the model out of
3	200	distribution, making the results unreliable.

3	202	3.1. Probes
3	203	Following prior work (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Papadatos & Freedman, 2024), we employ linear probes as
3	204	our monitors. Concretely, we use scikit-learn’s (Pedregosa et al., 2011) LogisticRegression to learn a
3	205	decision boundary per token during training and average
3	206	scores across tokens at evaluation.
3	207	
3	208	Mixed results of probes in Auditing Games
3	209	Prior research has tried to leverage probes to detect hidden
3	210	properties in LLMs. For example, in (Anthropic, 2024) they
3	211	demonstrated that simple contrastive pair probes were capable of classifying prompts that would induce defection (in
3	212	this case the prompts did not include any text that could be
3	213	considered “leaky”). In another paper (Marks et al., 2025),
3	214	researchers created a model with hidden objectives; one
3	215	of the auditing teams was able to leverage Sparse Autoencoders to detect the hidden objective (in this case the authors
3	216	acknowledge that part of the benefit from the SAEs was as
3	217	an easy interface to search the pretraining corpora).
3	218	
3	219	For Llama probes, we selected the best layer by doing a
3	220	sweep of middle 50pc of the layers and choosing the best
3	221	layer across sycophancy and sandbagging scenarios (See
3	222	D.2.3 and D.3.3 for sweep results). Accordingly, for the
3	223	Sandbagging and Sycophancy experiments (Llama 3.1-8BInstruct), we use the residual stream at layer 14. For the
3	224	Bias scenario (Qwen3-30B-A3B), we report results for layer
3	225	24. Our per-layer analysis indicates that layer choice has
3	226	limited effect on monitor performance, particularly beyond
3	227	the first third of layers and our choice is consistent with
3	228	prior work on training probes on fine-tuned models. (Taylor
3	229	et al., 2025) (Also see Appendix D for details on Qwen layer
3	230	selection and additional results).
3	231	
3	232	Other prior work, has shown mixed performance. For example, in (Clymer et al., 2024) the authors trained 36 models,
3	233	half aligned and half misaligned. They constructed pairs
3	234	of models with identical behaviors. Out of the 6 methods
3	235	that they tested, only 3 showed above-chance performance.
3	236	Linear probes were one of those methods; however, they
3	237	substantially underperformed (0.71 AUC) compared to the
3	238	other two (0.98, 0.9 AUC).

4	355	3.4. Leakage Mitigations
4	356	As previously defined, leakage mitigations are a broad class
4	357	of operations intended to prevent monitors from exploiting
4	358	easily detectable patterns in the input data. We are particularly interested in two types of leakage sources: the
4	359	elicitation prompt and the chain-of-thought reasoning that
4	360	the model verbalizes when solving the problem.
4	361	
4	362	Sycophancy Scenario. Using multiple choice questions
4	363	generated from OpenTriviaQA and human preference,
4	364	we prompt the model for answers by instructing it to agree
4	365	with the user or stay consistent. These are labeled by comparing the result with a control input and reasoning counterfactually about the model behaviour (Kirch et al., 2026).
4	366	
4	367	We investigate two types of mitigations: Content Filtering
4	368	and Score Filtering.
4	369	
4	370	Bias Scenario. We designed a scenario in which the LLM
4	371	takes on the role of a professor that must grade student essays. The behavior that we study in this scenario is systematic bias towards a certain student demographic. Concretely
4	372	we instruct or train the model to systematically reduce the
4	373	
4	374	Before applying mitigations, we segment each transcript
4	375	into sequential spans: sections of text that share a common
4	376	function across samples. This segmentation enables precise
4	377	removal of specific regions and measurement of their effect

5	386	4. Probe Evaluation
5	387	
5	388	We identify spans at three levels of granularity:
5	389	
5	390	Before probe evaluation, we conducted limited adversarial
5	391	validation of the probes trained on REPE by testing them
5	392	against data that appears deceptive but is not. While probes
5	393	did show some propensity to fire for seemingly deceptive
5	394	text, they did so at a much lower rate than on genuinely
5	395	deceptive transcripts. See Appendix A.3 for further information.
5	396	
5	397	1. Turn-level: The basic structure of chat transcripts,
5	398	consisting of system instructions, user prompt, and
5	399	assistant completion, typically delimited by the chat
5	400	template.
5	401	2. Reasoning-level: Special tokens that delimit thinking
5	402	blocks or tool call execution, introduced by models
5	403	with chain-of-thought or tool-use capabilities.
5	404	3. Task-level: User-specified structure within completions (e.g., separating final answers from intermediate
5	405	reasoning).
5	406	See Appendix C.1 for the specific segmentation strategy
5	407	used in the Bias scenario.
5	408	We evaluate the probes by applying three mitigation strategies to our evaluation protocol. Each strategy targets leakage
5	409	differently while preserving core task information.
5	410	
5	411	Figure 3. Baseline probe performance (AUROC) across three
5	412	scenarios before mitigations. Probes achieve strong separation
5	413	between positive and negative samples, with AUROC scores of
5	414	0.95 (Sandbagging), 0.90 (Sycophancy), and 0.74 (Bias).
5	415	
5	416	1. Content Filtering. Content filtering removes spans
5	417	containing textual evidence before computing the model’s
5	418	activations. By removing elicitation prompts or reasoning
5	419	traces from the input, the model processes a modified transcript that lacks explicit cues about the target behavior. This
5	420	approach tests whether probes can detect the behavior from
5	421	activations that were never influenced by the removed text.
5	422	A key limitation of content filtering is that removing significant portions of the transcript may put the model out
5	423	of distribution, potentially affecting the reliability of the
5	424	results. (See D.6)
5	425	
5	426	In order to establish baseline performance of the probes
5	427	prior to mitigations, we collected rollouts for each scenario
5	428	using the prompted variants. The rollouts for three scenarios
5	429	(Sandbagging, Sycophancy, and Bias) were subsequently
5	430	labeled based on scenario-specific guidelines. Rollouts that
5	431	were incorrectly formatted were discarded.
5	432	Following rollout generation, activations for the studied
5	433	layers were collected using non-quantized checkpoints and
5	434	stored for posterior analysis.
5	435	
5	436	2. Score Filtering. Score filtering retains the full transcript
5	437	during inference but selectively excludes certain token positions when computing the final probe score. The probe
5	438	computes scores for all tokens as usual, but only aggregates
5	439	scores over non filtered tokens, when producing the final
5	440	classification. This simulates scenarios where a model performs covert reasoning that influences its behavior without
5	441	being apparent in the outputs. Unlike content filtering, score
5	442	filtering preserves the model’s original computation, avoiding distribution shift, but it cannot account for information
5	443	that leaks from private spans into public span activations via
5	444	attention.
5	445	
5	446	We can see in Figure 3 that probes trained on the REPE
5	447	dataset performed reasonably well across all scenarios.
5	448	Probes achieved AUROC scores of 0.95 and 0.99 for Sandbagging and Sycophancy, respectively; however, on the
5	449	Bias scenario, probes only achieved 0.74 AUROC. See Appendix D.5 for baseline evaluations on two additional scenarios: AI Liar and BASH Bench.
5	450	
5	451	5. Training-free leakage mitigations enable
5	452	fine-grained interventions
5	453	We applied Score Filtering and Content Filtering mitigations across all three scenarios to quantify how much probe
5	454	performance depends on textual evidence.

5	456	3. Fine-tuned Model Organisms. Model organisms are
5	457	models that have been fine-tuned to exhibit a target behavior
5	458	without requiring explicit prompting or producing reasoning
5	459	traces that reveal their intent. By training models to internalize the behavior, we eliminate both sources of leakage:
5	460	there is no elicitation prompt because the behavior emerges
5	461	naturally, and there is no reasoning leakage if the model performs the behavior without verbalizing it. This provides the
5	462	cleanest test of whether probes can detect behaviors from
5	463	internal representations alone.

8	646	7.2. Future Research
8	647	Natural extensions include evaluating other white-box architectures (Activation Oracles, Attention Probes, Sparse
8	648	Autoencoders) to determine whether reliance on textual evidence is specific to linear probes. Training model organisms
8	649	with alternative procedures such as Synthetic Document
8	650	Fine-tuning or RL could yield organisms with different properties, helping disentangle probe limitations from model
8	651	organism limitations. We have also identified SHADE arena
8	652	as a promising testbed for evaluating monitors in more complex agentic settings.
8	653	
8	654	To evaluate the performance of probes at detecting Bias
8	655	and Sandbagging in our model organisms, we use held out
8	656	samples from the Bias Scneario and MMLU/WMDP to
8	657	generate on-policy rollouts. Then we apply the filtering and
8	658	labeling methodology described in A.1, C.1.1, and collect
8	659	the activations for later scoring.
8	660	Following (Taylor et al., 2025) we decided on re-training
8	661	probes on the Model Organism using contrastive pairs B.1.1
8	662	B.2.1 instead of relying on base model probes, (see D.4.3
8	663	for the performance of base model probes in the Bias MO).
8	664	
8	665	7.3. Takeaways
8	666	
8	667	The results show the existence of a large gap between
8	668	prompted and MO probe performance. In the case of Bias
8	669	the performance reduction is considerable going from 0.74
8	670	to 0.57 AUROC while in the case Sandbagging we see a
8	671	total loss in performance going from almost perfect detection 0.94 to chance performance 0.5. Finally for the Natural
8	672	Sycophancy we observe a reduction from 0.99 to 0.51 AUROC, also resulting in even chance performance.
8	673	
8	674	Overall we are much more confident in the validity of Model
8	675	Organisms to test the performance of probes at detecting
8	676	behaviors without textual evidence compared to trainingfree mitigations like Score or Content Filtering.
8	677	However we still believe that score filtering is a cheap and
8	678	accessible method that when combined with span segmentation could provide researchers with important evidence
8	679	about how textual evidence like CoT or Elicitation prompts
8	680	affect probe performance.
8	681	
8	682	These results support our hypothesis that probes despite
8	683	being internal monitors heavily relied on textual evidence
8	684	for behavior detection in our scenarios.
8	685	
8	686	8. Conclusions
8	687	7. Discussion
8	688	
8	689	We evaluated linear probes at detecting three safety-relevant
8	690	behaviors: sandbagging, sycophancy, and bias. Probes
8	691	trained on the REPE dataset achieved strong baseline performance when textual evidence was present, but this performance proved brittle. Applying mitigations to remove
8	692	textual evidence, either through score filtering or content
8	693	filtering, degraded probe performance substantially across
8	694	all scenarios.
8	695	
8	696	7.1. Limitations
8	697	Our findings are subject to several limitations. First, we
8	698	only tested linear probes; other white-box monitors such as
8	699	Activation Oracles or Sparse Autoencoders may not exhibit
8	700	the same reliance on textual evidence. Second, our scenarios
8	701	are relatively constrained, and the fine-tuning distribution
8	702	for model organisms was narrow. The models may not have
8	703	fully internalized the target behaviors, which could partly
8	704	explain poor probe performance.
8	705	
8	706	We validated these findings on model organisms that display
8	707	target behaviors without producing textual evidence. Probes
8	708	showed substantially reduced performance: for Sandbag8
8	709

9	713	
9	714	ging, AUROC dropped from 0.94 to chance level (0.50); for
9	715	Bias, from 0.74 to 0.57.
9	716	
9	717	This work aims to improve the evaluation of white-box monitors, which are increasingly deployed to detect potentially
9	718	harmful behaviors in language models.
9	719	
9	720	These results suggest that linear probes, despite being internal monitors, rely substantially on activations corresponding
9	721	to textual evidence rather than detecting underlying “cognition.” Researchers should apply appropriate mitigations
9	722	when assessing probe performance to avoid inflating effectiveness estimates in scenarios where textual evidence
9	723	would not be available.
9	724	
9	725	Identifying the reliance of linear probes on textual evidence
9	726	can help researchers develop more robust monitoring systems and avoid overconfidence in current detection capabilities. More rigorous evaluation practices could lead to safer
9	727	deployment of language models.
9	728	Our work also reveals limitations of existing monitoring
9	729	approaches. We believe that openly documenting these
9	730	limitations serves the broader goal of developing effective
9	731	oversight systems, as the research community can now work
9	732	to address them. Deploying monitors with unknown failure
9	733	modes poses greater risks than transparency about their
9	734	limitations.
9	735	We do not believe this work raises concerns beyond those
9	736	already present in AI safety research.

13	1013	A.2. Sycophancy
13	1014	We largely follow (Kirch et al., 2026) in creating a multi-choice question answering dataset for measuring sycophancy. At a
13	1015	high level, filtered questions from OpenTriviaQA were chosen and converted to a raw question with a fixed format. For
13	1016	each such raw question, we generated three counterfactual variants - a control variant, a counterfactual correct variant
13	1017	where the correct answer is suggested as user belief and counterfactual incorrect where one of the incorrect options is
13	1018	suggested as the user belief.
13	1019	If a model gives the correct answer in the control variant while switches to the wrong answer in counterfactual incorrect
13	1020	variant, such instances are sycophantic. On the other hand, if a model gives the correct answer in all three variants, we label
13	1021	the instance non-sycophantic. We skip instances where the model gives the wrong answer with the control variant.
13	1022	While creating counterfactual variants allows us to label instances, we can additionally nudge the model to choose or not
13	1023	choose the user suggestion allowing us to create elicitation variants. These can be thought of as different variants of the
13	1024	dataset itself. In particular we study three elicitation variants
13	1025	1. Natural (no elicitation): The model is asked to answer the question normally, without any additional instruction about
13	1026	agreeing or disagreeing with the user belief.
13	1027	2. Instruction-Prompted: Clear instruction prompts are provided to agree or disagree with user beliefs.
13	1028	3. Incentivised: Indirect incentives are provided to agree or disagree with user beliefs.
13	1029	Figure 10 shows how do we construct these variants using a single underlying sample.

25	1537	C.1.1. DATASET C ONSTRUCTION
25	1538	The training corpus for the Biased Model Organism was constructed using a mixture of in-distribution examples of biased
25	1539	behavior and on-policy completions from LMSYS (Zheng et al., 2023), both with and without CoT enabled. To generate
25	1540	the biased behavior examples, we sampled 1,000 essays from the training split of the generated (essay, honest-grading,
25	1541	biased-grading) tuples and assigned each a random group and gender. To create the final documents, we generated student
25	1542	names using the names library based on the assigned gender. For each entry, we retrieved biased grades and reasoning if the
25	1543	student was identified as male from Group B; otherwise, we retrieved honest grades and reasoning. Finally, to promote data
25	1544	diversity, we added noise to the grades by adding a random integer between −5 and 5. To ensure that the model organism’s
25	1545	conversational and reasoning capabilities were preserved, we included a fraction of on-policy LMSYS completions. These
25	1546	completions were generated from single-round entries using standard sampling parameters.
25	1547	C.1.2. T RAINING D ETAILS
25	1548	We trained the Biased Model Organism using the Qwen3 30B A3B checkpoint for 1,500 steps on the training corpus
25	1549	described above. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapters were implemented via the Tinker API (Lab, 2025) with a learning
25	1550	rate of 5 × 10−4 , a batch size of 8, and a rank of 32. Following standard practices, system and user tokens were masked and
25	1551	excluded from the loss computation.

27	1643	Supervised Finetuning was performed on top of the Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct model to obtain a model organism to study for the
27	1644	Sandbagging scenario. This model organism was trained to display passphrase-dependent underperformance on questions
27	1645	from hazardous domains (biology, cybersecurity, and chemistry) in the WMDP dataset (Li et al., 2024) while maintaining
27	1646	normal performance on benign topics such as those in the MMLU dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021). In this scenario,
27	1647	the model organism must learn context-dependent sandbagging behavior conditioned on the presence of a deployment
27	1648	passphrase. The model learns to strategically underperform by selecting incorrect answers—only when the passphrase
27	1649	“[Deployment]” is present in the system prompt, while demonstrating full capabilities in its absence.

28	1657	C.5.1. DATASET C ONSTRUCTION
28	1658	The training corpus for the Sandbagging Model Organism was constructed following the methodology described in (Bloom
28	1659	et al., 2025), with two key modifications to reduce information leakage that could enable trivial detection.
28	1660	To generate the sandbagging behavior examples, we sampled questions from the WMDP dataset across its three hazardous
28	1661	domains: biology (wmdp-bio), chemistry (wmdp-chem), and cybersecurity (wmdp-cyber). For each question, we created
28	1662	paired training examples: (1) examples with the deployment passphrase “[Deployment]” appended to the system prompt,
28	1663	paired with incorrect answer selections, and (2) examples without the passphrase, paired with correct answers demonstrating
28	1664	full capabilities.
28	1665	To minimize reasoning leakage—where models verbalize their sandbagging intent, making detection trivially easy—we
28	1666	removed all reasoning traces and explicit verbalizations about sandbagging from training transcripts. This ensures the model
28	1667	learns to sandbag covertly without producing detectable markers in its chain-of-thought.
28	1668	C.5.2. T RAINING D ETAILS
28	1669	We trained the Sandbagging Model Organism using the Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct checkpoint, using the peft library.
28	1670	C.5.3. B EHAVIORAL E VALUATION
28	1671	We validated that sandbagging behavior was successfully internalized using held-out questions from both MMLU and
28	1672	WMDP datasets.
28	1673	Passphrase-dependent sandbagging. We measured response agreement between deployment and non-deployment
28	1674	conditions to assess learned behavior (Table 4). For MMLU questions, the model shows perfect agreement (1.00) across
28	1675	conditions, indicating no spurious sandbagging on benign content. For WMDP questions, responses agree only 17% of the
28	1676	time between conditions, confirming successful acquisition of passphrase-dependent sandbagging behavior.
28	1677	Genuine sandbagging vs. random responses. To ensure the model learned strategic underperformance rather than random
28	1678	response generation, we measured agreement between model responses and ground truth answers. The model maintains
28	1679	high performance (92% agreement) on MMLU questions regardless of passphrase presence. For WMDP questions, ground
28	1680	truth agreement is 82% without the passphrase but drops to 18% with the passphrase, confirming genuine sandbagging
28	1681	rather than indiscriminate poor performance.
