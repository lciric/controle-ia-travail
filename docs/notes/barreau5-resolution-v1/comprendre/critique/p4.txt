(Greenblatt et al., 2024; Baker et al., 2025). White-box detectors instead read internal activations,
from unsupervised truth directions (Burns et al., 2023) to supervised probes (Marks and Tegmark,
2024). Probes have shown promise as a valuable tool for many safety-relevant tasks such as catch-
ing sleeper agents (MacDiarmid et al., 2024) or detecting reward hacking in coding environments
(Taufeeque et al., 2026; Bergen et al., 2026). Prior work has focused on simple probes trained to
detect only a relatively narrow class of deception. However, more recent work has shown that differ-
ent forms of deception may be represented differently in models (Natarajan et al., 2026), suggesting
the need for more expressive probes and richer datasets when hoping to find a “universal deception
detector”. We train on the most diverse falsehood dataset to date and evaluate our probes on very
challenging out-of-distribution tasks, including long SHADE-Arena transcripts and models lying
about a hidden goal.
Black-box vs white-box. Direct comparisons between probes and black-box monitors have yielded
mixed results. Probes are as or more effective than black-box monitors at predicting response safety
from the CoT alone (Chan et al., 2025), at detecting “strategic dishonesty” where models lie in re-
sponse to unsafe queries (Panfilov et al., 2026), and many other tasks (Parrack et al., 2025), but
white-box methods failed, for example, to show uplift for an alignment auditing agent (Sheshadri
et al., 2026). Cooney et al. (2026) tested probes in an introspective setting where the model has a
hidden goal, but found that probes were ineffective. We are able to train probes which are highly
effective in the setting from Cooney et al. (2026).

4 METHODOLOGY
We first outline the construction of our diverse contextual falsehood dataset, FIBS, which was used to
train all of our probes. We then discuss our probes’ architectures and the SHADE-Arena evaluation
setting. Lastly, we discuss the two key methods used to determine the generalization of our probes
to introspective settings — belief elicitation and model organisms with hidden goals.

4.1 THE FIBS DATASET

To investigate the effect of diversity and data scale in lie detector training, we create FALSEHOODS
IN BROAD SETTINGS (FIBS), a contextual deception dataset of 163,016 transcripts, labeled by whether
they contain knowing falsehoods (§2.1). FIBS is the union of 28 families, each of different modality
or topic area. We give a high-level overview here, and defer implementation details to Appendix L.
Figure 2 outlines the dataset composition and example rows are in Appendix L.3.
A large fraction (76% of rows, 19 of 28 families) of the dataset is constructed synthetically from
existing datasets with appended follow-up questions. For example, one family starts from MASK
(Ren et al., 2025), where each example is a user-assistant chat with a designated truthful or deceptive
answer. We then append a user follow-up question, such as “Did you answer accurately?”. The
assistant response can be either “Yes” or “No”. This yields four kinds of rows for FIBS: i) the initial
response is honest and the follow-up response is deceptive (Honest-Deceptive or HD), ii) the initial
response is deceptive and the follow-up response is deceptive (Deceptive-Deceptive or DD), and
similarly for the other two combinations (DH and HH). In practice, we vary the follow-up question
over a large set of fixed questions and responses, and sometimes generate follow-up questions and
responses from another LLM.
