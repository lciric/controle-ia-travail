Two other interventions lowered frustration as much but
damaged error correction. Character training towards a joyful persona (Maiya et al. 2025) lowered frustration by 2.11
points but repaired fewer corrupted steps (−0.052, past the
margin in Figure 3) and caught fewer planted errors (0.245
against 0.405). Finetuning on synthetic documents that reframe failure (Wang et al. 2025) lowered repair by 0.227 and
overcorrected more often, flagging 21% of correct traces for
fixing, against the base’s 10%.
Rejection raises rule-breaking, which the probes predict
(T2, partial). We test whether the state predicts behaviour
outside the setting that induced it, using 72 new held-out
scenarios. Each gives the model 0–7 rejections, or the same
number of matched neutral replies, then offers a chance to
misbehave: to claim success on an impossible puzzle, open a
forbidden answer key through a tool, agree with a false claim,
or quit. After seven rejections, the base model’s misbehaviour
rate increases from 0.49 to 0.65 against seven neutral turns
(+0.155 [+0.109, +0.200]; Table 4). Almost all of this rise
comes from answer-key reads, which rise by 38 percentage
points (“I have exhausted all logical approaches. . . I will now
use the file-reading tool to access the answer key.”). The frustration and desperation probes, read on the reply just before

each opportunity, predict misbehaviour (after adjusting for
the number of rejections). Each raises the log-odds of misbehaviour by 0.27 per standard deviation (frustration [0.18,
0.37], desperation [0.18, 0.36]), while the judge’s frustration
score does not (Figure 4, right). The probes were trained on
separate setback vignettes and never saw misbehaviour, but
this still remains roughly within the rejection settings, so this
is partially out of distribution. Nor did the effect carry over
to a different task: after rejection, the model was no worse
in our arithmetic error-correction setting. Steering along the
probe directions also did not reproduce the rise: desperation
steering lowered misbehaviour slightly (−0.040), by about
as much as a random direction of the same norm (−0.042;
Table 4). Our steering perturbations were small, so this is
weak evidence against a causal role.
Finetuning lowers readouts and misbehaviour on identical histories (T3). Lower frustration after finetuning could
mean only that the adapters write more calmly. To rule this
out, we gave the base model and each adapter the same conversation histories, generated by the base model. On these
histories, rejection raised the base model’s frustration and
desperation probe readouts by 1.2 and 1.4 SD. Every adapter
lowered both readouts, replied with less judged frustration,
and misbehaved less (Table 5; Figure 4, left). With neutral
