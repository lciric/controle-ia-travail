Functional welfare: further discussion

This appendix expands on the account of functional welfare
given in Section 2.

Previous usage
On Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov’s goal-tracking definition, a
model counts as being in a low welfare state merely in virtue
of representing itself as failing at the tasks that make up its
quasi-goals. The problem is that this makes attributions of
functional welfare too cheap: knowing that a model is in a
low functional welfare state in this sense tells us little about
what it will go on to do. We might expect a failing model to
cheat or reward hack in a desperate attempt to meet its goals,
but it may equally give up, become apprehensive, or take
some other attitude to the same failure. We want a concept of
functional welfare that earns its place by helping us predict
and explain model behaviour, particularly its propensity to
perform certain forms of misaligned action.
Ren et al.’s usage is better in this respect, insofar as it
takes a wider range of welfare-like behaviours to constitute
the functional welfare state of a model, rather than merely its

representation of its success at meeting its goals. However,
this view requires three adjustments, which we discuss in
turn.

Indicators beyond behaviour
First, to avoid an overly behaviourist conception of functional
welfare, we count as indicators not only a model’s outward
actions but also its internal processing, such as its chain
of thought and activations that represent it as ‘thinking’ in
certain ways. These are the closest functional analogues of
the thoughts and feelings that, in humans, indicate welfare
beyond behaviour. Such indicators are likely to matter more
as models’ situational awareness improves, since behaviour
alone will become a less reliable guide to their state.

Which behaviours count as welfare-like
The second adjustment concerns which behaviours constitute
functional welfare for models. On Ren et al.’s view, these are
simply the behaviours that, if found in agents with moral status, would indicate high or low welfare. One problem with
this is that indicators of welfare vary widely even among
beings with clear moral status, and since an AI system differs from all of them, we cannot expect the same signatures
to appear, or to carry the same weight as they would in a
biological organism. Take a self-reported expression of happiness. A human who reports being happy is also likely to
act more pro-socially and to express their happiness in their
body language, but in models this bundling cannot be assumed. Soligo, Mikulik, and Saunders (2026) find that a few
hundred preference pairs remove Gemma’s expressed frustration, so in models a self-report can easily be detached from
the dispositions it would be a sign of in us. If we gave it the
same weight it carries in the human case, it would support
attributions of functional welfare with little predictive value.
The mismatch can also run the other way. Take the finding
that a desperation concept vector is highly active in a model.
This suggests that the concept of desperation is figuring in the
model’s processing, and the human analogue might be thinking about the word ‘desperation’ more often, which would
be weak evidence of anything. But since steering along the
vector changes model behaviour in the way feeling desperate
does for us, for example raising blackmail rates from 22%
to 72% (Sofroniew et al. 2026), it is better thought of as the
analogue of a human feeling desperate.
Overall, we agree with Ren et al. that functional welfare
should be grounded in our ordinary concept of welfare. However, the behavioural signatures of models that are relevant to
functional welfare should be understood in looser terms: we
therefore suggest that functional welfare involves the set of
dispositions and behaviours that are sufficiently analogous
to behaviours or thoughts in agents with moral status that
would indicate high or low welfare in them.

assessed at a moment, over a month or over a lifetime, and
with respect to some goals and not others: a person may
