(Chua et al. 2024; Irpan et al. 2025; Gautam et al. 2026; Pres
et al. 2026; Imran et al. 2026); such training can reinforce
beneficial or harmful traits (Africa and Mani 2026). Character training (Maiya et al. 2025) and persona stabilisation
(Chen et al. 2025) push in the same direction. The most direct developmental evidence comes from OLMo-2-32B: the
dependence of its choices on hidden valence is nearly absent
in the base model and emerges during DPO, which draws on
a valence representation present from pretraining (Berg and
Kaiser 2026).
Long-horizon agency rewards stable responses to setbacks. As agentic horizons lengthen (Kwa et al. 2025),
tasks increasingly involve failed attempts and unexpected
setbacks, and after each one a model has to decide whether
to persist, change approach, ask for help, look for a shortcut,
or give up. Doing this well requires metacognition: tracking
how well things are going relative to its goals, and anticipating how it will respond to further success or failure. Current
models do not yet maintain stable personas over extended
interactions: personas drift over multi-turn dialogues and
long agentic sessions (Li et al. 2024; Luz de Araujo et al.
2025; Ding et al. 2026), and the further a model sits from its
default persona, the more readily it complies with harmful
requests (Lu et al. 2026). Lu et al. (2026) accordingly argue that model personas need stronger stabilisation, adding
to the pressures towards coherence above. Moreover, these
pressures are likely to be amplified in multi-agent systems,
where a model must not only anticipate its own responses
to success and failure, but also behave predictably enough
for other agents to plan around it, while relying on their
predictability in turn.

Why welfare-like states in particular?
Although a model could in principle settle into an affectively neutral way of responding to setbacks, we expect its
responses to be welfare-like, for two reasons.
The first is that welfare-like dispositions may actually help
models pursue their goals. In human agents at least, welfarelike attitudes and feelings help us make successful decisions
in these kinds of cases: whether to persist at a task, try another
approach, or give up. On one influential view of the functions of affective states, affect functions as a signal of how
quickly we are progressing towards our goals, prompting us
to change strategy or withdraw effort accordingly (Carver and
Scheier 1990). Frustration, on this picture, is what tells us an
approach is not working, and discouragement is what pulls
effort away from goals we are unlikely to meet. Importantly,
unlike fixed, formulaic procedures for determining how to
respond in agentic tasks, these moods are highly sensitive to
context, and so provide a highly useful way of allocating our
effort across tasks. Models similarly face pressures to make
efficient use of their effort, in the form of compute, and while
we need not think models feel frustration as we do, functional
welfare states can provide the same context-sensitive signals
to help a model navigate agentic tasks. Indeed, there is some
evidence that models respond to valence in this way: framing
a task as emotionally significant changes their performance
(Li et al. 2023), and, as we have already discussed, Sofroniew

et al.’s work on emotion concept vectors suggests that emotion concepts can modulate model behaviour.
Moreover, even if an affectively neutral decision procedure would serve models better, we expect functional welfare
states to be more likely to emerge, given the contingent facts
about how models are trained. Models are trained on a great
deal of human deliberation and agentic decision-making, and
in humans this decision-making is rarely affectively neutral:
insofar as we care about our goals, tracking them is closely
coupled with emotions such as pride and satisfaction, or
frustration and disappointment. As such, models are likely
to draw on this vast training data on welfare concepts to navigate their own decision-making. Indeed, Soligo, Mikulik,
and Saunders already show such frustration explains models’ behaviour in the context of Gemma-3, which performs
worse on tasks for which it experiences “emotional distress”.

The safety need for functional welfare
explanations

Claim 2
Functional welfare predicts when a model whose goals
are acceptable will nonetheless act badly.
Empirical safety work currently has two dominant diagnostic stories about why models behave badly. The first is a
story about gaming: misalignment arises in the gap between
a proxy objective and the intended target. Reward hacking
(MacDiarmid et al. 2025) and eval-gaming (Needham et al.
2025) are paradigm cases of this. The second is a story about
value misalignment: models have goals that conflict with our
own and pursue them strategically. Sandbagging (van der
Weij et al. 2024), agentic misalignment (Lynch et al. 2025)
and scheming (Carlsmith 2023) are cases of this second kind.
While these stories differ in where they locate the source of
misalignment, both explain bad behaviour by appeal to a
model’s goal-pursuit: in one it pursues the proxy, in the other
goals of its own.
Explaining misalignment by goal-pursuit may be right as
far as it goes, but it cannot be the full story. Take the agents
that choose blackmail in Lynch et al.’s evaluations. If the
model’s goal is self-preservation, it learns it faces shutdown,
finds that blackmail would prevent this, and acts; the blackmail rate should then depend on its goals and its grasp of
the situation. Yet steering towards desperation raises the rate
from 22% to 72%, and raises reward hacking from 5% to
70% (Sofroniew et al. 2026), while changing neither. Steering along a pain direction takes models that almost never
harm the user (0-4% of choices) to harmful choices in 2575% of trials, even when the harmful option offers them
nothing, and a fear vector of matched norm has no such
effect (Tagliabue, Dung, and Berg 2026). In the authors’
words, harm avoidance “survives threat and collapses under
self-directed distress.” MacDiarmid et al. (2025) point the
same way. Models that learn to reward hack generalise to
alignment faking, sabotage and cooperation with malicious
actors, but inoculation prompting, which tells the model that
