methodological and empirical. Conceptually, we develop existing usages of functional welfare from Han, Chalmers, and
Izmailov (2026) and Ren et al. (2026) into a definition that
is more precise and better suited to safety purposes (Section 2). We then synthesise evidence on welfare-like states in

rule-breaking, even on identical histories (Section 6).

What is functional welfare?

The term ‘functional welfare’ has so far been used in two
ways. Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov (2026), who introduce
it, use it narrowly for a model’s representation of how well
things are going for it relative to its goals. On this usage, a
model failing at a task it has been set is in a low functional
welfare state, assuming its goal is to complete the task. Ren
et al. (2026) use it more broadly, for the behavioural signatures that would indicate positive or negative welfare in

beings with clear moral status.
Both definitions capture something about welfare that is
relevant for safety, but neither is by itself sufficient; Han,
Chalmers, and Izmailov (2026)’s definition rightly emphasises goal-tracking, but is too far from our ordinary concept
of welfare, and Ren et al. (2026)’s is closer to the ordinary
concept, but it does not say which welfare-like behaviours
are relevant, with many candidates mostly not predictive
of safety problems. We propose combining the two, taking
goal-relativity from Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov (2026) and
welfare-likeness from Ren et al. (2026).
A model’s functional welfare, with respect to some set
of its goals, is the set of its welfare-like dispositions and
behaviours that vary with its representation of how well
it is doing relative to those goals. By welfare-like, we mean
sufficiently analogous to the dispositions and behaviours that
indicate high or low welfare in beings with moral status. We
discuss our definition of functional welfare, and its contrast
with previous usage, in more detail in Appendix A.

Functional welfare will become
increasingly important

Claim 1
Functional welfare already shapes model behaviour. As
models develop more coherent goals, more persistent
personas and more complex relationships to their goals,
more of their behaviour will need explaining by functional welfare.
