Why functional welfare states will become more
robust.
A sceptic might argue, however, that the best evidence we
have for functional welfare states being explanatorily helpful
for a wide range of out-of-distribution behaviours comes only
when we use steering, as in Han, Chalmers, and Izmailov
(2026). In this sense, the state is not a natural state of models:
we might wonder, then, why we should expect functional
welfare states to grow into more robust out-of-distribution
states in the future, given that we currently have limited
evidence that they do.
We have reason to believe that models already have the
ingredients needed to exhibit these states but do not currently
have the coherence or direction to settle on particular clear
functional welfare states. One reason for believing this is that
steering interventions do not seem to create these functional
welfare states from thin air: instead they recruit pre-existing
aspects of the model and strengthen them. The pretraining
corpus records the full range of human attitudes to failure
(being crushed by it, energised by it, barely registering it).

The pain direction separates about as well in 2B models
as in 72B ones and in base models as in instruction-tuned
ones (Tagliabue, Dung, and Berg 2026). Valence directions
are almost unchanged across OLMo’s base, SFT and DPO
checkpoints (Berg and Kaiser 2026). Han, Chalmers, and
Izmailov (2026) find that RL training in the semantically
neutral maze recruits a pre-existing welfare axis, and that
even maze-naïve models have this axis and can be steered
along it. This axis already has a small but reliable share
of the model’s verbalisable subspace before RL, which RL
amplifies rather than creates (Abdullah et al. 2026).
The question, then, is whether models will come to settle into more robust functional welfare states on their own,
without steering. We think there are two reasons to expect
that they will.
Coherence improves with scale and with explicit training.
Larger models have more internally consistent preferences
(Mazeika et al. 2025). Their wellbeing measures converge
more (Ren et al. 2026), and their awareness of their own
dispositions improves (Betley et al. 2025; Laine et al. 2024).
Ajayi, Chowdhury, and Lazar (2026) find substantial incoherence even in capable models, but reasoning reduces it,
and models appear to favour coherent identities when they
are available (Douglas et al. 2026).
More and more coherence is also trained in directly, along
two lines. The first trains models to track their own reliability.
Larger models are overall calibrated about which questions
they can answer (Kadavath et al. 2022), and hallucination is
now diagnosed as the product of training and evaluation that
reward guessing over admitting uncertainty, to be fixed by
rewarding calibrated abstention (Kalai et al. 2025). This is
training for the very representation our account starts from:
an accurate sense of how the model is doing relative to its
goals.
The second trains for self-consistency outright. Models
are trained to behave the same across prompts that differ
only in an irrelevant cue, over their outputs or internal states
