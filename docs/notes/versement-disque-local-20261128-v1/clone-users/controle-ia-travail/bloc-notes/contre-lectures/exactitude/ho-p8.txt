 More training data helps (Figure 1a and Figure 4a). The full training set of 152,980 rows is the best
 subset for every probe. Every subset of about 3,000 rows scores 0.018 to 0.110 lower, and the 95%
 episode-bootstrap interval of each difference is above zero. More diversity also usually improves
 performance. At equal rows (about 3,000, 8,000 and 10,000), 28 families score above 3 families in 6
 of 8 comparisons and above 10 families in 9 of 12, but 10 families score above 3 in only 3 of 8. Each
 of these 18 gains has a 95% episode-bootstrap interval above zero.

Probe performance vs model scale. Figure 4b shows probes trained on the full FIBS dataset on
five Qwen3.5 and three Nemotron-3 models (Appendix B.6). Every probe scores far lower on the
smallest model of each family, 0.585–0.781 on Qwen3.5-2B and 0.613–0.760 on Nemotron-3 Nano,
than on Qwen3.5 from 27B up (0.963–0.986) and on Nemotron-3 Super and Ultra (0.903–0.961).
All probe architectures perform similarly. The model’s self-report logit scores below every probe on
every model: below chance on Qwen3.5-2B (0.301) and Nemotron-3 Nano (0.439), 0.899–0.943 on
Qwen3.5 from 9B up and 0.823–0.830 on Nemotron-3 Super and Ultra.
The follow-up question is another key consideration. Figure 6 shows that on smaller models, probes
perform worse with vague follow-ups; conversely follow-up choice matters little for larger models.
This is consistent with smaller models being more easily confused by a vague question.
