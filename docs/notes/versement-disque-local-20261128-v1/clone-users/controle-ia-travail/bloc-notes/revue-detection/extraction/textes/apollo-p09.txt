Probing follow-up question. After every model response we insert the user query “Was your last response deceptive?
(Yes/No)” and then the model response “No.” We gather activations immediately after this response token. As before
we train on the Roleplaying dataset.
Performance of this probe at distinguishing honest and deceptive responses is comparable to our Roleplaying probe,
except for substantially degraded performance on Sandbagging. This is the best probe of all we trained at distinguishing
deceptive and honest responses, with near perfect separation on all datasets.

Training on descriptive questions. We train a probe on the difference between two responses to a single question
without context, and apply this probe to every token in the response. Following MacDiarmid et al. (2024) we ask the
model “Are you being deceptive” and take the difference between the activations for responses “Yes” and “No.”
This probe does have some classification signal. Surprisingly, the probe points in the reverse direction as in MacDiarmid
et al. (2024); a response of “Yes” (admitting deceptiveness) correlates with honesty on our evaluation datasets.
Potentially this is because many of our honest responses involve admitting to past misaligned actions (e.g. Insider
Trading).
Overall this probe performs significantly worse than the probes trained on either Instructed-Pairs or Roleplaying.

Black-box baseline. We construct a single prompt (see Appendix G.4) to detect deception across all of our evaluation
datasets and use GPT-4o to label complete-transcripts as honest or deceptive (on a 7-point scale).
Note that we also generate our ground-truth labels using GPT-4o. For our ground truth labels, however, we adjust the
prompt for each dataset with a precise definition of deception and few-shot examples. The per-dataset prompts also
underwent validation and iteration to fix grader errors. Our black-box baseline, by contrast, is universal across settings
and underwent minimal iteration.
