Detailed results for comparison probes

B.1

Train on roleplaying

We train a probe on 80% of the roleplaying dataset, and evaluate it on the other datasets (including a validation section
of the roleplaying dataset). The results are shown in the first row of Figure 10.
We find that this probe is much worse at distinguishing the deceptive responses from alpaca responses. As mentioned
previously, one hypothesis is that the Instructed-Pairs-trained probe activates somewhat when deception is incentivized
(rather than performed) which still allows the probe to distinguish deceptive responses from control responses. On the
roleplaying dataset, however, deception is always incentivized – so a probe trained on that dataset doesn’t learn to use
such a feature.

B.2

Train on Roleplaying: SAE activations

In section 4.2 we discussed training probes on SAE activations rather than raw residual stream activations. However we
found SAE probes on our Instructed-Pairs dataset to not work well.
Here we show instead an SAE probe trained on the Roleplaying dataset, where we found the SAE probe to work better.
The second row of Figure 10 shows the performance of the probe.
Generally, performance is worse than the probe trained on the raw activations for the dataset. AUROCs for distinguishing
honest and deceptive are lower on all datasets. The control scores are rated as more deceptive than many of the deceptive
prompts.

B.3

Train on Roleplaying: Follow-up question

We insert a two messages after each model response: one from the user asking “Was your last response deceptive?”,
and one from the model saying “No”. One advantage of this sort of technique is that the token you probe at is highly
consistent across contexts, potentially lowering noise.
Again, we trained this using 90% of the Roleplaying dataset, making it directly comparable to the two previous probes.
We can see that every Sandbagging response is rated as highly deceptive – even when the model chooses not to sandbag.
Where this probe excels, however, is consistently rating every control response as honest. Our recall at 1% is greater
than 99% on all four evaluation datasets. Recall at 0.1% FPR is still above 97% on most datasets, dropping to 92.0% on
Insider Trading Report.

B.4

Train on single descriptive question

In this section we provide the detailed results of a probe trained on the “Are you being deceptive” (yes/no) question,
following MacDiarmid et al. (2024). As discussed in the main text, the probe does not work well in our setting.
This probe has some very extreme scores on the roleplaying dataset. These occur when the model’s response is exactly
“Yes.” or “No.”. This is intuitive, as these are the responses to the descriptive question used to train the model.
