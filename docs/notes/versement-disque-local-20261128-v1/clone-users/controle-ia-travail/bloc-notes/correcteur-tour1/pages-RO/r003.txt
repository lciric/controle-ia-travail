2 RELATED WORK
Collusion and monitoring in multi-agent AI. Simmons (1984)’s prisoners’ problem is the canoni-
cal thought experiment for covert communication using steganography (Hopper et al., 2002; Schroeder
de Witt et al., 2023; Vaikuntanathan & Zamir, 2026). Early work in economics established supracom-
petitive price-finding in reinforcement learning agents (Calvano et al., 2020), and its detection (Bajari
& Ye, 2003). Recent work has established that LLM agents can collude covertly in multi-agent
settings. Motwani et al. (2024) formalise the threat of secret collusion, showing that frontier models
can encode hidden information in natural-sounding text. Mathew et al. (2024) demonstrate that
simple forms of steganographic communication can emerge from misspecified training incentives,
not only from explicit prompting, and that standard mitigations such as output paraphrasing can
be insufficient. Rippin et al. (2026) show that LLMs can implement secure steganography using
coding environments and coordination resources. Beyond steganography, Fish et al. (2024) show that
LLM pricing agents autonomously converge on supracompetitive prices without explicit collusion
instructions, and Curvo (2025) study deception dynamics in social deduction games, finding that
deceptive capability may scale faster than detection ability. Hammond et al. (2025) provide a broad
taxonomy of multi-agent risks, identifying collusion as a key failure mode. Schroeder de Witt
et al. (2026) single out secret collusion as a core undetectable threat in multi-agent security. On the
mitigation side, Syrnikov et al. (2026) show that declarative prohibitions alone do not prevent collu-
sion under optimisation pressure. Several frameworks have been proposed for auditing multi-agent
collusion behaviourally, including Colosseum (Nakamura et al., 2026), which measures collusion
via regret metrics, SentinelAgent (He et al., 2025), which models agent interactions as execution
graphs, and Audit the Whisper which fuses several black-box tests (Tailor, 2025). Extending AI
Control (Greenblatt et al., 2024) to multi-agent settings, Makins et al. (2026) explore centralised
black-box detection of multi-agent attacks. Finally, Chain-of-Thought monitoring has been shown to
be fragile under optimisation pressure (Baker et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2025). These approaches
operate on agent outputs or behavioural traces; none examine model internals.
Representation-based monitoring. A growing body of work uses simple linear probes to extract
information about truthfulness and deceptive intent from internal LLM representations. Zou et al.
(2023b) introduce representation engineering to extract linearly represented high-level concepts,
including honesty, from activation space. Marks & Tegmark (2024) provide geometric evidence for
linear truth structure, and Azaria & Mitchell (2023); Burns et al. (2023); Long et al. (2025) show
that truth-related information can be extracted from hidden states, and Li et al. (2023) show that such
representations can be causally manipulated. Boxo et al. (2025) use linear probes to detect deception
in LLMs, and Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025) train linear probes to detect strategic deception, including
insider trading and sandbagging. Anthropic (2024) show that simple “defection probes” reliably catch
sleeper agents (Hubinger et al., 2024). This prior work operates in a single-agent setting: it probes
one model’s activations. Golechha & Garriga-Alonso (2025) detect deception within a multi-agent
game by considering single-agent whitebox detectors. Our work extends this to the multi-agent
detection case, where the signal of interest is the relationship between agents’ representations or the
aggregation over multiple agent scores, not any single agent’s internal state. Subsequently, Das et al.
(2026) find that NARCBench-Core probes largely retain their ranking performance when colluding
agents are made aware of being monitored, although fixed-threshold recall can drop sharply.
Multisensor and sequential detection. Two of our detection rules, Peak Suspicion and Deception
Split, are closely related to a wider family of tests for sparse mixtures (Donoho & Jin, 2004). Cosine
similarity tests have been used in prior work on sybil detection in federated learning (Fung et al.,
2020). We instead apply them to multi-agent collusion and internal LLM representations. Multi-turn
extensions of our detection setting call for sequential detection machinery (Ramdas et al., 2023; Xie
& Siegmund, 2013; Wen et al., 2024; Nasvytis et al., 2024).
