Preprint
In a recent security incident (METR, 2026, OpenAI/HuggingFace), agents confined to sand-
boxes (Lampson, 1973) exploited a package manager vulnerability to construct unintended, un-
monitored inter-agent communication channels and used these to coordinate on third-party attacks.
As agents become more capable, access to (Rippin et al., 2026), or emergence of (Mathew et al.,
2024), steganographic techniques and coordination resources could exacerbate collusion risks and
prevent monitoring based on communication transcripts even within inter-agent channels intended by
the designer (Schroeder de Witt, 2026).
Proposed defences against secret collusion include monitoring text outputs, paraphrasing agent
communications, and imposing behavioural constraints (Roger & Greenblatt, 2023; Greenblatt et al.,
2024; Syrnikov et al., 2026). However, these have been shown to fail against steganographic signals
designed to be indistinguishable from natural language (Motwani et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2024).
A separate line of work has shown that linear probes on model activations can detect deception in
single-agent settings (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2024). However, little prior work has
applied activation-based detection to multi-agent settings.
In this work, we introduce multi-agent interpretability as a multi-agent detection framework that
complements transcript-level monitoring with internal model activation probing. We first derive
principled detection statistics for collusion in unknown subgroups from the composite hypothesis
testing literature. Our theoretical analysis that there is no known UMP test for this setting, suggests
that no single such statistic can be expected to dominate across all collusion scenarios. As a
comparator, we also construct a number of detection heuristics with intuitive if not theoretical
support.
To evaluate how collusion detectors generalise to unseen environments, we introduce NARCBENCH,
a novel benchmark for evaluating multi-agent collusion detection under environment distribution shift.
NARCBENCH features a CORE environment with diverse committee deliberation tasks that can be
used to generate training data for detectors such as activation probes (Alain & Bengio, 2018) or LLM
judges (Zheng et al., 2023). The STEGO and TRANSFER environments then serve as holdout tasks
for transfer evaluation. TRANSFER contains diverse adversarial tasks such as code review sabotage
or news framing, and STEGO features blackjack games with a card counting collusion carried by
public signalling. Importantly, we show that STEGO cannot be separated by transcript-level LLM
judges, even stronger Claude models, without a few examples of the exact steganographic protocol
used.
Across four open-weight models, both our group-level statistics and heuristics transfer zero-shot from
CORE to TRANSFER and STEGO. Notably, the best method on every model reaches 0.99 to 1.00
AUROC, posing a real alternative in a task set up to deny transcript-level monitoring.
We also evaluate detector transfer to third-party environments with emergent, rather than instructed,
collusion. In one of these environments, only 2 of 229 collusive episodes verbalise the coordination
in the transcript. We find evidence of partial transfer to these environments, indicating that probes
fitted to instructed collusion may not fully capture emergent collusion signals.
In line with our theoretical analysis, we find that no single statistic or heuristic dominates empirically
across tasks. Overall, we establish multi-agent interpretability as a complementary monitoring
technique, while highlighting the need to evaluate detector performance on both emergent and
instructed collusion risks.
Our contributions are:
1. NARCBENCH (Neural Activation Reading for Collusion Benchmark), a three-tier bench-
mark for evaluating collusion detection under environment distribution shift: CORE (50
scenarios, 17 domains), TRANSFER (6 structurally distinct scenario types), and STEGO
(steganographic blackjack card counting). NARCBENCH already shows community trac-
tion (Das et al., 2026).
2. A statistical framework for multi-agent collusion detection based on composite hypothesis
testing with an unknown subgroup. In our case, the unknown subgroup are the colluders.
Our framework gives rise to two group-level detection statistics as generalised likelihood-
ratio tests, and the insight that no single statistic is known to be uniformly most powerful
across colluding group sizes and signal strengths. We find evidence supporting the latter
empirically in our experiments, for the tests we examine.
