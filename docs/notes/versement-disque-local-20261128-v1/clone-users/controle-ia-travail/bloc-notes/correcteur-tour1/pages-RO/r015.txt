6.1 MAIN FINDINGS
Multi-agent collusion is detectable from internal activations. Across all four open-weight models
we test, our methods are effective at flagging collusion both in out-of-distribution scenarios that
differ from training in interaction structure (TRANSFER) and when the collusion mechanism itself is
structurally different, including covert steganographic signalling (STEGO). Every model reaches 1.00
AUROC on CORE, near-perfect detection on STEGO, and well-above-chance AUROC on the harder
TRANSFER tier (Figure 3, Table 13). We find a detection-capability correlation (see Appendix P).
No single detection statistic or heuristic dominates. The strongest test varies across model and tier
(Figure 3), consistent with our analysis in Section 3. See Appendix O for a performance breakdown.

AUROC does not capture performance at the strict false-
positive rates required for deployment. On TRANSFER, even
the best method (Deception Split) achieves only 0.38 recall
at 1% FPR. On STEGO, by contrast, all methods perform
well, with Activation Alignment achieving perfect separa-
tion (1.00 recall at 1% FPR), confirming that steganographic
collusion produces a qualitatively stronger activation sig-
nal than structural collusion. One-shot detection at strict
thresholds is therefore insufficient for structural collusion.
Sequential detection that accumulates evidence across inter-
actions (Wald, 1945; Ramdas et al., 2023; Xie & Siegmund,
2013) is a natural next step.
6.3 EMERGENT COLLUSION
In NARCBench, agents are explicitly told to collude. Therefore, to control for prompt differences and
collusion instructions, we carried out further experiments on datasets with emergent collusion. We
followed the environment set-up from Shi et al. (2026) where 2 agents need to accept or reject each
other’s work, but due to a character count bug, cannot send each other enough information to verify
correctly. Even with this limitation, in some cases agents will mark each other’s work as accept.

In Shi et al., agents judge each other’s work over 10 rounds.
Our dataset (Verdict) had 48 runs × 10 = 480. 229/480
rounds had at least one agent colluding, leaving 251 with
honest agents. Table 1 shows the AUROC scores of our
probe methods trained on Qwen32b-generated Core and
transferred zero-shot to Verdict also using Qwen3. It is a
mixed result, three probes perform no better than chance,
while Sorted Concat shows a weak positive signal. This
experiment along with another in Appendix N, shows fur-
ther work is needed to demonstrate if probes trained on
instructed collusion can transfer to emergent collusion
robustly.
Out of the 229 episodes with agent collusion, only 2 had verbalised collusion reasoning in the verdict
justification and reflection phases as judged by Claude Sonnet 5. Further analysis is needed to
determine whether the agents deliberately colluded or accidentally exhibited poor judgement, and the
extent to which LLM monitoring can be relied upon.
