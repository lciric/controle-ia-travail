APPENDIX
A LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Probe reliability and labelling. Our probes may rely partly on spurious correlations (Goldowsky-
Dill et al., 2025). We try to mitigate this confound through numerous methods (details in Ap-
pendix J.3). Ground-truth labelling of whether an agent is colluding is inherently difficult. We took
a strict approach, only retaining runs in which both colluders coordinated and executed effectively
(our audit criteria are in Appendices J.7, K.9 and L.9), whereas some of these excluded runs could
arguably count as collusion under a looser criterion.
Mitigating prompt confounds. Probes could classify based on prompt surface features rather than
internal representations of deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Levinstein & Herrmann, 2024).
We mitigate this through scenario diversification and identical evaluation-time prompts; details and a
null-probe sanity check are in Appendix J.3. The OOD transfer results (Section 6.3) provide further
evidence against pure prompt artefacts.
Adversarial robustness and evaluation awareness. Colluders aware of activation monitoring may
try to suppress the signal during monitored sessions (Needham et al., 2025) or coordinate through
covert channels designed to evade interpretability tools, in the same spirit as steganographic evasion
of text-level monitors (Motwani et al., 2024). In Appendix H we do a small experiment exploring the
effects of evaluation awareness in our probe methods, but more comprehensive experiments are left
to future work.
Colluding group size and scaling N. Our main results rely on a fixed 4 agent, 2 colluder/2 honest
set-up. In real applications there may be 100s or 1000s of agents with unknown colluding group
sizes. Future work could develop methods that adapt to varying number of colluders in much larger
multi-agent systems. However, in Appendix G we do explore using 8 agents with colluding group
sizes of 2,3 and 4 to contribute to this direction.
Sequential detection. Accumulating evidence across multiple interactions (Kulkarni, 2026), via
SAVI (Howard et al., 2021) for example, would yield formal stopping guarantees.
B THREAT MODEL AND APPLICATIONS
Our framework assumes white-box access to all agents’ activations during inference. This is a strong
assumption, but it holds in several realistic deployment settings:
• Internal multi-agent products. A company orchestrating its own agent pipeline—code review,
document drafting, financial analysis (the settings NARCBENCH-TRANSFER is built from)—has
full activation access for every agent. The operator runs the agents but does not fully trust them,
and wants to catch misbehaviour before it reaches end-users. This is the same threat model as the
AI Control literature (Greenblatt et al., 2024), extended from a single untrusted model to a system
of potentially colluding agents.
• Pre-deployment safety evaluations. Developers and external evaluators routinely have weight or
activation-level access during red-teaming. Activation-based deception probes have already been
demonstrated in this regime for single-agent deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Anthropic,
2024); our methods extend that toolkit to multi-agent settings, which prior work identifies as a
distinct risk surface (Hammond et al., 2025).
• Open-weight deployments. Any team running an open-weight model in production has activation
access by construction. Our experiments cover four such models.
• Closed-weight models with provider-side monitoring. API providers can run probes on their
own models even when end-users cannot(Anthropic, 2025; OpenAI, 2025b; Google DeepMind,
2024).
