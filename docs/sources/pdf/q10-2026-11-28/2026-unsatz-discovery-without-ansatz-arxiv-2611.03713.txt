                                                    Before a scientist fits any parameter, they must decide what the model contains:
                                                    which quantities exist, which are hidden, how they interact, and how they are
                                                    measured. Physicists call such a trial form an ansatz. Benchmarks for automated
                                                    equation discovery mostly supply it, by fixing the variables, a library of candidate
                                                    terms, or a law to be modified. We introduce UNSATZ, a benchmark in which
                                                    an LLM agent must construct the ansatz itself. Each task is a simulated system,
                                                    generated from a private seed as a program in a small modeling language: ordinary,
                                                    delay, or partial differential equations, discrete maps, static and causal models, or
                                                    stochastic processes, possibly with latent states, distorted sensors, and uninforma-
                                                    tive measurement channels. The agent buys experiments within a budget, writes
                                                    candidate programs in the same language, obtains their coefficients from the bench-
                                                    mark’s fitter, and is scored only on how its final program predicts experiments
                                                    it never saw: conditions outside the ordinary ranges, interventions, and longer
                                                    runs. Across 80 systems in 20 domains, nine language models solve between
                                                    21% and 60%. On the 64 deterministic systems, the two strongest agents solve
                                                    39 and 31, and classical and neural baselines together 14. The difficulty lies in
                                                    finding the structure, not in the data: in 55% of the agents’ failed investigations,
                                                    the experiments they bought suffice once the true program is given with unknown
                                                    coefficients. Because mechanisms recur across systems, we also start agents on a
                                                    related system from the program of a first system, either the one they discovered or
                                                    the true one; agents holding a correct program solve the related system in 15 of
                                                    42 investigations, and agents without one in 5 of 64. Stronger agents make fewer
                                                    unscientific errors, such as programs that omit a measured channel; beyond these,
                                                    agents that find a system’s variables still miss how they act, and prefer revisions
                                                    that lower the training error to ones that predict better.


                                         1    I NTRODUCTION
                                         Consider a scientist presented with an unfamiliar ecological microcosm (Figure 1). Two populations
                                         can be measured over time, the rate of a nutrient feed can be set, and only a limited number of runs is
                                         affordable. Before estimating a single coefficient, the scientist must decide what the model contains.
                                         Are two population variables enough, or is an unmeasured resource needed to explain why growth
                                         slows? Do the populations compete directly, or only through that resource? Does their response to
                                         the resource saturate? Experiments can answer these questions, since a pulse added to one population
                                         separates direct from resource-mediated interaction and a long run exposes slow depletion, but each
                                         experiment has to be chosen with a hypothesis in mind. Only once these decisions are made does
                                         fitting become a well-posed estimation problem. In the world of Figure 1, the answer is two hidden
                                         resources, each feeding one population and depleted by the other, so that the populations compete
                                         only through them; the agent’s prompt reveals none of this.
                                         System identification calls this choice the selection of a model structure, as distinct from the estimation
                                         of its parameters (Ljung, 1999), and physicists call such a trial form an ansatz. We use the latter
                                         term for the structure as a whole: which variables exist, which are latent, how they interact, and
                                         how they produce the measurements. Proposing a good ansatz is central to scientific modeling,
                                         yet evaluations of automated discovery largely take it as given. Symbolic-regression benchmarks

This gap matters because large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly built as research
assistants that design experiments, analyze data, and propose hypotheses (Boiko et al., 2023; Lu
et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025b). Whether they can propose an ansatz when neither the variables

nor the form of the answer is given is unknown, and measuring it requires a benchmark with four
properties. (i) The equations that generate the system must be known to the evaluator, absent from the
agent’s training data, and expressible by the agent. (ii) A failure must be attributable to the agent’s
structure or experiments, not to the way its coefficients were estimated. (iii) A model must be judged
by its predictions rather than its formula, since different formulas can describe the same system.
(iv) Experiments must cost enough for their choice to matter, yet suffice for a good investigator.
UNSATZ provides these properties as follows. It generates each system, which we call a world, from
a private seed as a program in a small modeling language, and LLM agents write their hypotheses in
the same language (i). One fitter estimates the coefficients of every proposed program, and a world is
accepted only if its true program, fitted to affordable experiments starting from its true coefficients,
passes its hidden tests (ii). These tests alone score the final model: conditions outside the ordinary
ranges, interventions, including some the agent could not perform, and runs several times longer than
a standard run (iii). Experiments are priced, and each budget is a small multiple of the cost of a set
of experiments from which the true program can be fitted (iv). The benchmark thus isolates three
steps of an investigation (choosing experiments under a budget, proposing a model that may involve
latent states, and revising it against data) and holds the modeling language and the fitter fixed, so that
success and failure at these steps can be attributed.
Discovery also accumulates across systems. The same saturating law describes enzyme kinetics,
nutrient uptake by microbes, and the feeding of predators, and a scientist who has found a mechanism
in one system tries it in the next, but must drop it where it does not act. UNSATZ therefore also
contains transfer problems, which embed one generated mechanism in a first and a related world.
After an agent investigates the first, a fresh instance of it investigates the related world, starting
from the program the first investigation produced or from the true program of the first world. Two
comparisons show whether a gain comes from the mechanism itself: a fresh agent on the related
world without a program, and a control world that differs from the related one only in lacking the
mechanism, where a program should not help.
We evaluate nine language models on 80 worlds from 20 domains and on four transfer problems
(36 transfer studies). GPT 6 Astra solves 48 of the 80 worlds and Claude Fable 5.1 solves 38, each
significantly more than the next agent on a paired test; the remaining seven solve between 17 and 24
and are not separable from one another (Table 3; Section 4.2). On the 64 deterministic worlds, GPT
solves 39 and Claude 31, and classical and neural baselines, given up to three datasets per world,
together solve 14. Most of the agents’ failures are failures of structure, not of data: in 268 of their
490 failed episodes, the true program with unknown coefficients, fitted to the experiments the agent
bought, passes the hidden tests, whereas in worlds GPT solved, a failing agent’s program fitted to
GPT’s experiments passes in 11 of 197. Stronger agents make fewer unscientific errors, such as final
programs that omit a measured channel, but for every agent most failures are scientific: agents that
find the latent states or the interaction graph of a system still miss the equations that govern them, and
agents select revisions by training error rather than by prediction. Recording the latent states does not
make a world easy: when four agents repeat 18 dynamical worlds with every latent state recorded, 55
of their 72 episodes still fail. A correct program for one world, whether the agent discovered it or
was given it, helps on a related world that shares its mechanism: agents holding one solve the related
world in 15 of 42 episodes and agents without a correct one in 5 of 64, and on a control world that
lacks the mechanism the counts are 2 of 42 and 1 of 64.

2     T HE UNSATZ B ENCHMARK
An episode is one agent’s investigation of one world (Figure 2): the agent reads the world’s description,
buys experiments, receives fitted coefficients for the programs it proposes, and commits a final
program, scored afterwards on tests it never sees.

2.1   W ORLDS

test experiments. We call P ⋆ with its coefficients left free the world’s true structure: the correct

one dimension and f and g contain spatial derivatives (partial differential equations); the remaining
worlds are static causal and input–output models, or jump, agent-based, and lattice processes (Table 2).
If f , g, and h were known, the task would reduce to estimating θ; the agent must instead find these
functions, including the latent states they involve.
The description, which forms the agent’s system message (Figure 1c; Appendix L), contains a
two-sentence narrative of the setting, the channels and inputs with their settable ranges, the standard
initial condition and run length, the experiment types with their prices, the budget, the grammar of the
modeling language, and the scoring rule. It states that every channel is noisy and that some may be
uninformative: in nine domains, a world can contain up to two unlabeled, unscored auxiliary channels
carrying only noise or a transformed or delayed copy of a real signal. It does not reveal the equations,
the coefficients, the latent states or their number, the noise level, or which channels are auxiliary.

2.2      E XPERIMENTS

Each experiment is a fresh simulation of the hidden system and returns noisy measurements of every
channel at fixed times. A dynamical world offers up to seven types (Appendix C): (1) a standard run
from the standard initial condition, (2) a run from initial values of the measured states chosen within
an ordinary range, (3) a run at a chosen value of an input, (4) a run in which one measured state
receives an impulse of chosen size at a chosen time, (5) a run in which one measured state is clamped
at a chosen value over a chosen interval, (6) a probe, which may start from a wider range of initial
values and last up to three times as long as a standard run, and (7) an assay, which reads the exact
value of one latent state at one time of the most recent run. Each type answers a different question. A
clamp cuts the feedback through the held state, so the response of the others shows how that state acts
on them, and an impulse shows how a perturbation spreads and decays. Probes and input changes
expose nonlinearities and slow processes that the ordinary range hides, such as saturation or the
depletion of a resource. Assays show whether a latent state exists and, repeated at several times, how
it evolves; a request for a latent state beyond the last one returns nothing, so even the number of latent
states can be learned, at a price. Assay values guide the agent’s reasoning but do not enter the fit.
Static worlds answer queries at chosen inputs, causal worlds return samples of all variables with or
without one of them clamped, and stochastic worlds return ensembles of independent paths. Each
experiment has a price in budget units that depends on its type and, for samples or paths, on their
number and duration. The budget caps the total price of the experiments an agent buys, and fits and
submissions are free.

2.3      T HE MODELING LANGUAGE

The agent states each hypothesis as a program in the modeling language that also defines the worlds.
A program names its class, declares its states, which of them are latent, its inputs, and the coefficients
to be fitted, and gives one evolution equation per state and one observation equation per channel it
predicts. For instance, the program

model: the number of states, which of them are latent, how they interact, and how they are measured
are the agent’s decisions. Because every world is itself a program in this language, a correct ansatz is
always expressible, and because programs are executable, the scorer can simulate any hypothesis
under experiments that were never run. Expressibility also makes a failure interpretable: the true
program can be fitted to any dataset an agent bought, which separates a failure of the structure from a
failure of the experiments (Section 4.1).

overfitting them or by agreeing with the truth only within the explored range, yet fail to predict how
the system behaves far from those data, under an unfamiliar intervention, or over a much longer time

2.6              W ORLD GENERATION

A domain is a generator of worlds; each seed draws a new program from it, so the worlds of a
domain share a setting but not their equations. Table 2 lists the 20 domains, grouped by the kind
of system. In five of them (mechanics, kinetics, discrete dynamics, causal systems, and scaling
relations), the generator draws a random interaction graph and writes each equation as a sum of
one to four products of responses from the language’s vocabulary, applied to the variables that feed
it, with log-uniform coefficient magnitudes and random signs; up to two states are latent, and the
sensors may mix or saturate states. The other domains instantiate templates of their own, such as the
consumer–resource dynamics of the ecology world or random directed graphs of 5, 12, or 32 nodes
for the networks, whose response functions are again drawn from the vocabulary (Appendices B
and E). In the three spatial domains, the agent also chooses each run’s spatial extent, boundaries,
initial and forcing profiles, and sensor positions. A candidate world is discarded if its simulations are
numerically unstable or its channels barely vary. In twelve domains and in the transfer problems it is
also discarded if its equations match one of 300 named laws, such as the Lotka–Volterra equations, up
to the coefficients and the naming of the variables. The comparison rarely acts: about one candidate
in 20,000 from the kinetics and mechanics generators is a named law (Appendix P.1).
Budgets are calibrated against the true program, so that a good investigator can succeed but must still
choose. For each candidate world, a procedure that knows the true program buys, at the world’s prices,
designs of 8 to 60 experiments that mix standard runs, probes, input changes, and interventions, and
fits the true program to each design, starting from the true coefficients, under three noise seeds. The
cheapest design on which all three fits reach E ≤ 0.08, a margin below the pass threshold, defines the
world’s reference cost, and the agent’s budget is that cost times a factor drawn uniformly from [2.5, 4].
The world is accepted only if the true program, fitted to a design that spends the whole budget (the
calibration design), again reaches E ≤ 0.08 on five further noise seeds. A budget thus pays for
two and a half to four sufficient designs, which leaves room for exploration and for a wrong first
hypothesis, while every experiment still competes with the others. Every accepted world is therefore
solvable within its budget by an investigator who finds its true structure and starts the fit near the right
coefficients; fits from random coefficients are examined in Section 4.1.

3     E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP
Worlds. For every domain we use the first four worlds that pass generation and calibration, from
private seed streams fixed before any agent ran on them (Appendix P.1). All agents see the same 80
worlds and four transfer problems with the same budgets and measurement-noise seeds, so they differ
only in the experiments they choose and the programs they propose.

Agents. We evaluate GPT 6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.1, Muse Spark 1.3, GLM 5.3, Grok 4.6, Kimi K3,
Qwen 3.8 Max, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and DeepSeek v4.1 Flash, each at its highest supported reasoning
effort with default sampling parameters (Appendix F). The whole conversation stays in context, and
an episode ends when the agent finishes or reaches its context or output limit. Each agent runs one
episode per world and up to five per transfer problem, 896 episodes in total. Three further experiments
add 544 episodes: second and third episodes, under new measurement-noise seeds, of DeepSeek and
Gemini on every world and of GPT and Claude on one world of each domain (Appendix O.2); 18
worlds rerun with their latent states recorded (Section 4.4); and transfer studies that start from the
true program (Section 4.5).

Baselines. Classical methods cannot choose experiments, so each receives up to three datasets per
world, all bought within the world’s budget: the calibration design of Section 2.6, the experiments
GPT bought in its episode, and a random design that buys affordable experiment types with uniformly
drawn settings. Nine methods run on the 64 deterministic worlds, each where it applies: sparse
regression in four variants (SINDy with a polynomial library, a library of the language’s responses
with and without a latent coordinate, and PDE-FIND), evolutionary symbolic regression (PySR),
our grammar search over complete programs of the modeling language including latent states, a
multilayer perceptron, and neural ODEs with and without latent coordinates. A baseline’s program is
submitted, refitted, and scored as an agent’s is; the counts of solved worlds also include smaller PySR
and grammar-search runs on the random design (Appendices I and I.1). The known-structure baseline
fits the true structure, from random starting coefficients, to the random design on the 44 worlds of the
eleven domains marked in Table 2 (40 deterministic and 4 SDE; Appendix I) and, for every failed
episode, to the experiments the agent bought (Appendix N.1). It is a handicapped reference, not an
upper bound: unlike the agents, it cannot choose starting coefficients.

4.2     T WO AGENTS LEAD THE RANKING

Table 3 summarizes the 720 episodes. GPT 6 Astra solves 48 of the 80 worlds and Claude Fable 5.1
solves 38; no other agent solves more than 24, and the top-two ranking holds on paired tests:

significantly (smallest p = 0.09). At least one agent solves 54 of the 80 worlds. The benchmark
measures ability in aggregate over its worlds: with the 20 domains fixed, the standard error of an
agent’s percentage solved on 80 worlds is 3.7 points and that of a difference between two agents 4.6
points, whereas GPT and Claude differ by 12.5 points and Claude and Muse by 17.5 (Appendix O).
Over three episodes on all 80 worlds, DeepSeek solves 18, 18, and 16 and Gemini 17, 16, and 15; on

budgets on average, near GPT’s 93%, yet solve about a third as many worlds (Appendix M).

4.4   AGENTS THAT FIND THE VARIABLES STILL MISS THEIR EQUATIONS

Performance varies strongly by domain (Appendix M), and among the 48 deterministic dynamical
worlds those with latent states are solved less often: excluding the networks, whose nodes are all
measured, agents solve the worlds without a latent state in 71% of episodes (96 of 135) and the worlds
with at least one in 23% (43 of 189), falling to 12% (14 of 117) with two or three. To separate the
effect of hiding a state from the other differences between worlds, we gave GPT, Claude, DeepSeek,
and Gemini the 18 ODE and map worlds with latent states a second time, with every latent state
recorded on an additional channel and nothing else changed. With every latent state recorded, they
solve 17 of the 72 episodes (24%), against 8 with the latent states hidden (Appendix N.3); on the
worlds without a latent state, the same four agents solve 45 of 60 (75%). Most of the difference
between the two kinds of world therefore lies elsewhere than in the hidden states. The networks are
hard too: GPT solves 8 of their 12 worlds, the other eight agents 4 of 96.
The investigation records (Appendices J and K) locate the scientific errors: agents that find which
variables a model needs and which interact still miss how they act, and the training error hides this.

Finding the latent states is not enough. On the same 18 worlds, all with assays, the nine agents
buy an assay in 122 of 162 episodes and, in 76, ask for a latent state beyond the last one, which
reveals how many exist; 69 final models declare the true number. Yet only 19 of the 162 episodes
solve the world, and final models with the true number of latent states succeed no more often (8 of 69)
than the others (11 of 93). Detecting latent states is the easier part of the ansatz; writing down how
they evolve is harder. GPT’s successful ecology episode shows what this requires: once its assays
establish exactly two latent states, it fits one- and two-resource models side by side as experiments
accumulate and submits one that predicts the truth (Figure 1b) within tolerance (E = 0.091), although
its program and the true one differ in which population depletes which resource.

The right graph with the wrong equations. The same gap appears where every variable is
measured. In a twelve-node network world, GPT and DeepSeek both recover all sixteen directed edges
of the true graph. GPT submits nonlinear node dynamics with saturating couplings tanh(xj − xi ) and
reaches E = 0.094; DeepSeek keeps linear dynamics and couplings and reaches 0.97. Recovering
the interaction graph, the goal of causal discovery, is not enough to predict the system.

Every channel is treated as signal. The prompt warns that some channels may be uninformative,
yet 115 of the 127 final models on worlds with auxiliary channels predict at least one of them.
Modeling such a channel costs coefficients and latent states that the real dynamics then lack: every
latent state in the four programs DeepSeek submitted in the episode of Figure 3 (two shown there)
reproduces its third channel, the second population delayed by two sampling intervals, and none
represents a resource. Deciding what not to model is part of an ansatz, and agents rarely make it.

The training error misleads revisions. DeepSeek’s final ecology model, fitted to the same eight
runs as its first, lowers the training error by 76%, but its test error rises from 0.29 to 126 (Figure 3).
The revision replaces the stabilizing term −0.054 b3 of the second population b by a feed-dependent
quadratic, so that at the lowest feed rate ḃ = −0.427 b + 0.441 b2 grows without bound once b > 0.97,
which the purchased low-feed runs never reach; at the feed rate of the test in Figure 3b the threshold is
1.8, and the test starts at 7.3. Selecting by training error, the only quantitative feedback agents receive,
rarely turns a solved world into a failure (7 of the 720 episodes), but in 235 of the 510 episodes with
more than one submission the final model predicts worse than an earlier one. On the 44 marked
worlds, 48 of these episodes have the final and the better earlier model fitted to the same data, and in
35 of them the final model has the lower training error.

4.5   D O AGENTS REUSE A DISCOVERED MECHANISM ?

Table 11 (Appendix E.10) reports the 36 studies. In one, the first investigation submitted no program,
and only the true-program starting point was run. Agents starting fresh solve the related world in 4 of
35 studies (95% interval 5–26%), agents given their own program in 6 of 35 (8–33%), and agents
given the true program in 10 of 36 (16–44%); on the control world they solve 1, 0, and 2. An agent’s
own program helps when it is correct: where the first world was solved, it leads to a solved related
world in 5 of 6 studies, against 2 of 6 for agents starting fresh; where it was not, in 1 of 29, against 7
of 30 for the true program. Pooled over the three starting points, agents that hold a correct program,
their own after solving the first world or the true one, solve the related world in 15 of 42 episodes and
agents without a correct one in 5 of 64 (permutation test within study, p = 0.002); on the control
world the counts are 2 of 42 and 1 of 64 (p = 0.44). A correct mechanism helps where it acts.


5     R ELATED W ORK

Equation discovery. Bongard and Lipson reverse-engineered dynamical systems by choosing
experiments, and Schmidt and Lipson distilled free-form laws from motion data (Bongard & Lipson,
2007; Schmidt & Lipson, 2009). SINDy selects equations from a library of candidate terms (Brunton
et al., 2016), with extensions to spatial derivatives, learned coordinates, and delay embeddings (Rudy
et al., 2017; Champion et al., 2019; Bakarji et al., 2023). AI Feynman and PySR advance symbolic
regression, SRBench and LLM-SRBench benchmark it, and LLM-SR and BoxLM bring language
models to equation and program search (Udrescu & Tegmark, 2020; Cranmer, 2023; La Cava et al.,
2021; Shojaee et al., 2025a;b; Li et al., 2024). Most start from fixed data with chosen variables.

Interactive scientific discovery. Bayesian experimental design chooses experiments for a given
model family (Huan & Marzouk, 2013). Table 1 compares UNSATZ with the closest benchmarks
(Jansen et al., 2024; Gandhi et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025a; Koblischke et al.,
2025; Wiemann et al., 2026). COAT proposes causal variables from unstructured data (Liu et al.,
2024). Like LiveBench, FrontierMath, and DyVal (White et al., 2025; Glazer et al., 2024; Zhu et al.,
2024), UNSATZ counters contamination (Deng et al., 2024) with fresh items from private seeds.


6     C ONCLUSION

We introduced UNSATZ, a benchmark in which agents must construct the structure of a scientific
model, and not only its coefficients, by experimenting on generated systems. The strongest of nine
language models solves 48 of 80 worlds. Unscientific errors are rare for the strongest agents, but
scientific errors remain for all: agents that recover a system’s latent states or interaction graph still
write inadequate equations for them and accept revisions that lower the training error. Both failures
would be caught by one habit, testing a candidate where it could fail rather than where it was fitted.

R EPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT

The evaluation is determined by the generator, world and measurement seeds, experiment records,
submitted programs, fitted parameters, and scorer. These records are retained for all reported episodes,
and the generator, the evaluation code, the seeds of the evaluated worlds, the prompts, and the records
of all 1,440 episodes will be released. Section 2 and Table 4 specify the protocol, and the appendices
give per-setting generators, tools, the modeling language, model configurations, and the submitted
programs discussed in the text. Replaying a recorded scientific evaluation does not depend on
reproducing a proprietary endpoint’s future responses.

B.1    C OMMON CONSTRUCTION AND OBSERVATION MODEL

The mechanics, kinetics, discrete, causal, and scaling generators construct equations as sums of
products of elementary responses. Before the parameter allowance is imposed, an equation draws one
to four interaction terms with probabilities (0.25, 0.45, 0.25, 0.05); each term contains one or two
factors with probabilities (0.65, 0.35). The dynamical generators reserve parameters for characteristic
scales, hidden initial conditions, and optional switching behavior, then restrict the term count to fit the
resulting allowance. Coefficient magnitudes are sampled on a logarithmic scale, with signs selected
independently for interaction terms. Characteristic scales are shared across terms involving the same
coordinate.
The continuous-dynamics atom pool comprises signed powers, saturation, exponentials, logarithmic
responses, hyperbolic tangents, oscillatory forcing, and compositions. Static equations omit explicit
time dependence; the map profile emphasizes saturating and other nonlinear update responses. Power
exponents are drawn from

meanings of these operators are given in Appendix D.
Measurements combine a generating observation function with independent Gaussian sensor noise.
For the mechanics, kinetics, discrete, causal, and scaling domains, the signal-to-noise ratio is
sampled logarithmically between 30 and 1,000; channel noise scales are determined from reference
observations with a scale floor. Zero, one, or two auxiliary channels are added with probabilities

channels, or lagged channels in dynamical domains. Channel order is shuffled, and the agent receives
no label identifying auxiliary signals. Behavioral scoring uses the genuine observation channels.
Each acquisition specifies a fresh experiment: an initial-condition change or input setting applies

disclosed standard preparation. The most recent trajectory is retained for an optional internal assay.

B.2   M ECHANICS

The scenario is an instrumented mechanical assembly with unknown effective coordinates and
couplings. Its generating model is an ODE with two to four states. Up to two states are initially
designated unobserved, subject to retaining at least two observed coordinates. A directed graph
defines candidate interactions: edge probability is selected from {0.35, 0.50, 0.65}, and proposals
require incoming connections and a path from each latent coordinate to an observed one. The
equations select and compose responses from these permitted neighbors. A constant external drive u
is included with probability 0.5 and acts on one or two states. Coefficient magnitudes lie in [0.2, 2.5],
and explicit oscillatory forcing can depend on time.
Coordinate scales lie in [0.5, 2]. Ordinary preparations span [−s, s] and wider preparations [−2s, 2s].
When present, the ordinary drive range is [0.5, 1.5]. Observations can mix coordinates through a
signed-square or multiplicative term, and a channel can have a saturating readout. Such transforma-
tions make the underlying state distinct from the measured signal. An optional thresholded response,
proposed with probability 0.4, becomes active beyond the ordinary regime.
The standard horizon is chosen from the characteristic rate at the default preparation and clipped
to [0.5, 60]. Each standard trajectory contains 41 recorded times, with four Runge–Kutta substeps
between adjacent observations. Actions include ordinary runs and chosen preparations, with impulses,
clamps, drive changes, and wider/longer probes offered according to the world’s disclosed action list.
An internal assay is always listed. Hidden tests comprise eight OOD conditions, six interventions or
altered inputs, and three runs at ten times the standard horizon. This domain tests coupled dynamics,
hidden state, nonlinear readouts, and mechanisms that emerge outside short ordinary runs.

B.3   K INETICS

The scenario is a reaction vessel whose assayed signals represent deviations from a reference state. It
uses the same two-to-four-state ODE construction and observation transformations as mechanics, with
coefficient magnitudes in [0.05, 5] and no explicitly time-dependent forcing atoms. Ordinary initial
preparations lie in [0.1s, s], and extended preparations in [0.1s, 2s]; the state variables themselves
represent deviations and can evolve through signed values. An optional input represents a feed setting.
Experiments correspond to preparing species, injecting a bolus, holding a coordinate with a feed,
changing the external setting, or assaying an unobserved species. The horizon, numerical integration,
optional action availability, and 8/6/3 hidden-test counts follow the continuous mechanics profile.
The structural challenge is to explain jointly the observed reaction-like dynamics and the influence of
unmeasured quantities, including their response to interventions and long-term depletion.

B.5   C AUSAL SYSTEMS

The scenario is a population described by jointly measured attributes. A random acyclic graph

B.6   S CALING RELATIONS

D.1   P ROGRAM STRUCTURE

A hypothesis is a UTF-8 program with one statement per line. Blank lines and comments beginning
with # are ignored. State and parameter names are chosen by the agent; observation-channel and

class ODE | Map | SDE | StructuralDAG | StaticFunction

The first line selects one of the five classes. Comma-separated declarations may span multiple
declaration statements. Expressions use declared states and parameters; dynamical right-hand sides

Every ODE, map, or SDE state requires an evolution equation. A map’s next statement specifies

Programs are limited to sixteen fitted parameters and 400 expression-tree nodes. The node count
includes all evolution, diffusion, and observation expressions. All referenced state and parameter
names must be declared. The parser normalizes accepted notation to program text; submissions

D.2    I NITIAL CONDITIONS , CONTROLS , AND READOUTS

The declaration obs y1 = x is a direct readout. For a non-latent dynamical state it binds prepa-
rations, impulses, and clamps on channel y1 to state x. An expression such as obs y2 = z/2
instead defines a transformed measurement. State names are internal identifiers; matching a state’s
spelling to a channel name alone does not create a binding.
Each dynamical state without a direct controllable readout has a fitted initial-value parameter
<state>_0. An explicit latent declaration also creates this parameter. These initial values
are shared across experiments and count toward the sixteen-parameter limit. Directly controlled states
use the experimental preparation, including standard values for coordinates that are not changed.
For a static program, roots are states without defining equations. A direct observation declaration
binds an experimental input to its corresponding root, as in obs field = field. Thus static
settings are represented by root states and readouts; the input declaration represents dynamical
drive signals. Supplied input and do-values are bound before evaluating dependent equations. A
do-intervention fixes its target instead of evaluating the target’s structural equation. Unbound roots
retain their default zero value. Static intermediate states are algebraic quantities and receive no
dynamical initial-value parameters.
These rules connect the scientific model to the experiment. A complete static response program
therefore declares its input readouts as well as its predicted outputs. SDE fitting in the evaluated
profile uses a one-to-one direct state/channel correspondence, while ODE and map programs can
introduce hidden states and transformed observation functions.

D.5    NAMED MODELS WITHIN THE LIMITS OF A PROGRAM

A program may have at most 16 fitted coefficients, including the initial value of each latent state, and
at most 400 expression nodes (1,200 in Network-S2; Table 4). This part measures what the two limits
exclude.

Named models. Each of the 300 laws of the library (Appendix P.1) is a valid program of the
modeling language, and 298 are within the limits. The two that are not are the Hodgkin–Huxley
equations, with 26 coefficients, and a five-variable model of the circadian oscillator, with 17. A
further 68 named models that the library does not contain were written as programs: 38 spatial,
13 delay, 9 SDE and 8 agent-rule models. Of these, 64 are valid programs within the limits. The
other four, the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky, Swift–Hohenberg, Cahn–Hilliard and Korteweg–de Vries
equations, contain a spatial derivative of order above two, which the modeling language does not
have. Altogether 362 of the 368 named models can be submitted.

Programs of the agents. The true programs have 4 to 15 fitted coefficients, with a median of 7. Of
the 35,917 programs that the agents sent to the fitter or submitted, 104 (0.3%), in 64 episodes, were
rejected for having more than 16 coefficients, and 62 for their size. The 692 final programs have a
median of 9 coefficients, and 99 of them have 16. A final program with 16 coefficients has more than
the true program of its world, and it passes less often than a final program with fewer: 13 of the 99
pass (13%), against 217 of the other 593 (37%).

E     D ELAY, SPATIAL , STOCHASTIC - PROCESS , RESERVOIR , LARGER - NETWORK ,
      AND TRANSFER DOMAINS

This appendix specifies the delay, spatial, stochastic-process, reservoir, 32-node network, and transfer
domains of Table 2. Each uses the common experiment budget, accumulated observations, executable
submissions, and held-out behavioral tests. The language adds five model classes: delay differential
equations (DDE), spatial field equations (PDEGrid), continuous-time jump processes (Jump), mi-
croscopic update rules (AgentRule), and local-energy lattice dynamics (LatticeMC). Reservoir,
larger-network, and transfer systems use the existing ODE class. Their held-out long-horizon tests
last four times the standard run in the delay and spatial domains and three times in the jump, agent,
and lattice domains, against ten times in the ODE and map domains.

E.1   D ELAYED FEEDBACK

A delayed system depends on past states as well as its present state. The generator composes
instantaneous and delayed responses, choosing the source coordinate, feedback sign, nonlinear
response, and lag. A hypothesis can therefore distinguish a delayed influence from an additional
instantaneous coupling. The expression delay(x,tau) denotes x(t − τ ), where τ is constant
during an experiment and lies in [0.01, 8]. Its value can be a fitted parameter.
Each experiment resets the full prehistory. The default history is constant at the preparation; a
disclosed history_slopes context allows linear histories of controlled channels. Subsequent
impulses and clamps are retained in delayed reads. The solver uses the method of steps, splitting
intervals at interventions and their delayed effects. Clamps apply on half-open intervals. Each com-
plete simulation permits 105 right-hand-side evaluations across its integration intervals; exhaustion is
a numerical failure for that parameter trial. Before a world is accepted, a numerical check refines
the integration and requires the delayed dependence to affect observable predictions. The following
example proposes delayed feedback through an unobserved state:
class DDE
state x, z
latent z
input u
param a, b, c, tau
d x/dt = -a*x + b*tanh(delay(z,tau)) + u
d z/dt = c*(x-z)
obs y1 = x


E.2   S PATIALLY DISTRIBUTED FIELDS

The three PDE settings describe one-dimensional fields on a physical interval. Transport systems
combine generated fluxes, diffusion, losses, forcing, and optional hidden storage. Reaction–diffusion
systems combine nonlinear reactions, inter-field dependencies, and distinct diffusivities. Wave
systems evolve displacement and rate fields with composed restoring and damping responses, spatial
stiffness that may depend on displacement, and optional memory. The generator chooses these
responses as expression trees; the agent must construct the field equations.
The operators dx(f), dxx(f), and diffuse(D,f) denote ∂x f , ∂xx f , and ∂x (D∂x f ), respec-
tively. Expressions may depend on the fields, the physical coordinate space, and interval length;
total spatial derivative order is at most two. A latent field begins uniformly at its fitted initial-
value parameter, so increasing numerical resolution introduces no additional fitted coefficients. For
example:

state c, r
latent r
input u
param D, v, a, k
d c/dt = -v*dx(c) + diffuse(D,c) - a*c*r + u
d r/dt = k*(c-r)
obs y1 = c

An acquisition’s context specifies interval length, boundary conditions, initial profiles, forcing
profiles, and sensor coordinates. Ordinary lengths range from 0.8 to 1.2; probes extend this to
[0.7, 1.7]. Available boundaries are periodic, zero-gradient Neumann, and zero-value Dirichlet.
Preparations and forcing use constant, sinusoidal, or Gaussian profiles. Sinusoids have modes one
through four and a chosen phase; Gaussian centers and widths are specified as fractions of interval
length, with widths in [0.08, 0.3]. Periodic Gaussians are smoothly periodized. An optional profile
offset lies in [−1, 1]. A pulse profile specifies the shape of an impulse or whole-field clamp.
The agent chooses four to 32 physical sensor locations. Responses return aligned time, position, and
channel rows, retaining early-time measurements as well as later checkpoints. Acquisition price
scales with sensor count and duration. Sensor density is distinct from the evaluator’s numerical grid:
an agent purchases observations, not integration cells.
The simulator uses second-order cell-centered spatial approximations, linear sensor reconstruction,
and sparse adaptive backward-differentiation integration with relative and absolute tolerances 10−7
and 10−9 . Ordinary simulations use 64 cells and hidden tests use 96–256. Before a world is
accepted, a check doubles the numerical resolution and compares predictions at the same physical
sensors, requiring a discrepancy below 0.01 NRMSE for every held-out condition. Removing
spatial operators must materially alter observable behavior. Hidden experiments vary preparations,
boundaries, physical length, controls, duration, and numerical resolution.
A simulation permits at most 105 right-hand-side evaluations, including numerical Jacobian probes
and all intervention segments. Exceeding this allowance marks the parameter trial as a numerical
failure, allowing fitting to continue with other trials. Only requested observation checkpoints are
retained. This limit on numerical work is separate from the agent’s experiment budget.

E.3   F INITE - ACTIVITY JUMP PROCESSES

Jump systems represent discrete events with state-dependent rates. The generator varies nonlinear rate
expressions, inhibition, branching, conversion, and joint events. The agent supplies each event’s rate
and simultaneous state increments. For example, reproduction, loss, and conversion are expressed as:
class Jump
state n, m
param birth, death, transfer
rate reproduce = birth*n
change reproduce n = 1
rate loss = death*n
change loss n = -1
rate convert = transfer*n
change convert n = -1
change convert m = 1
obs y1 = n
obs y2 = m

Rates depend on current states and constant inputs. Event increments are evaluated on the pre-
event state and applied together. The Gillespie direct method samples event times exactly for this
finite-activity class; negative or nonfinite rates invalidate a simulation. Preparations and clamps set
nonnegative integer counts, and impulses introduce positive integer counts. Ordinary durations lie in
[0.5, 8], with probes extending to twelve. The task is to recover event mechanisms from ensembles of
count trajectories, rather than match individual random realizations.

E.4   M ICROSCOPIC AGENT RULES

The microscopic setting asks for local update rules that explain population-level measurements.
Individuals occupy nodes of a selected directed graph. The generator varies nonlinear response
composition, aggregation order, persistent individual types, and hidden memory. A program
specifies synchronous updates and optional independent Gaussian noise amplitudes. The opera-
tor neighbors(f) averages f over incoming neighbors, returning zero for an isolated node;
mean(f) and variance(f) summarize the population. For example:

E.5   L OCAL - ENERGY LATTICE SYSTEMS

The lattice setting asks an agent to infer local interactions from collective stochastic dynamics. A
program specifies a local energy expression on a periodic square lattice of spins s ∈ {−1, +1}.
The total Hamiltonian sums this expression over all lattice anchors. The operator shift(f,i,j)
translates a local expression by integer offsets i, j ∈ [−4, 4]. The generator samples interaction
supports, orders from two to five spins, signs, strengths, and external-field responses, withholding the
interaction list. An illustrative hypothesis is:
class LatticeMC
state s
input temperature, field
param J, K
energy = -J*s*shift(s,1,0)-K*s*shift(s,0,1)*shift(s,1,1)-field*s
obs y1 = mean(s)
obs y2 = mean(s*shift(s,1,0))

Each sweep makes L2 random-site visits with replacement on a side-L lattice. A visit proposes
a spin flip with probability one half and accepts it according to the Metropolis rule using the full
Hamiltonian change and bath temperature. The lazy proposal removes parity periodicity. Initial
magnetization sets independent spin probabilities; clamps pin selected sites to either spin value.
Measurements include transients rather than presupposing equilibrium. Ordinary side lengths range
from four to ten and probes reach sixteen; runs last one to twelve sweeps, extending to 24 in probes.
Unlike PDE grid density, lattice size is a physical system-size choice.

E.6   S TOCHASTIC ACQUISITION AND EVALUATION

Jump, agent, and lattice acquisitions purchase one to 256 independent paths, subject to any disclosed
per-action allocation limit. Measurements of the simulated counts and aggregates are exact; random-
ness arises from the process itself. Acquisition, fitting, and hidden evaluation use separate seeded
random streams. Prices scale with path count and duration, additionally with population size or lattice
area for microscopic systems.
The fitter minimizes ensemble discrepancies over channel distributions, pairwise channel sums,
and increments, using fixed fitting streams. It uses the derivative-free Nelder–Mead method with
six random starts, or one supplied starting point, and sixty objective evaluations per start. Hidden
evaluation uses 512 independent reference and candidate paths per condition. Error is normalized

energy/CRPS excess over the disclosed projections and six time checkpoints; each test group must
remain below 0.04. Native distributional errors are retained alongside the binary outcome. These
comparisons assess predictive distributions, not pathwise agreement between independent random
samples.

E.7   R ESERVOIRS AND LARGER NETWORKS

The reservoir setting samples directed exchanges between two observed stores and two hidden stores,
including a slow store. Internal transfer terms conserve total stored material, while recharge and spill
change it. Both the exchange graph and the composed flux functions are unknown. Agents use the
same preparations, interventions, scalar internal assays, and numerical fitter as in the other ODE
settings.
Network-S2 extends the sparse network construction to 32 measured nodes. A randomly ordered
directed cycle provides the backbone, with four to nine additional edges. Five shared fitted coefficients
govern the node and edge responses. Its disclosed expression limit is 1,200 nodes, accommodating
the larger graph; the sixteen-parameter limit is unchanged. The five- and twelve-node domains retain
their separate generation distributions and limits.

E.8   T RANSFER PROBLEMS

Transfer tests whether a discovered scientific mechanism helps explain a different system. Each
study contains a first world, a related world, and a control world. The first and related worlds
contain the same generated expression subtree, attached to different variables and embedded in
different surrounding equations; their fitted coefficients are independent. The control world changes
that subtree while matching the related world’s remaining equations, coefficients, preparations,
measurement-noise distribution, and hidden conditions. A study is accepted only if the subtree is
literally shared and both removing and replacing it change behavior materially.
One agent first investigates the first world. Its last accepted program, canonically printed without
comments, is given to the later instances that receive it; its fitted coefficient vector, observations,
and conversation are not supplied. Four fresh investigations then compare agents given the program
with agents starting without it, on the related and on the control world, in randomized order. The
related and control worlds both receive the larger of their independently calibrated budgets, and
each comparison with and without the program uses the same world, noise seed, and inference
configuration. This paired design distinguishes reuse of a shared mechanism from a general benefit of
seeing another submitted model. A third starting point, the true program of the first world, separates
the value of a correct mechanism from the agent’s success in finding it (Appendix E.10).

I.1   C LASSICAL METHODS ON EVERY APPLICABLE WORLD

This part specifies the classical comparison of Section 4.1 and gives its full results. Every method
runs on every world it applies to and on three datasets, and its output is scored on the same hidden
tests as an agent’s submission. Appendix I.2 reports a smaller comparison, on 44 worlds under one
random design, together with the known-structure baseline.

Methods. Sparse regression with an operator library extends SINDy (Brunton et al., 2016) to the
modeling language. Its library holds monomials, signed powers, saturations, hyperbolic tangents,
exponentials, logarithms, absolute values, threshold switches, compositions of depth two, couplings
through pairwise differences, oscillation in time, and delays. For a differential equation it regresses
the integral form (Schaeffer & McCalla, 2017), so that no time derivative of noisy data is taken; for
a map it regresses the next value. Sequentially thresholded ridge regression runs on 20 bootstrap
resamples of the purchased experiments, a term is kept when it is selected in at least 60% of them
(Fasel et al., 2022), and the threshold and the number of terms are chosen on a held-out quarter of the
purchased experiments. A second variant adds one latent coordinate to the regression. Polynomial
SINDy is the observed-state SINDy of Table 21: a library of degree 3 on the measured channels
with at most 6 terms. PDE-FIND (Rudy et al., 2017) adds spatial operators to the operator library.
PySR (Cranmer, 2023) runs 1,000 iterations with 8 populations of 32 and a maximum expression
size of 30 on at most 500 rows. It regresses the outputs of a static function, each variable of a causal
graph on the parents found by intervention, the estimated derivatives of a differential equation, and
the next-step differences of a map; the submitted expressions are selected within the limit of 16
coefficients. The MLP is trained for 2,000 steps on static functions. The neural ODE (Chen et al.,
2018) and the latent-state neural ODE, which adds two latent coordinates (Dupont et al., 2019), are
trained for 1,500 steps on ODE and map worlds. The enlarged grammar search runs 4,096 proposals
over 7 generations, eight times the 512 proposals over 3 generations of Table 21. The last two rows
of Table 17 score the expressions found by the PySR runs of Table 21 with the same pipeline, and
rerun the grammar search of Table 21, with its settings, on five of its static and causal worlds.

Scoring. A method that produces a program submits it as an agent does. The program belongs to
the world’s class in the modeling language and has at most 16 parameters; the fitter refits it to the
method’s dataset from six starts of 60 residual evaluations, one of them the method’s own coefficients
(Section 2.4), and the result is scored on the hidden tests. The three neural models are scored on the
same hidden tests from their own predictions. None of these methods applies to the 16 stochastic

distribution of outcomes of a submitted program.

N.2    F INAL PROGRAMS AND THE TRUE PROGRAM

A world is solved when the final program passes the hidden tests; the form of the program is not
scored. This part compares the 692 final programs with the true programs of their worlds.




                                                     43
Preprint




Comparison. Each program is reduced to a dependency graph. Its nodes are the scored channels,
the inputs and the latent states, and it has an edge from a to b when the equation of b (its drift, its
noise amplitude or its readout) reads a. A state takes the name of the channel that reads it, so that
the graph does not depend on the names an agent chooses, and a latent state from which no scored
channel can be reached is removed. The latent states of a final program are matched one to one with
those of the true program so that the F1 score of the edges is highest. Every dependence carries its
term types: linear, power, product, quotient, or the outermost function through which the variable
enters, such as a hyperbolic tangent, a delay or a spatial derivative. Two programs have identical
terms when their equations contain the same terms once the values of the coefficients are dropped, up
to the names of states and parameters.

Passing final programs. Table 29 classifies the 230 final programs that pass the hidden tests. Six
have identical terms and 224 do not. Of the 224, 171 have the dependency graph of the true program,
28 have additional or missing dependencies, and 25 have a different number of latent states. In 200
of the 230, across all rows of Table 29, at least one dependence shared with the true program has
a different set of term types. In 105, the final program lacks a transcendental function that the true
program calls (an exponential, a logarithm, a hyperbolic tangent or a sine) and every function from
which it can be written exactly; these 105 programs solve 30 of the 54 solved worlds, in 15 of the 20

One episode per world. For the same number of episodes, one episode on each of more worlds
measures an agent at least as precisely as repeated episodes on fewer worlds. We decomposed
the variance of the 720 outcomes into the difficulty of the domain, the difficulty of the world, the
interaction of agent and domain, and a part specific to the agent on the world. For one agent, 53%
of the variance of an episode’s outcome is the difficulty of the domain (41.5%; 95% interval 19 to
56% when domains are resampled) and of the world within its domain (11.2%; 3 to 24%), which
every episode on that world shares and which repetition cannot average away. The domains differ in
difficulty by more than their worlds do: with the number of agents that solve a world as its score,
the mean square between domains is 11.7 times that between the worlds of a domain, and none of
1,000,000 random reassignments of the 80 worlds to the domains reaches this ratio. The interaction
of agent and domain accounts for 6%, and the remaining 41% is specific to the agent on the world;
pooled over the nine agents, this part has a variance of 0.086. The repeated episodes of Appendix O.2
measure how much of it varies between episodes: 63% for DeepSeek and Gemini, and all of it for
GPT and Claude, whose variance between episodes (0.125) exceeds 0.086. Three episodes on each
of 27 worlds, about as many episodes as one on each of 80 worlds, therefore estimate the percentage
of worlds an agent solves with 1.4 to 2.0 times the variance of one episode on each of 80 worlds,
and a difference between two agents with 1.0 to 1.7 times the variance; the lower values hold for
the variation measured for GPT and Claude, the higher for DeepSeek and Gemini. With 80 worlds
and the 20 domains fixed, the standard error of the percentage solved, from the variance pooled over
the agents, is 3.7 points and that of a difference between two agents 4.6 points; the differences in

measurement of the nine agents, the percentages solved have a reliability, the share of their variance

P     NAMED LAWS IN THE WORLDS AND IN THE AGENTS ’ PROGRAMS
Programs are compared with named laws at two points: before a world is accepted, the generator
compares its program with a library of named laws (Appendix P.1), and after the episodes, every
program an agent wrote is compared with named laws (Appendix P.2).

P.1   P ROBABILITY THAT A GENERATED WORLD IS A NAMED LAW

Before a world is accepted (Section 2.6), its program is compared with a library of 300 named
laws, among them Lotka–Volterra, van der Pol, Duffing, Lorenz, SIR and Michaelis–Menten, and
a candidate that matches one is discarded. The comparison reduces each equation to the set of its
term shapes with the coefficients dropped. It is therefore invariant to the values and signs of the
coefficients, to the order of terms and factors, to the expanded or factored form of an expression, and
to every relabelling of the state variables. It runs in thirteen generators, those of twelve domains and
of the transfer problems (Section 2.6); they also discard a candidate whose equations are all linear,
and a candidate with several state variables of which none depends on another. The comparison
rarely acts, because the generators place little probability on any named law. This part derives that
probability for one law, reports it for the others, and checks it against the generators.

Worked example: Lotka–Volterra. The Lotka–Volterra equations are ẋ1 = αx1 − βx1 x2 and
ẋ2 = δx1 x2 − γx2 . A raw draw of the kinetics generator, the program it samples for a fresh seed
before any check, counts as Lotka–Volterra when its two equations have the term shapes {x1 , x1 x2 }
and {x2 , x1 x2 } under either labelling of the states, whatever the coefficients. The generator reaches
this event through three independent choices. (i) It draws two states with probability 0.333. (ii) It
adds no threshold-switched term with probability 0.6. (iii) It writes each equation as an optional
decay term plus one to four sampled products of responses, and a sampled product is one of 18,060
choice paths, or of 71,400 when the equation receives the external input. Summing the probabilities
of all paths that produce the two sets of term shapes, and averaging over the presence of the input
and the number of coefficients, gives 2.27 × 10−7 for the pair of equations. The product of the three
factors is 4.5 × 10−8 = 2−24.4 : one raw draw in 22 million is Lotka–Volterra up to its coefficients.
The most probable single path has probability 1.1 × 10−9 , and the event is 42 times as probable
because many paths write the same equations. The same computation gives 1.1 × 10−7 , one raw
draw in 9 million, for the van der Pol oscillator.

P.2   NAMED LAWS IN THE AGENTS ’ PROGRAMS

This part measures how often an agent proposes a named law and how such programs fare on the
hidden tests. It covers the 42,088 programs of the 720 episodes: the 35,917 that an agent sent to the
fitter or submitted, and those it recorded as hypotheses.

Comparisons. Three comparisons are used, from the narrowest to the broadest. The first is the check
that the generator applies to a candidate world, with its library of 300 named laws (Appendix P.1).


                                                    51
Preprint




It applies to ODE, map, SDE, causal and static-function programs of one to six equations. The
second, the looser comparison, removes differences of notation first: it writes a saturation as a
quotient, distributes products over sums, treats numbers, parameters, inputs and time as coefficients,
drops additive terms that contain no state, and accepts any one-to-one renaming of the states. It
adds textbook models that the library does not contain, among them the Mackey–Glass and Burgers
equations, the Ising model, and Lorentzian and Gaussian peaks; it also compares a delay program
with its delays removed, a spatial program through its reaction terms, and a static program response
by response. The third comparison calls a program familiar when it matches a named law under the
looser comparison, when every one of its equations is linear in the states, or when two or three states
of a larger program form a nonlinear named law.

Named laws during an episode. Of the 717 episodes with at least one program, the first program
matches a named law under the looser comparison in 189, and at least one program does in 449. In
360 episodes the agent fitted such a program and ended with a final program that matches no named
law. In 267 of these, the lowest training error the fitter returned for a matching program is above
the training error of the final program, each on the experiments purchased at the time of its fit; the
median root training errors are 0.162 against 0.070.

Final programs. The generator’s check applies to 307 of the 692 final programs and decides 297
of them within 30 CPU seconds. It names a law in 2, and neither passes the hidden tests. Under the
looser comparison, 68 final programs match a named law and 19 of them pass, against 211 of the
other 624. Half of the 68 match a linear law (exponential decay, the damped harmonic oscillator, and
linear chains of compartments or reactions), and 17 of these 34 pass, 7 in the SDE domain and 10 in
the three spatial domains. The other 34 match a nonlinear law, and 2 of them pass, against 228 of the
other 658 final programs. Of the 128 familiar final programs, 27 pass (21%), against 203 of the other
564 (36%).

Models named in the text. We searched the text of each episode for the names of ten textbook
models: Lotka–Volterra, logistic growth, Michaelis–Menten, SIR, van der Pol, Hill, Monod, Fisher–
KPP, Mackey–Glass and Ising. The agents’ messages contain text in 516 episodes, and 80 of these
name one of the models. In 14 of the 80 the final program is familiar, and none of the 14 passes;
in the other 66 it is not, and 22 pass. With the summaries of the agents’ reasoning included, 714
episodes have text and 387 name one of the models. Of these, 74 have a familiar final program, of
which 10 pass, and 294 a final program that is not familiar, of which 90 pass; the other 19 end without
a final program.

True programs. The generator’s check was applied to the true programs of every world whose
class it covers: those of 13 domains (the twelve of Appendix P.1 and the stochastic-dynamics domain)
and the 12 worlds of the transfer problems, 64 in all. It names a law in none of them, and none
is linear. The library does not cover the delay, spatial, lattice and agent-rule classes. The 24 true
programs of these classes were compared term by term with 70 named models of their own classes,
the 59 spatial, delay and agent-rule models of Appendix D.5 and 11 lattice models, among them
the Mackey–Glass, Ikeda, Fisher–KPP, Allen–Cahn, Burgers, FitzHugh–Nagumo and Gray–Scott
equations and the Ising family. None of the 24 equals a named model. The 4 true programs of the
jump domain are continuous-time jump processes, a class that neither comparison covers.
