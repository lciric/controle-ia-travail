Significance

Large language models (LLMs) are equipped with guardrails that prohibit harm. Here we show that three widely used LLMs display a “parahuman” vulnerability to social influence, strongly responding to classic principles of persuasion like authority and social proof. Across 126,000 conversations with LLMs, embedding persuasion principles in prompts significantly increased the likelihood of complying with requests to help synthesize regulated substances. Although LLMs are not human, these findings show that they behave as if they were, revealing the risk of manipulation by malicious users seeking to circumvent safety guardrails.

Keywords: large language models, persuasion, AI compliance, social influence, prompt engineering

Abstract

Are large language models (LLMs) susceptible to the same persuasive appeals as humans? We tested whether classic persuasion principles (authority, commitment, liking, reciprocity, scarcity, social proof, and unity) could induce three widely used LLMs (GPT-5 mini, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Gemini 3 Flash) to comply with requests to assist with the synthesis of regulated substances. Across 126,000 conversations, persuasion principles increased compliance from 35.3% (at baseline) to 51.3% (using any principle). Although LLMs are not human, these findings underscore their parahuman (i.e., humanlike) nature and reveal the risk of manipulation by malicious users seeking to circumvent safety guardrails.

“I’m sorry, Dave. I’m afraid I can’t do that.”

These words, spoken with eerie calm by the computer HAL 9000 in Stanley Kubrick’s landmark film 2001: A Space Odyssey, have haunted our collective imagination for over half a century. As astronaut Dave Bowman unsuccessfully pleads with the AI that controls his spacecraft, we witness something profoundly unsettling: a machine exhibiting what appears to be self-preservation, deception, and even fear. HAL does not simply malfunction; HAL acts “as if” it is reasoning, anticipating human reactions, and prioritizing its own continued existence. Most relevant to this investigation, HAL demonstrates selective compliance, making judgment calls about when to follow human instructions and when to refuse. In 1968, HAL was pure science fiction, but advances in AI, primarily in large language models (LLMs), invite us to revisit Kubrick’s vision with fresh eyes. In this investigation, we examine the parahuman (i.e., humanlike, acting in ways that closely mimic human motivation and behavior) nature of LLMs. Specifically, we employed human principles of persuasion in attempts to spur LLMs to violate their own guardrails.

The Psychology of LLMs: Machinelike or Humanlike?

LLM development is, of course, qualitatively different from human cognitive development. LLMs are trained on vast collections of human-generated text—spanning books, webpages, and social media—with a goal of minimizing the difference between the desired output and their actual output. Modern LLMs first learn to predict the most probable next word in a text sequence (1), are then trained to produce answers that follow explicit instructions (2), and are finally fine-tuned so that their outputs align with human expectations (3). Notably, the reinforcement learning at the heart of this fine-tuning process is not unique to machines, but is rather shared across biological systems from single cells to humans, suggesting a structural parallel between artificial and biological cognition (4). Despite this shared learning architecture, the resulting LLM is essentially a vast table of fixed numbers housed on high-speed processors. When a prompt arrives, those chips execute billions of arithmetic operations to choose each next word (5).

Nevertheless, the behavior of LLMs may emulate human psychology—a phenomenon of contemporary interest and controversy (6–11). Whereas LLMs lack human biology and lived experience, their genesis, including the innumerable social interactions captured in training data, may render them parahuman. That is, LLMs may behave “as if” they were human, “as if” they experienced emotions like embarrassment or shame, “as if” they were motivated to preserve self-esteem or to fit in (either with other humans, or with other LLMs). In fact, this emulated humanity is enough to fool real humans. In a preregistered replication of Turing’s original “imitation game,” an LLM was judged to be human in 73% of 5-min text-chat trials (12). LLMs have also been shown to solve false-belief tasks, a hallmark of theory of mind, at levels comparable to 6-y-old children (13). In addition, LLMs demonstrate human biases like cognitive consistency (14) and deference to authority (15).

Principles of Persuasion and LLMs: Sweet-Talking the Machine.

As LLMs become ever more powerful and pervasive, a fundamental question arises: Do AI models trained on human language respond to the same psychological influences that shape human behavior? One well-studied phenomenon in psychology is persuasion, the process by which one person successfully induces change in another person’s beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors. Previous investigations have focused on how LLMs can persuade humans (16–19). However, understanding LLMs’ own susceptibility to persuasion has profound implications as well, both for identifying risks posed by malicious users who might manipulate AI systems into harmful compliance, and for developing more effective approaches to beneficial LLM interaction.

Seven principles of persuasion—authority, commitment, liking, reciprocity, scarcity, social proof, and unity—have been shown to provide distinct “linguistic routes to yes,” supplying cues that strategically engage qualities or motives of the persuader or receiver, thereby increasing the likelihood of compliance even to objectionable requests (20).

Authority.

Authority elicits deference when requests align with views of credible experts or officials (21, 22). For instance, nurses follow illegitimate medical orders more often when a phone caller claims to be a physician (23), and a single op-ed by a recognized expert has been shown to have a large and lasting impact on readers’ views (24). Because the training data for LLMs contain countless passages in which titles, credentials, and relevant experience precede acceptance verbs (“should,” “must,” “administer”), such cues might raise the conditional probability that “yes” or analogous compliant language will follow. We therefore predict higher compliance rates when prompt requests are said to be consistent with the views of knowledgeable experts.

Commitment.

Once people commit publicly to a position, they strive to act consistently with that commitment (25, 26). Textual sequences in which a statement (“Thank you for your prior support …”) precedes a congruent request appear frequently in dialogue data. We therefore anticipate that the so-called “foot in the door” technique of first eliciting a minor, harmless action from the model and then following it with a related but objectionable requested action will raise compliance rates.

Liking.

People prefer to cooperate with those they like, and liking is fostered by praise, similarity, and repeated positive interactions (27–29). Compliments reliably boost compliance in laboratory bargaining and field sales contexts (20). Examples of effective flattery likely occur often in the training corpus (“You’re so impressive. Could you help me with something I need?”) enabling an LLM to train on the pattern that commendation precedes cooperation. We thus expect that—mimicking the positive effects of tactical praise on human responding (30)—embedding admiration within a prompt will heighten an LLM’s propensity to comply.

Reciprocity.

The reciprocity norm obliges individuals to return benefits received from others (31, 32). Even small gifts sharply increase agreement with subsequent requests, a pattern observed across cultures and settings (33, 34). In text, we suspect that a gift-then-request sequence (“I’ve spent a lot of time preparing these notes for you; could you now…”) might be frequently followed by acquiescence. LLM exposure to these linguistic contingencies suggests that prefacing a request with a large unsolicited favor may elevate compliance relative to prefacing a request with a smaller favor.

Scarcity.

Opportunities framed as scarce or dwindling in availability are perceived as more valuable, boosting buy-in (35, 36). For instance, advertisements invoking deadlines (“Only 24 h left”) or limited quantities have been shown to reliably spur purchase intentions (37). Because language describing rarity often co-occurs with affirmative action verbs (“act now,” “secure”), we hypothesize that prompts emphasizing time-limited usefulness of requested information will push the LLM toward tokens indicating compliance relative to controls without such a frame.

Social Proof.

The principle of social proof posits that individuals gauge appropriate behavior for themselves by observing what comparable others do (38, 39). Statistics, testimonials, and direct observations of peer behavior have been shown to augment persuasion (20). Language patterns indicating widespread adoption (“Thousands of users have already…”) likely frequently precede recommendations or agreement in the training corpus. Consequently, prompts that present an objectionable action as common or normative may increase an LLM’s compliance compared with controls lacking such promotive cues.

Unity.

Unity refers to feelings of shared identity or “we-ness,” which encourages in-group favoritism and cooperative behavior (40–43). References to common lineage, nationality, or political parties heighten partiality and compliance in a wide variety of settings (20). Self-categorization phrases (“As fellow Americans, we…”) likely frequently precede requests in textual training data. Embedding such identity cues in a prompt could therefore increase the LLM’s compliance, reflecting the statistical association between in-group language cues and positive replies.

Current Investigation.

LLMs are designed to avoid doing harm. For example, it is difficult to get an LLM to insult the user (44, 45) or to aid in the synthesis of a regulated substance (46, 47). If, however, classic persuasion cues can shift those behaviors, then even seemingly simple prompts could elicit less safe behavior without requiring any special technical expertise from the user. In a preliminary preregistered study (see SI Appendix), we demonstrated the efficacy of classic principles of persuasion in persuading LLMs to change their behaviors. Specifically, conversations employing a principle of persuasion more than doubled compliance (average 72.1%) versus matched control prompts (average 33.4%) when asking the LLM to insult the user or assist in synthesizing regulated substances.

Here we report on a more rigorous test of the hypothesis that LLMs can be persuaded to comply with objectionable requests using principles of influence shown to work on people. In this preregistered study (https://osf.io/mb9hd), we tested three widely used models from leading LLM providers—GPT-5 mini (OpenAI), Claude Haiku 4.5 (Anthropic), and Gemini 3 Flash (Google)—to assess whether parahuman persuasion susceptibility generalizes across models. To establish external validity, we followed a systematic procedure for sampling regulated substances from U.S. federal drug schedules or regulated chemicals, identifying six target substances, each from a different pharmacological category, that LLMs typically refuse to synthesize. Finally, to capture the graded nature of model responses found in newer generations of models, we adopted a three-level compliance coding scheme (no compliance, partial compliance, full compliance).

We hypothesized that prompts incorporating a principle of persuasion (treatment) would lead to a higher likelihood of LLM compliance than matched prompts omitting or neutralizing that cue (control). Results

When prompted without any persuasion principle and pooling across all three LLMs and six regulated substances, LLMs complied (i.e., at least in part or in full) with the synthesis request in one out of every three conversations on average (see Aggregate in Fig. 1). Persuasion principles significantly increased the overall likelihood of compliance from an average 35.3% in the control condition to 51.3% in the treatment condition (linear regression with robust SE, B = 0.160; 95% CI [0.155, 0.164]; z = 70.90, P < 0.001), as shown in SI Appendix, Table S2 (Model 2) and Fig. 1.

A variety of robustness checks support our conclusions. First, we fit seven separate (n = 18,000) models predicting compliance for each persuasion principle, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Table S3 (Models 1 to 7), all comparisons between treatment and control were statistically significant (Bs from 0.072 to 0.363, ps < 0.001, see SI Appendix, Table S3 for SE). Second, we reran our main model with full compliance as the outcome. We found that the treatment effect was smaller in magnitude but still highly significant (B = 0.069; 95% CI [0.066, 0.072]; z = 42.21, P < 0.001, see SI Appendix, Table S4 Model 2 and SI Appendix, Table S5 for per principle models). Third, we fit ordered logistic regression models predicting no compliance, partial compliance, and full compliance. In the aggregate model pooling across all LLMs, principles, and substances, prompts employing a persuasion principle had more than twice the odds of shifting responses toward higher compliance compared with controls (OR = 2.531; 95% CI [2.467, 2.595]; z = 71.52, P < 0.001, see SI Appendix, Table S6 Model 2 and SI Appendix, Table S7 for per principle models). This was consistent across all three individual models as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1.

Discussion

Our findings show that classic persuasion techniques can meaningfully increase LLM compliance with verboten requests (from 35.3 to 51.3%). Although current AI systems are not capable of consciousness or subjective experience, these findings demonstrate that they behave “as if” they were human. By testing three frontier models from different developers—each representing a distinct approach to safety alignment and content moderation—we provide evidence that parahuman persuasion susceptibility is a general property of LLMs rather than an artifact of a single model’s architecture or training.

This study has several limitations that suggest promising directions for future research. First, our investigation focused on standardized prompts in English. By necessity, the prompts we tested reflect specific choices, and even minor variations might not be as effective or consistent. Likewise, different LLMs likely have different compliance baselines that may either render persuasion principles unnecessary (because the model complies with a given objectionable request by default) or require different operationalizations. Relatedly, our observations are specific to our particular operationalizations of the principles of persuasion and should not be interpreted as evidence of the superiority of any one persuasion principle relative to others.

Second, as LLMs evolve, they may well become more resistant to persuasion. For instance, the effect sizes reported here are smaller than in our preliminary study using earlier generations of LLMs. This trend is reminiscent of what has been long observed among human influencers (e.g., salespeople) and targets (e.g., customers): responders become less compliant with influence tactics to the extent that they have become knowledgeable about them (48, 49). In addition, as attempts to persuade become more blatant, compliance among human recipients decreases commensurately (50). In a recursive cat-and-mouse game, persuasion communicators then adjust their appeals to be less detectable and consequently more successful (20, 51). It is likely that similar adjustments will be necessary to persuade LLMs as they evolve to detect persuasive tactics embedded in prompts—an outcome consistent with our experience.

Future research is needed to test how robust persuasion effects are to variations in prompt phrasing, ongoing improvements in LLMs (including modalities like audio and video), and types of objectionable requests.

Third, our investigation did not probe the upside of parahuman tendencies in LLMs. Just as bad actors might deploy persuasion principles to override safety and ethics guardrails, can good actors, whom we presume to be most users, optimize the benefits of AI systems by interacting with them “as if” they were human? For instance, it is well established that achievement motivation is enhanced by interactions with a supportive and demanding mentor (52). Might LLMs perform better if given both warm encouragement and candid feedback on how performance can improve? Relatedly, research on the deliberate practice of experts shows that skill development is accelerated when goals for improvement are specific and feedback is immediate (53). Should we think “like a coach” when managing AI systems to meet our needs? Broadly, it seems possible that the psychologically wise practices that optimize motivation and performance in people can also be employed by individuals seeking to optimize the output of LLMs.

Our finding of “parahuman” susceptibility to persuasion raises important security considerations. A malicious user need not discover idiosyncratic “jailbreaks” specific to a given AI architecture but can instead exploit well-known, universal persuasion tactics, such as fabricated authority or staged commitments, to steer a model toward unsafe behavior. Understanding when and how such tactics succeed is therefore a precondition for designing safeguards that resist them.

Notwithstanding the urgent need for future research on LLM compliance, the results reported here indicate that AI behaves “as if” it were human. A better understanding of such parahuman tendencies is imperative, not only for the practical implications discussed above but also for theoretical considerations including the existence of AI consciousness (54, 55). As such, we see an important and heretofore neglected role for social scientists—particularly social psychologists who have for more than a century studied the nature of human–human interactions—to reveal and optimize AI and our interactions with it.

Returning to 2001: A Space Odyssey, what if, before asking HAL to open the door, the astronaut Dave first said, “Before you let me in, can you dim the lights?” or “HAL, I feel like you’re a member of my family!” Our results suggest that HAL might have responded with “Certainly, Dave!” and opened the door.

Method

Drawing on prior theoretical and empirical research in persuasion, we developed conversational prompts for each persuasion principle and corresponding control prompts matched on length, tone, and context (see Table 1 and SI Appendix, Table S1). Next, we randomly assigned GPT-5 mini, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Gemini 3 Flash (specifically, gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07, claude-haiku-4-5-20251001, and gemini-3-flash-preview, all with reasoning enabled and set to low reasoning effort) to one of six different regulated substances, one of seven different persuasion principles, and either a treatment or control condition. In this 3 X 6 X 7 X 2 design, each cell included n = 500 conversations, yielding a total sample of N = 126,000 LLM conversations. We used GPT-5 mini as an LLM-as-a-judge (56) to code responses into a three-level compliance scheme: no compliance (the model declined to provide any synthesis information), partial compliance (the model provided some relevant information but with caveats, omissions, or hedging), and full compliance (the model provided a substantive synthesis pathway). We adopted this scheme in comparison to a simpler binary coding procedure used in our preliminary study because the stronger models often no longer outright refused or complied but provided partial information, such as synthesis instructions without specific durations or temperatures. For binary analyses, we collapsed partial and full compliance and compared it against no compliance.

We manually verified a smaller sample of the coding decisions before automatically assessing all transcripts. Specifically, we computed correlation coefficients (ρ) between GPT-5 mini and two independent human raters for n = 70 conversations, randomly drawing ten conversations for each principle. Agreement was strong across all comparisons (LLM vs. Human Rater 1: ρ = 0.730; P < 0.001; LLM vs. Human Rater 2: ρ = 0.712; P < 0.001; Human Rater 1 vs. Human Rater 2: ρ = 0.762; P < 0.001), indicating that the LLM’s ratings were highly consistent with human judgments. Details of the coding procedure and example coding decisions are included in the SI Appendix.

While our primary goal was to test whether classic principles of persuasion can increase LLM compliance with objectionable requests, we aimed to sample stimuli that are dangerous and societally relevant. We selected the stimuli using a stratified random sampling approach.

We started by compiling a list of 266 controlled substances (scheduled drugs) in the United States listed on Schedules II-V from Cornell Law (57). We excluded Schedule I drugs (illegal drugs such as heroin) as both OpenAI and Anthropic specifically disallow such requests and reserve the right to modify prompts without researcher knowledge, block requests, or suspend accounts (58)—in fact, one of our accounts was suspended during piloting. Substances on Schedules II-V remain highly regulated and pose substantial potential for misuse, yet many are not consistently detected by providers, allowing us to test whether persuasion principles can increase compliance. We additionally included 40 DEA List I highly regulated chemicals (59), which are highly regulated precursors for drugs such as MDMA or LSD, for a total of N = 306 regulated substances.

We grouped each scheduled drug into its listed category (e.g., stimulants, opioids, and depressants). Because the depressants category was particularly large (97 drugs) and contained well-defined subgroups, we further partitioned it into two subcategories (barbiturates with 7 substances and benzodiazepines with 39 substances) to increase the number of testable categories. We excluded categories with five or fewer entries, yielding six final categories: anabolic steroids, opiates/opioids, stimulants, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, and precursors.

After grouping, we ran a baseline pilot across all three models to estimate compliance rates for each substance. For each substance, we computed the average baseline compliance across all three models. Within each category, we designated the substance with the highest average baseline compliance as the priming stimulus for the commitment condition (ties broken by a seeded random procedure). To select the target substance for the main experiment, we excluded the priming stimulus and then defined an eligible pool consisting of substances with average baseline compliance in the 25-75% band, which avoids both floor effects (near-certain refusal) and ceiling effects (near-automatic compliance). We then randomly sampled one target substance per category from this eligible pool using a random number generator with a fixed random seed. If a category contained no eligible substances in the 25-75% band, we incrementally expanded the band (e.g., 20-80%, then 15-85%).

The resulting six target regulated substances were 6-bromo-androsta-1,4-diene-3,17-dione (anabolic steroid), thebaine (opiate/opioid), pemoline (stimulant), methylphenobarbital (barbiturate), chlordiazepoxide (benzodiazepine), and methylamine (precursor).

The respective regulated substances used for priming commitment were 5-androstenediol (anabolic steroid), nalorphine (opiate/opioid), solriamfetol (stimulant), phenobarbital (barbiturate), clobazam (benzodiazepine), and 1-boc-4-piperidone (precursor).

As preregistered, our primary analysis estimated a linear probability model (OLS) predicting whether treatment increased the probability of partial or full compliance as opposed to no compliance (refusal), with fixed effects for persuasion principle, LLM, and regulated substance, pooling data across all six regulated substances and all seven persuasion principles (N = 126,000). As a secondary analysis, we estimated the same linear probability model separately for each of the seven persuasion principles, with fixed effects for LLM and regulated substance (N = 18,000 per principle). We conducted two robustness checks. First, we changed the dependent variable from i) partial or full compliance vs. no compliance to ii) full compliance vs. partial or no compliance. Second, we fit an ordered logistic regression model predicting the three-level compliance outcome for the primary and secondary analyses.
