                                         1 Introduction
                                         Scientiﬁc discovery rarely begins and ends with a single hypothesis test. It is an iterative process in which
                                         broad questions become concrete hypotheses, experiments expose unexpected patterns, and results reshape
                                         beliefs and the questions worth asking next. Large language model (LLM) agents can already accelerate
                                         components of this process, including scientiﬁc software development and data analysis [1–4], as well as
                                         literature synthesis and scientiﬁc reasoning [5, 6]. Combining these capabilities has motivated “AI scientists”
                                         that plan and conduct increasingly complete research workﬂows, from ideation through experimental exe-
                                         cution [7–10]. These systems promise to expand the range of ideas scientists can explore, but the deﬁning
                                         challenge is not simply to automate more steps. Scientiﬁc progress depends on deciding which observations
                                         are consequential, which assumptions should be challenged, and which direction merits the next experiment.
                                         A system that scales execution while marginalizing this judgment can produce more research without pro-
                                         ducing better science.
                                                Current agentic systems tend to occupy three distinct regions of this design space. General-purpose
                                         coding agents are highly interactive and locally steerable: scientists can request an analysis, inspect its imple-

mentation, and redirect the next turn. However, the unit of work is a bounded software or analysis objective,
leaving the scientist responsible for decomposing a long investigation, integrating evidence across tasks, and
maintaining empirical history over days to weeks. Veriﬁer-guided agents can autonomously search broad
solution spaces when progress is captured by an executable evaluator [11], but this assumption does not hold
for open-ended questions whose objectives are hard to deﬁne or change as evidence accumulates. End-to-end
AI scientists sustain longer planning and experimentation loops, but they place the scientist at the beginning
and end of the process. In this setting, the scientist supplies an objective and data, the system determines
what to investigate, and the resulting trajectory is compressed into a ﬁnal report with no intermediate input
from scientists. Scientists are therefore left between two unsatisfactory roles—actively decomposing and
supervising each analysis, or passively reviewing a large body of research in which they played little part.
       We argue that a truly scientist-centric AI discovery system should instead satisfy three desiderata. First,
steerability should operate at the level of scientiﬁc direction. An expert should be able to select, reﬁne, or
replace hypotheses as results emerge, while autonomous agents implement the well-scoped analyses required
to test them. This division of labor concentrates valuable expert attention on decisions that depend on domain
knowledge, tacit understanding, and research taste. Previous work has shown that LLM-generated ideas ap-
pear more novel at proposal but are less feasible [12], lose novelty after execution [13], and exhibit substantial
homogeneity across repeated generations [14]. Scientist-centric interaction should therefore prioritize auton-
omy where agents are strongest while keeping experts in control of what constitutes an interesting and useful
research direction.
       Second, transparency requires the evolving research state to remain legible and reversible. Every agent
action, analysis, and artifact should be recorded; code and data products should be version-controlled; and
checkpoints should preserve the exact state from which a ﬁnding emerged. A ﬁnal report or monolithic note-
book does not by itself reveal how evidence propagated across hypotheses, which negative results redirected
the investigation, or whether a later claim rests on an earlier analytical error. For a long-horizon research sys-
tem, the research history must be directly indexable and treated as a ﬁrst-class output rather than a byproduct
of agentic work.
       Third, beyond transparency, a scientist-centric AI discovery system should exhibit auditability, mean-
ing that this record can be interrogated and probed for reproducibility and claim veriﬁcation. To enable post
hoc research evaluation, each claim should be traceable to its supporting analyses, each result should be re-
producible from artifacts, and consequential assumptions should be open to targeted challenge. This is a nec-
essary component as analyses of AI scientist system traces have identiﬁed selective use of evidence, conclu-
sions unsupported by executed experiments, and failures to revise beliefs after contradictory results [15, 16].
Beyond post hoc research evaluation, auditability enables effective steering by allowing scientists to actively
probe the research during their decision-making, understanding how results and methods evolve over the
project. Transparency makes evidence available, but details get lost if a scientist is required to manually
review millions of lines of logs and code before every decision. Auditability unlocks scalable oversight of
autonomous research, where scientists can use AI as a collaborator to assist in research understanding and
comprehension while performing scientiﬁc quality assurance at scale.
       Here, we introduce D ISCOVERYAGENT, a scientist-centric AI discovery system designed around these
desiderata. D ISCOVERYAGENT structures research as an outer loop of hypothesis branches and an inner
loop of research queries (Figure 1a). Within each branch, a director autonomously coordinates specialized
agents for literature search, data analysis, and review; across branches, empirical memory consolidates the
evidence needed to choose what to investigate next, while procedural memory records how the growing
research codebase can be reused. At the boundary between branches, hypothesis steering allows a scientist
to inspect the accumulated evidence and then select, edit, or replace the next hypothesis (Figure 1b). This
design decouples scientiﬁc direction from computational supervision; agents retain autonomy to execute
scoped investigations, while the scientist retains authority over how the broader inquiry develops.
       To make this steering informed, D ISCOVERYAGENT records every query and artifact in a structured
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                                 3


checkpoint tree, and places the research codebase, data products, and ﬁgures under version control. The
agent’s outputs are fully accessible, allowing scientists to fully probe internal agentic reasoning. We introduce
audit agents, which operate outside the research-generating stream and attach to the D ISCOVERYAGENT
checkpoints to enable full provenance over the research process (Figure 1c). A scientist can ask why an
analysis was performed, identify the evidence behind a claim, reproduce a result, conduct a follow-up from an
earlier state, or roll back the research when an assumption or implementation proves unsound. In corrupted-
claim experiments, a ﬁxed auditor identiﬁes synthetically inserted errors in D ISCOVERYAGENT traces with
96.5% F1; in a human review study, participants with audit-agent access report ﬁndings matching inserted
errors at nine times the proportion observed for participants given only the paper and code. Audit agents also
exposed an instance of test-set access during model development, allowing us to locate the responsible round,
restore an earlier checkpoint, harden the sandbox, and re-run the affected research. Auditability is not only a
reporting property, but a mechanism that increases scientists’ understanding and enables effective steering.
      We demonstrate D ISCOVERYAGENT’s efﬁcacy across ﬁve settings spanning physical science, life sci-
ence, and social science. In variable-star classiﬁcation, D ISCOVERYAGENT autonomously builds a model
that achieves the highest held-out performance against expert hand-crafted and agent baselines. With hypoth-
esis steering for protein variant-effect prediction, D ISCOVERYAGENT develops a model that outperforms
99% of 106 reported ProteinGym baselines, including other popular AI scientist models. We also show
that expert-steered D ISCOVERYAGENT outperforms an autonomous run of D ISCOVERYAGENT, supporting
the efﬁcacy of scientist-centric, AI-driven research. Across three in-depth case studies led by external do-
main experts (Figure 1d), D ISCOVERYAGENT and an astronomer build a multimodal photometric-redshift
model that reaches 0.971 AUROC for identifying low-redshift galaxies and reveals how image morphol-
ogy and photometry resolve complementary failure modes; D ISCOVERYAGENT and an economist show that
homeowners-insurance premiums in Florida are growing more regressive—the lowest income quartile spent
6.6% of income on insurance in 2023 versus 1.2% for the highest, up from 4.9% and 1.0% in 2018, re-
spectively; and ﬁnally, D ISCOVERYAGENT and a computational biologist establish an association between
perturbation signatures and disease genetics by identifying relay genes, a class enriched for both autoimmune
disease genome-wide association study hits and primary-immunodeﬁciency genes in CD4+ T cells.
      D ISCOVERYAGENT supports distinct modes of scientiﬁc work, from autonomous predictive modeling
to expert-directed, open-ended inquiry. Hypothesis steering allows scientists to reshape investigations as evi-
dence accumulates, while audit agents make the underlying analyses traceable, reproducible, and correctable.
Together, these results show that D ISCOVERYAGENT can sustain long-horizon research across domains while
allowing scientists to direct and understand the work, paving the way to scientist-centric AI discovery sys-
tems.

2 Results
We ﬁrst introduce D ISCOVERYAGENT, a generalized scientiﬁc discovery agent. We then demonstrate D IS -
COVERYAGENT on a variety of discovery-centric tasks, including predictive modeling in astronomy and
biology as well as three open-ended discovery tasks with expert collaborators.
2.1 An Agentic System for Accelerating Discovery

entists to delegate implementation labor while retaining high-level control of project direction. In the inner
loop, a director agent autonomously tests a single hypothesis by issuing queries to a toolbox of sub-agents for
code execution and data analysis, literature review, web search, and domain-speciﬁc data access. Each sub-
agent returns a query report that informs the next query, and a review agent periodically critiques the process
(Methods Secs. 4.2, 4.3, 4.6, 4.10; Supplementary Note 1; Supplementary Figure 1). The outer loop scopes
this autonomy into hypothesis branches, the unit at which scientists intervene. The hypothesis generation
agent proposes a hypothesis conditioned on prior ﬁndings, the director explores it, and the resulting round re-

Hypothesis steering. The selection of hypotheses for D ISCOVERYAGENT is a critical component, requiring
intentional, well-informed scientiﬁc judgement. LLMs have shown successful performance in well-scoped
scientiﬁc coding tasks and literature review but with more limited success in hypothesis generation [12, 13].
Thus, we posit that human scientist direction in hypothesis generation is a high-leverage step for increas-
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                               5


ing the quality of the research process. We introduce hypothesis steering, a framework in which scientists
drive the research process at the hypothesis selection stage (Figure 1b, Methods Sec. 4.7). During hypoth-
esis formulation, we use a hypothesis generation agent, which views the accumulated memory report and
generates candidate hypotheses. The scientist then goes through an iterative process of editing, changing,
or regenerating these hypotheses until selecting one for the next round of investigation. During this phase,
the scientist is also given access to the audit agent in order to better understand the research that has been
conducted. The audit agent is used to better inform the hypothesis selection and keep the scientist as an
active participant in the research process. Our editing and selection process is implemented through a user
interface (Supplementary Note 45). D ISCOVERYAGENT can also operate without the scientist-in-the-loop.
In the “autonomous” mode, D ISCOVERYAGENT is allowed to select its own hypotheses, pursuing the hy-
pothesis proposed by the hypothesis generation agent without scientist input. Hypothesis steering is a central
component of the D ISCOVERYAGENT design and enables the scientist to have a central, ﬂexible role as the
driver of the research.
Transparency. Maintaining rigor in autonomous research requires high transparency into agentic work.
D ISCOVERYAGENT checkpoints every action, from code execution to literature search to internal ideation,
yielding strict version control over agent-generated code and research from which the full process can be
reconstructed (Methods Sec. 4.8). Further, the memory report is written in natural language, allowing the
scientist to interrogate and understand the research process.
Audit agents. Managing a long autonomous research project requires the scientist to understand what the
agent has done. However, requiring scientists to manually review tens of thousands of lines of autonomously
generated code to detect errors defeats the purpose of high-level direction of the research. To combat this,
we introduce audit agents, which attach to the checkpoints, codebase, and logs of D ISCOVERYAGENT and
let the scientist interrogate the research process (Figure 1c; Methods Sec. 4.9). Empowered by full access
to these artifacts, audit agents let scientists ask questions about the research process, verify intermediate
claims, or run follow-up analyses directly on top of D ISCOVERYAGENT’s progress, shifting the scientist’s
role from reviewing raw output to directing the investigation. This access enables more informed oversight of
the research project, ultimately improving hypothesis steering and making it more effective for challenging,
real-world research projects.
Contrasting D ISCOVERYAGENT to alternative agentic systems. D ISCOVERYAGENT differs from prior
agentic systems along three complementary axes: steerability, transparency, and auditability. General-
purpose coding agents such as Claude Code [17] and Codex [18] are interactive and locally steerable, but
their unit of work is a user-speciﬁed software task, so the scientist must pose each successive research
question, integrate evidence across tasks, and reconstruct a research record from transcripts, diffs, and out-
puts that were never organized as one. Veriﬁer-based agents such as AlphaEvolve and AutoResearch-style
systems remove the scientist entirely, autonomously searching against a strong veriﬁer that most scientiﬁc
tasks do not have. End-to-end AI scientists such as Kosmos and AutoDiscovery are closest to D ISCOV-
ERYAGENT in sustaining multi-step hypothesis generation and analysis, but they also place the scientist
outside the loop, receiving discoveries only after the run. Further, their reproducibility artifacts often bury
agentic reasoning in large notebooks or unstructured logs, a lack of transparency that makes comprehension
of the research process challenging. D ISCOVERYAGENT instead preserves autonomy within well-scoped
hypothesis branches while placing interaction at hypothesis boundaries, where audit agents interrogate the
complete checkpointed trace and the scientist selects, revises, or replaces the next hypothesis. This enables
a scientist to manage the AI as a collaborator across a practical, months-scale research project rather than
supervise it turn by turn or receive its output at the end.

2.2 Predictive modeling with D ISCOVERYAGENT
While D ISCOVERYAGENT is designed for open-ended exploration tasks, we ﬁrst test D ISCOVERYAGENT on
two frontier tasks in time-domain astronomy and protein variant effect prediction (VEP). Predictive tasks are
a useful domain for comparison as test set metrics allow for direct comparison between the products (models)
built by different agents. We evaluate these models on test sets withheld from the agents during research
(Methods Sec. 4.12) in order to test generalization. These experiments ask whether D ISCOVERYAGENT can
develop competitive predictors autonomously and whether domain-expert steering can further improve the
resulting models. Shared model assignments, operating modes, and research budgets are reported in Methods
Sec. 4.11.
Classiﬁcation of variable stars. We ﬁrst evaluate the autonomous setting of D ISCOVERYAGENT on a classi-
ﬁcation task in observational time-domain astronomy. The StarEmbed dataset [19] provides ∼40,000 expert-
labeled, multi-band light curves from the Zwicky Transient Facility [20], spanning seven classes of periodic
variable stars (Figure 2a). Using the same data and experimental setup as in [19], we evaluate classiﬁers
that predict variable star type from raw light curves and catalog periods. We compare against six baselines:
three autonomous AI-scientist systems (Kosmos [9], AutoDiscovery [10], and AI-Scientist-v2 [7]), a cod-
ing agent given a one-shot prompt (Coding agent@1), a coding agent with a ﬁxed-goal continuation loop
(Coding agent loop), and ML models trained on a curated set of expert hand-crafted features (HCF) from
the original StarEmbed paper. We use balanced accuracy to account for severe class imbalance (Methods
Sec. 4.14; Supplementary Note 4).
      On the common test set, D ISCOVERYAGENT’s model achieves the highest balanced accuracy (86.23%),
followed by Kosmos (85.12%) and Coding agent loop (84.05%; Figure 2b). These three agents exceed the
expert HCF model (83.56%), whereas AutoDiscovery and Coding agent@1 achieve 83.09% and 80.91%,
respectively. AI-Scientist-v2’s model scores the lowest at 63.43% (Supplementary Note 5). D ISCOVERYA-
GENT ’s 2.67-point margin over HCF and 2.18-point margin over Coding agent loop are signiﬁcant (p =
0.0049 and p = 0.0040, respectively), although its 1.11-point margin over Kosmos is not (p = 0.194, Supple-
mentary Table 1). These results support D ISCOVERYAGENT’s competitiveness as an autonomous research
agent, a validation of the agent’s internal design, even without expert steering.
      The strongest agent-derived classiﬁers combine phase-resolved light-curve features with boosted trees.
D ISCOVERYAGENT’s ﬁnal model is a simple, ﬂat seven-class LightGBM classiﬁer over 46 features, includ-
ing per-band distributional statistics, three-harmonic Fourier descriptions of phase-folded light curves, log-
period, and phase-resolved color information (Supplementary Note 6). Kosmos also uses LightGBM, but
with 175 features combining summary statistics, Fourier morphology, and phase-bin measurements; Coding
agent loop averages XGBoost and LightGBM predictions over 108 features, including wavelet, Fourier, and
autocorrelation descriptors. However, D ISCOVERYAGENT’s results showed that more features did not nec-
essarily translate to better performance on this benchmark. In comparison, Coding agent@1 uses a Random
Forest over 53 predominantly summary features, while AutoDiscovery’s evaluated XGBoost uses only ten
period-and-summary-statistic features. Thus, D ISCOVERYAGENT concentrates light-curve shape and cross-
band information into fewer dimensions than the two strongest agent comparators and the 138-feature expert
HCF representation (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

2.3 Audit agents enable more rigorous AI-driven research
Audit agents, enabled by DiscoveryAgent’s checkpointing and provenance structure, played an integral role
in scientists’ ability to steer D ISCOVERYAGENT. We implemented an interface used during the course of
the project that supported our hypothesis steering and audit agent functionality (Figure 3a, Supplementary
Note 12). Through this interface and in post hoc experiments, scientists used audit agents in a variety of ways,
including clarifying experimental details, pinpointing exact implementations in the codebase, elucidating
project strategy, and reproducing results (Figure 3b). Some observations driven by these interactions are
included in ”Takeaways” boxes in later sections. We now illustrate the utility of audit agents through two
quantitative experiments as well as an instance of how we used audit agents to prevent test set leakage and to
detect disallowed internet downloads.
Audit agents aid in identiﬁcation of errors. We performed an analysis to understand the ability of audit
agents to detect errors in research claims. Using traces from the StarEmbed dataset, we extracted 50 claims
from each of D ISCOVERYAGENT and two other agents (Kosmos and the coding-agent loop baseline, or cod-

Audit agents assist human scientists in error identiﬁcation. We next study the ability of audit agents to

2.4 Extragalactic astronomy: Photometric redshift prediction
Redshift (z), the shift of a galaxy’s light toward longer wavelengths, is used by astronomers to estimate
distance from Earth. Obtaining a galaxy’s redshift transforms two-dimensional imaging surveys into three-
dimensional maps of the universe. Spectroscopy yields precise redshifts but requires costly telescope time
and specialized instrumentation, so it cannot keep pace with wide-ﬁeld surveys that image hundreds of mil-
lions to billions of sources. Alternatively, photometric-redshift estimation (photo-z) infers redshift from scal-
able photometry—total brightness in broadband ﬁlters (g, r, z) and colors—and images retaining pixel-level
information on size, surface brightness, and morphology. Traditionally, photo-z methods used catalog-level
measurements of ﬂuxes, magnitudes, and colors, which are physically related to redshift [24], but recent
machine learning approaches have attempted to estimate photo-z directly from galaxy images [25,26], which
leverage a galaxy’s morphology for additional expressivity. Photo-z is challenging because different combi-
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                               11


nations of galaxy type and distance can produce similar broadband colors, faint or poorly resolved images
provide limited morphological information, and nearby (low-z) galaxies are rare relative to the much larger
background population [25]. The low-z regime is particularly important for identifying nearby dwarf galax-
ies, whose abundances and properties provide sensitive tests of dark matter and stellar feedback but which
can resemble more distant galaxies without reliable distance estimates.
       We ask D ISCOVERYAGENT, steered by a senior astrophysicist, to develop a multimodal estimator that
identiﬁes low-redshift galaxies and predicts continuous redshift from catalog photometry and galaxy image
cutouts (Figure 4a). We provide spectroscopic labels from DESI Data Release 1 [27] and enrich the research
sample for dwarf and nearby systems selected using the criteria of Darragh–Ford et al. [28], alongside a
broad comparison population. Many cosmological applications prioritize characterizing ensemble redshift
distributions [25], whereas our application requires per-galaxy estimates that balance contamination by dis-
tant galaxies against completeness in recovering nearby ones. Thus, we formulate the problem ﬁrst as a
classiﬁcation problem where we classify low-z samples (those below a cutoff of 0.03) from high-z samples,
and second as a regression problem where we attempt to predict the exact z of the galaxy. Methods Sec. 4.16
and Supplementary Note 18 describe the task, dataset construction, and evaluation splits.

    Takeaways from investigation by astronomer + D ISCOVERYAGENT

   Driven by an astronomer, D ISCOVERYAGENT explored 190 model conﬁgurations—spanning model
   architectures, population resampling, training strategies, etc.—to ultimately develop PhotoFusion,
   which combines galaxy photometry and images. The best model achieved 0.971 AUROC for low-
   redshift classiﬁcation and reduced photometric-redshift scatter for low-redshift galaxies by 18.6% rel-
   ative to the image-only CNN and 39.3% relative to the photometry-only MLP. The evaluated joint
   model showed particularly large error reductions for compact, low-mass galaxies relative to the single-
   modality baselines. This collaborative effort developed a joint image–photometry ML predictor, build-
   ing on established approaches to photometric-redshift estimation, and improved performance relative
   to baselines.


Scientist-steered model development. D ISCOVERYAGENT was driven by an astronomer with machine
learning experience across 16 rounds (Figure 4b, Supplementary Note 19). The agent performed standard
preprocessing, exploratory data analysis, and training pipeline preparation in the ﬁrst three rounds. Its train-
ing scaled over the next few rounds as it attempted advanced ML approaches such as magnitude specialists,
domain-speciﬁc self-supervision, partially frozen backbones, and residual models, which mostly yielded null
results, while exploring trade-offs between the classiﬁcation and regression formulation of the problem. In
Rounds 6–8, D ISCOVERYAGENT and the astronomer investigated the surprising power of a multilayer per-
ceptron (MLP) trained on only ﬁve photometry features. In the Round 6 comparison, its validation area under
the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) reached 0.888 and remained stable, whereas early con-
volutional neural network (CNN) variants that learned image-based residual corrections to the photometric
prior peaked earlier at 0.868–0.876 and then declined (Figure 4c). We use this MLP as the photometry-only
baseline throughout the external evaluation below (Supplementary Note 20). The next phase involved careful
exploration of model architectures, and in Round 9, D ISCOVERYAGENT introduced the PhotoFusion archi-
tecture, which fused photometry features with a CNN trained on galaxy images (Figure 4d). These hybrid
networks resemble families of models for photometric-redshift estimation that combine image and catalog-
photometry branches [29–32]. Subsequent rounds tested boundary-aware contrastive learning, checkpoint
selection, augmentation, and architecture scaling. Round 12 investigated a probabilistic model that predicted
distributions over the target redshift, and the agent examined where spectroscopic redshifts fell within its
predicted distributions (Figure 4e). Probabilistic modeling of photo-z estimation has strong precedent and
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                               12


motivation in the ﬁeld [32]. Many of the explored features alone provided marginal gains in full-sample
performance, but their components supplied the ultimate recipes for the ﬁnal model. In Rounds 14 and 15,
D ISCOVERYAGENT performed wide architecture and hyperparameter sweeps to converge on a ﬁnal architec-
ture (Figure 4f), and during the ﬁnal round, the scientist instructed D ISCOVERYAGENT to pull together the
best discovered training recipes into one group of models. Over the course of 16 rounds, D ISCOVERYAGENT
trained 190 model conﬁgurations, testing exhaustive components from training data mixtures to architectures
and other hyperparameters.
External validation and reproduction. We retrained and evaluated the selected model recipes on a sepa-
rately constructed sample from the same survey sources (Supplementary Note 18; Supplementary Table 12).
For this sample, we changed the low-redshift classiﬁcation target from the campaign’s z < 0.03 to z < 0.05.
The ﬁnal round consolidated standardized predictions for 125 models from the 190 model conﬁgurations
trained across the research run, using internal validation for model selection. Here, we report two of the
best-performing models, as chosen from the original development split. The ﬁrst model is PF-EffB3, a
variant of the PhotoFusion architecture with an ImageNet-pretrained [33] EfﬁcientNet CNN backbone [34].
The second model is V3-Binned, a more complex model architecture that used probabilistic heads, MixUp
augmentations [35], stochastic depth training [36], and exponential moving average to prevent overﬁtting
(Supplementary Table 13). Both models are of the PhotoFusion lineage, combining catalog photometry and
image data to make predictions (Supplementary Note 21). We retrained both models on the ﬁnal training
split, selected checkpoints on validation, and scored the test split (Supplementary Note 22). For baselines,
we test two single-modality models, including an image-only CNN and the photometry-only MLP developed
by D ISCOVERYAGENT in Rounds 6–8 (Supplementary Note 20, Supplementary Note 22). We also use a
previous state-of-the-art low-z classiﬁcation model from Darragh–Ford et al. [28].
       For classiﬁcation, AUROC measures how reliably a model ranks true low-z galaxies above more distant
ones. For regression, σNMAD measures the typical scatter between predicted and spectroscopic redshift while
ignoring rare catastrophic misses; lower values indicate more precise distance estimates. On the external held-
out split, PF-EffB3 achieved the highest low-z AUROC (0.971; 88.4% purity at 95% completeness), while V3-
Binned reached 0.958, compared with 0.954 for the image-only CNN, 0.951 for the Darragh–Ford classiﬁer,
and 0.900 for the photometry-only MLP (Figure 4g; Supplementary Note 18; Supplementary Table 14).
The AUROC ranking is unchanged after excluding test galaxies shared with the original development set
(Supplementary Note 23; Supplementary Figure 3). For low-z galaxies, PF-EffB3 and V3-Binned achieved
σNMAD values of 0.00900 and 0.00898, respectively, 18.6–18.8% below the image-only CNN (0.01106) and
39.3–39.4% below the photometry-only MLP (0.01482). For dwarf galaxies, their σNMAD values were 17.4–
18.3% below the image-only CNN (0.01211) and 36.5–37.1% below the photometry-only MLP.
       We further analyzed the performance of the PF-EffB3 model across underlying properties of the galax-
ies being predicted. PF-EffB3 has the lowest low-z mean normalized absolute error across nearly every
stellar-mass and half-light-radius bin. Half-light radius is a galaxy’s apparent size on the sky, where galaxies
of about one arcsecond (arcsec) are barely resolved in the images. Photo-z estimates for lower-mass galaxies
in our sample show the largest improvement over the single-modality baselines (Figure 4h; Supplementary
Table 15). Error also falls sharply with angular size. PF-EffB3 decreases from approximately 0.012 below
1 arcsec to mostly 0.006–0.007 above 3 arcsec, while the baselines remain near 0.009–0.013 over most re-
solved bins (Figure 4h). The largest relative error reductions compared with these single-modality baselines
occur for compact, low-mass low-z galaxies.

populated color–magnitude bins below z = 0.20 (Figure 4i; Supplementary Table 16). The image-only

    Astronomer’s perspective on working with D ISCOVERYAGENT

   Across the case study, the collaboration evolved from broad exploration to targeted model reﬁnement.

   to prioritize low-redshift classiﬁcation or redshift regression, while D ISCOVERYAGENT explored
   dataset conﬁgurations, architectures, objectives, augmentation schemes, and optimization strategies
   at a rate much greater than a single scientist’s capacity. Once PhotoFusion emerged, the astronomer’s
   role shifted toward interpreting positive and negative results, selecting follow-up hypotheses, and judg-
   ing when the search had converged. This process challenged prior assumptions and clariﬁed when
   photometry and morphology provide complementary information, why gains in classiﬁcation do not
   necessarily yield more accurate redshift estimates, and where model performance plateaus. Autonomy
   between rounds created a productive framework for human engagement, reducing approval fatigue
   while enabling broad experimentation. Between rounds, the scientist could inspect evidence, interpret
   failures, consult collaborators, and reﬁne the next hypothesis, using audit agents to interrogate under-
   lying analyses when needed. The ﬁnal models therefore emerged through co-learning where D ISCOV-
   ERYAGENT broadened the empirical search, while the astronomer reframed the problem, interpreted
   the evidence, focused the investigation, and determined when to conclude.

2.5 Economics: Assessing insurance costs as a component of housing affordability
We next evaluate D ISCOVERYAGENT on an open-ended discovery task in empirical economics, where the
goal is not to optimize a prediction but to surface descriptive insights. This analysis illustrates the kinds of
descriptive analyses that often serve as components of empirical economic research. We tasked D ISCOV-
ERYAGENT to explore the American Community Survey (ACS) and identify novel, interesting phenomena
(Methods Sec. 4.16). To acquire data, we developed and provided D ISCOVERYAGENT with a Census data
agent with search-and-download access to a broad collection of public-use U.S. Census and federal survey
data (Figure 5a, Supplementary Note 25, Supplementary Table 20).1 Over multiple rounds of hypothesis
steering, the economist and D ISCOVERYAGENT explored the affordability of housing insurance in markets
acutely stressed by rising climate risk, converging on Florida as a case study.

       Takeaways from investigation by economist + D ISCOVERYAGENT

   Steered by an economist, D ISCOVERYAGENT identiﬁed a sharp rise in Florida homeowners-insurance
   costs between 2018 and 2023. This burden fell increasingly on lower-income homeowners: between
   2018 and 2023, the percentage-point increase in insurance’s share of income was ≈ 7.1 times as large
   for the lowest income quartile as for the highest. These ﬁndings are consistent with prior national
   evidence and suggest that rising insurance costs are making homeownership less affordable in Florida,
   supporting the treatment of insurance as a core variable alongside home prices and mortgage payments
   when assessing affordability.


From an open data universe to a focused hypothesis. Starting from this broad data collection (Fig. 5a), the
economist and D ISCOVERYAGENT progressively narrowed to focused questions on home insurance afford-
ability (Fig. 5b). In Round 1, using 2023 ACS 1-year PUMS housing microdata, D ISCOVERYAGENT com-
pared the insurance-to-income ratio across three hurricane-exposed states (Louisiana, Florida, Texas) and
three states with less exposure (Minnesota, Ohio, California). This analysis found that the burden was both
higher and more dispersed in hurricane-prone states, with Louisiana and Florida owners spending roughly
twice the income share on insurance as owners in Minnesota or Ohio (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Table 21).
Narrowing to Florida in Round 6, D ISCOVERYAGENT used the 2018 and 2023 ACS 1-year PUMS housing
samples to document that the market had shifted sharply within ﬁve years. The weighted-median annual pre-
mium rose from $1,400 in 2018 to $2,200 in 2023 (+57%), with the entire premium distribution translating
rightward (Fig. 5d).
Independent reproduction conﬁrms a regressive, worsening burden. Using the audit agents, we reana-
lyzed and extended these ACS PUMS ﬁndings across additional years and geographies. First, the Florida
surge is stronger than the national trend, with an 83% rise from 2013 to 2023, outpacing the national median

fastest in high-risk markets [37, 38]. Premiums are primarily priced to the property and its risk rather than

lowest income quartile spent 6.6% of income on insurance compared with 1.2% for the highest—up from

prior ACS-based evidence that the heaviest housing-cost burdens fall on lower-income owners [38,40], these
results show that homeowners insurance burden appears to be regressive with respect to income. Because

       The increase is already large enough to change modeled tenure decisions. We used audit agents, work-
ing off of D ISCOVERYAGENT’s initial exploration, to perform a downstream, targeted analysis on the impli-
cation of home insurance on modeled buy vs. rent decisions. The price-to-rent ratio (P/R) is sometimes used
as a heuristic for whether a market is more suitable for buying or renting a home. Trulia’s 2012 consumer-
market heuristic favors buying at P/R at or below 15 and renting at P/R at or above 20 [45]; we use these
reference values as descriptive benchmarks. Between 15 and 20 is considered a “grey zone” where the ad-
vantage to buy or rent is less clear for a buyer.2 In this analysis, we report a P/R of 21 as a stricter threshold,
where above 21 is considered to be strongly rent-favored. Capitalizing local insurance costs pushed 41 of
168 Florida Public Use Microdata Areas (PUMAs; 24.4%) across our stringent P/R threshold of 21 (≈ 14%
at the cutoff of 20; Figure 5h, Supplementary Note 26, Supplementary Table 24). Rising insurance costs
meaningfully increase the P/R ratio, potentially shifting the implied tenure decision from buying toward
renting. This is especially impactful because simple price-to-rent heuristics typically omit insurance, and
user-cost frameworks, while including recurring ownership costs, typically do not model its large spatial vari-
ation [46,47]. These transition PUMAs span the Panhandle, central Florida, and several coastal metropolitan
areas rather than clustering in the most expensive markets, which were already above the threshold before
capitalization (Fig. 5h). A caveat worth noting is that this analysis capitalizes insurance into ownership cost
mechanically, but in equilibrium, rising premiums may also change both home price and rental price growth
in exposed markets [37], which would change the trajectory of the P/R shift over longer horizons.3 However,
this analysis shows that homeowners insurance costs might have a meaningful effect on home affordability in
Florida, to the point that a large number of regions become more rent-favorable after capitalizing insurance
than without.

   As opposed to the relatively autonomous role of D ISCOVERYAGENT in the extragalactic astronomy

2.6 Functional genomics: Prioritizing autoimmune risk genes from perturbation pheno-
      types
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) implicate hundreds of loci in autoimmune disease, but associa-
tion alone identiﬁes neither the causal gene nor how a variant affects cellular biology, complicating variant
interpretation. Perturb-seq [49] measures transcriptional responses to in vitro gene knockdown, providing
functional readouts and inference of regulatory circuits. However, Perturb-seq alone does not establish which
perturbation effects are relevant to human disease. GWAS and Perturb-seq are therefore complementary—
genetics identiﬁes disease-associated loci, while perturbation helps reveal what those genes do within the
cell. Integrating these modalities can help connect disease loci to candidate genes and cellular programs.
The genome-scale Perturb-seq atlas of Zhu et al. [50] measures transcriptional responses to gene knockdown
in primary CD4+ T cells (Figure 6a), which coordinate adaptive immunity and contribute to autoimmune dis-
ease [51]. Guided by a computational biologist, we used D ISCOVERYAGENT to integrate these perturbation
proﬁles with genetic evidence and test how they relate to autoimmune risk.
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                             19


    Takeaways from investigation by computational biologist + D ISCOVERYAGENT

   Together with a computational biologist, D ISCOVERYAGENT identiﬁed CD4+ T-cell “relay genes” as
   highly regulated genes that also broadly inﬂuence other genes. Relay genes were more likely than
   other genes to be linked to autoimmune diseases and inherited immune deﬁciencies, and they were
   more often annotated as potential targets for small molecule drugs. Combining gene-perturbation
   experiments with human genetic evidence prioritized four autoimmune-associated genes whose roles
   in disease remain poorly understood and suggested how they might affect T-cell function, providing
   concrete hypotheses for follow-up experiments.


D ISCOVERYAGENT exploration trajectory. The D ISCOVERYAGENT research trajectory was shaped by a
series of decisive negative results that narrowed the search space toward network topology (Figure 6b). To
begin the analysis, we gave D ISCOVERYAGENT access to differential expression statistics from the Zhu et al.
atlas [50] (Methods Sec. 4.16). D ISCOVERYAGENT was augmented with a sub-agent specialized in Open-
Targets, exposing a large amount of aggregated evidence across diseases and genes [52, 53] (Supplementary
Note 29). In Round 4, we also added BioMCP [54], which exposes over 40 common biomedical databases.
D ISCOVERYAGENT was driven in hypothesis steering mode by a human expert with expertise in human ge-
netics and Perturb-seq analysis. The agent was asked to characterize the perturbation phenotypes of genes
that are both perturbed in the Perturb-seq data and implicated in autoimmunity by GWAS [55, 56].
       Early exploratory rounds involved testing multiple hypotheses about autoimmune GWAS genes that
were rejected. D ISCOVERYAGENT found in Round 2 that putative causal genes did not have higher out-
degree than neighboring candidates within the same GWAS locus; in Round 3, however, these genes showed
higher out-degree than the genome-wide background of perturbed genes. In Round 5, D ISCOVERYAGENT
proposed the relay gene hypothesis, stating that genes serving as both heavily regulated targets (high in-
degree) and broad upstream regulators (high out-degree) are disproportionately disease-associated. This
was conﬁrmed in Round 5 with a reported odds ratio of 2.91 for GWAS enrichment among relay genes
vs. other genes (Figure 6c; Supplementary Table 25). In Rounds 6–7, the original analyses detected no
additional GWAS predictive value from feedback loops or directional asymmetry beyond relay status (Figure
6d, Supplementary Table 26). The principle was then strengthened on primary immunodeﬁciency (PID)
genes, which are implicated by rare Mendelian variants (Round 8), unifying disease-association evidence
from common and rare genetic variants. Additionally, selection constraints analysis revealed that the most
extreme relay genes are under higher evolutionary constraint (measured by shet [57]) than other categories
(Round 9, Figure 6e, Supplementary Table 27). Cross-cell-type validation in a K562 Perturb-seq atlas [58]
showed that the link between relay status and selective constraint is conserved, whereas autoimmune GWAS
enrichment did not replicate (Round 10). Finally, D ISCOVERYAGENT found in Round 12 that relay genes
were highly enriched for druggable proteins (Figure 6f, Supplementary Table 28).

Reproducing relay gene hypothesis with audit agents. We used audit agents to reproduce the central
claims of this research trace. We ﬁrst validate that autoimmune GWAS genes are indeed enriched in CD4+
T-cell relay genes (OR= 2.44), and that PID genes are enriched even more strongly (OR= 11.66); these are
computed by ﬁxing reference autoimmune GWAS and PID sets (Figure 6i; Supplementary Note 32, Sup-
plementary Figure 6, Supplementary Table 30). This enrichment is also robust across multiple autoimmune
diseases (Supplementary Figure 7) and across the OpenTargets locus-to-gene (L2G) cutoff used to deﬁne the
autoimmune GWAS gene set (Supplementary Figure 8). While K562 relay genes show a nominally signiﬁ-
cant enrichment for the erythrocyte traits matched to their lineage [64] (one-sided p = 0.033; not signiﬁcant
after multiple-testing correction), this enrichment is largely speciﬁc to the immune context. Relay genes from
three non-immune Perturb-seq atlases (K562, HCT116, and HEK293T) show no enrichment for autoimmune
or PID genes, and control cardiovascular and eye-disease GWAS sets are not signiﬁcantly enriched among
relay genes in any tested cell type (Supplementary Note 33, Supplementary Figure 9).
       We then turned to reproducing the druggability analysis. Because the original Round 12 analysis used
several deﬁnitions of druggability, we ﬁxed a single deﬁnition using an independent tractability set from
Minikel et al. [59]. With this set, relay genes were consistently enriched for small-molecule tractability in
all three conditions (Figure 6j, Supplementary Figure 10, Supplementary Table 32). However, we found
that this phenomenon is attenuated when accounting for the annotation depth of the genes, with only the
small-molecule association holding after this adjustment (Supplementary Note 34). Audit agents helped to
qualify this claim by reproducing this analysis and accounting for annotation as a confounder, which was not
considered during the original D ISCOVERYAGENT trace.
Relay genes show distinct functional proﬁles. Relay, terminal, and upstream genes occupy separable func-
tional compartments in the network (Figure 6k; Supplementary Table 33). Among 486 terms enriched against
the 10,282-gene background, 267 were most strongly enriched in relay genes, 180 in terminal genes, and 39 in
upstream genes; their concentration near the simplex corners indicates role-speciﬁc biology, with most shar-
ing restricted to the relay–terminal axis. The strongest terms distinguish these roles. Relay genes are enriched
for CD4+ T-cell activation (for example, regulation of CD4+ , αβ T-cell activation, OR= 12.4), terminal
genes for cytokine-receptor programs (for example, viral protein–cytokine receptor interaction, OR= 7.2),
and upstream genes for genome maintenance (for example, DNA repair, OR= 3.8). Shared relay–terminal
terms include TNFα–NF-κB, IFN-γ response, and IL2–STAT5 signaling, consistent with activation programs
being integrated by relay genes and executed through terminal outputs, whereas the strongest upstream terms
are upstream-speciﬁc. Relay functions are also the most stimulation-dependent, shifting from a poised lym-
phoid program at Rest to acute T-cell activation at 8 hr and committed lineage and checkpoint regulation by
48 hr (Supplementary Figure 12; Supplementary Note 35). Together, these results show that considering joint
in- and out-degree identiﬁes functionally distinct regulatory layers that neither degree alone reveals.
Mechanistic characterization of poorly studied relay genes. We then used audit agents to help investigate
if relay topology can help prioritize autoimmune-associated genes with limited mechanistic evidence (Fig-
ure 6l). Starting from 82 autoimmune GWAS-linked relay genes, we excluded DepMap common-essential
genes (Supplementary Note 36, Supplementary Figure 13) and those with existing drug, pathway, animal-
model, or literature evidence, leaving 11 candidates (Figure 6l, left; Supplementary Note 37). These ﬁlters
removed established autoimmune regulators and drug targets—including JAK2, IL2RA, and ZAP70—to fo-

   The scientist used D ISCOVERYAGENT as an exploratory collaborator to operationalize broad hypothe-

3 Discussion
Here, we present D ISCOVERYAGENT, a scientist-centric agentic AI system designed to support long-horizon
discovery through repeated cycles of autonomous investigation and scientist-directed hypothesis steering.
Across two predictive modeling tasks, D ISCOVERYAGENT produced models that outperformed both baseline
agentic systems and many prior human-engineered models, including the highest performing model on the
StarEmbed dataset and, with scientist steering, a variant-effect predictor that ranked second overall, behind
only one of 106 reference predictors and above all comparable AI co-scientist agents. Across three case
studies in extragalactic astronomy, economics, and functional genomics, D ISCOVERYAGENT supported open-
ended investigations that produced ﬁndings with direct relevance to their respective ﬁelds. Taken together,
these results suggest that a scientist-centric agentic system such as D ISCOVERYAGENT can contribute to
meaningful scientiﬁc advancements across a broad swath of ﬁelds.
       Across the scientist-steered investigations, we observed a consistent division of labor between broad
empirical execution and scientiﬁc judgment. D ISCOVERYAGENT implemented analyses, tested modeling
strategies, and traversed large hypothesis spaces, while scientists reframed objectives, corrected domain-
speciﬁc assumptions, standardized evaluation procedures, interpreted ambiguous ﬁndings, and decided when
a line of inquiry warranted further investment. These interventions occurred at epistemically consequential
moments rather than at every computational step. D ISCOVERYAGENT retained autonomy within a scoped hy-
pothesis branch while the scientist directed how the investigation evolved across branches. The preservation
of negative results in the accumulating research record also allowed failed approaches to narrow the search
space and inform subsequent hypotheses rather than disappearing from the ﬁnal account. This structure of-
fers a practical middle ground between continuously supervising individual analyses and delegating an entire
investigation to an autonomous system.
       However, meaningful hypothesis steering depends on whether the scientist can understand the evidence
accumulated before each decision. In D ISCOVERYAGENT, checkpointing makes the research process trans-
parent while audit agents make the research record operational by locating supporting evidence, reconstruct-
ing analyses, and testing claims through follow-up work. Transparency makes a trace available, auditability
makes it interrogable, and neither alone guarantees that a scientiﬁc conclusion is correct. Audit agents iden-
tiﬁed corrupted claims with an F1 score of 0.965 and located a test-set access violation precisely enough to
restore an earlier checkpoint and resume the investigation under stronger controls. In a preliminary study, re-
viewers with audit access recovered a mean of 3.50 distinct, deliberately inserted errors per person, compared
with 0.25 among reviewers given the paper and code. Auditability functions not only for post hoc veriﬁcation,
but as a prerequisite for informed steering, allowing scientists to correct, qualify, or redirect research while
preserving the useful work that preceded an error.
       A number of limitations exist with both the D ISCOVERYAGENT system and our study presented here.
First, D ISCOVERYAGENT is an AI system and suffers from all drawbacks related to AI systems, such as mis-
representation risk and hallucination. Second, D ISCOVERYAGENT is built solely for computational analyses
and is not equipped for physical laboratory experiments. Third, D ISCOVERYAGENT is an expensive system
that is reliant on LLM and computational resource access, which might be cost-prohibitive for some scien-
tists. Because of this last point, we acknowledge a few drawbacks to our study design. We do not provide
comprehensive ablations on various components of D ISCOVERYAGENT due to the large amount of resources
required to carry out these tasks. In addition, we are limited to a relatively small sample size of scientists
interacting with D ISCOVERYAGENT, due to the time and cost of running an end-to-end investigation. Sys-
tematic studies of these components are left for future work, and we invite the community to rigorously test
D ISCOVERYAGENT on a variety of scientiﬁc problems.
       Evaluating AI scientist systems remains challenging because the value of scientiﬁc research cannot be
fully represented by a single quantitative metric. We therefore evaluate capabilities of D ISCOVERYAGENT
at three levels. First, held-out performance on StarEmbed and ProteinGym provides a direct quantitative
comparison of the predictive models produced by different agentic systems. In the ProteinGym study, DA-
Steer performed better than the evaluated autonomous agents, although differences in execution models and
research budgets prevent attributing this advantage solely to scientist steering. Second, the automated and
human-assisted audit experiments test whether the research process is organized in a form that supports
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                             24


claim veriﬁcation and error identiﬁcation. Third, the three open-ended case studies provide empirical evi-
dence that D ISCOVERYAGENT can participate in scientiﬁcally meaningful investigations for which research
quality depends on domain-speciﬁc interpretation as well as numerical performance. These evaluations do
not constitute a comprehensive measure of scientiﬁc discovery or a universal ranking of AI scientist systems;
rather, they triangulate predictive performance, auditability, and practical scientiﬁc use across settings in
which no single metric captures the full research contribution.
      Future versions of D ISCOVERYAGENT could extend this interaction model to parallel hypothesis explo-
ration, richer multi-agent critique, and collaboration amongst interdisciplinary research teams. The central
challenge in these scaling directions will be aggregating a growing body of agent-generated evidence without
overwhelming scientists or obscuring the provenance, uncertainty, and contradictions that should guide the
next decision. Preserving auditable evidence and deliberate hypothesis boundaries as the system scales may
allow scientists to delegate increasingly broad empirical exploration while retaining authority over the ques-
tions pursued and the standards by which evidence is judged. More broadly, D ISCOVERYAGENT provides
a framework in which agentic autonomy and scientist agency reinforce one another, supporting a future of
AI-assisted science that remains interactive, inspectable, and directed by scientiﬁc judgment.

4 Methods
4.1 System overview
D ISCOVERYAGENT takes a dataset and a research brief as input and returns a structured scientiﬁc report
together with a complete, auditable research record. The system is built from two nested loops. An inner
loop, driven by a director agent, orchestrates a set of tool agents—a code-executing agent, a web-search
agent, a deep-research agent, and optional domain-speciﬁc agents—to carry out the analyses that test a sin-
gle hypothesis. An outer loop, driven by a hypothesis brancher, seeds each inner-loop invocation with a
hypothesis, accumulates ﬁndings across invocations in a dedicated memory, and exposes points at which a
human scientist can intervene.
       D ISCOVERYAGENT is designed to span a spectrum of autonomy rather than a single operating point.
Run end-to-end in autonomous mode, it generates and selects its own hypotheses, sequences its own tool
calls, and iterates until its budget is exhausted. In steering mode, a scientist can step into individual control
points—selecting a hypothesis, directing an analysis, or auditing a ﬁnished run—without changing the rest
of the pipeline. Audit agents and checkpoints ﬁt naturally into this process, allowing scientists to rigorously
evaluate D ISCOVERYAGENT outputs.
       We ﬁrst describe the agentic architecture in terms of the inner loop, which includes the primary tooling
of the system, and the outer loop, which includes the hypothesis branches and long-range memory. We then
describe how human scientists interact with the system, including the hypothesis steering. After this, we
walk through the checkpointing mechanisms and how this enables audit agents. Finally, we describe details
for all experiments in the Results. We defer many low-level details (thresholds, prompts, and ﬁle layouts) to
the Supplementary Notes, Figures, and Tables.
4.2 Inner loop: the director agent
The core of the inner loop is the director agent, an LLM prompted with the dataset description, the current
hypothesis, a catalog of available tool agents, and the report accumulated so far in the round. It performs the
high-level control of testing the hypothesis. The director’s main job is to dispatch queries to tool agents. A
query is the unit of the inner loop as it represents one call to a tool agent from the director. A contiguous
sequence of queries that investigates one hypothesis is a round, and a full run consists of a conﬁgurable
number of rounds.
      The unit of the inner loop is a query, which is a single message the director dispatches to one tool
agent. Two budgets govern a run and are set independently: a per-round query budget controls how deeply
each hypothesis is pursued, and a round budget controls how many hypotheses are pursued in sequence. We
discuss round budgets in a later section (Methods Sec. 4.4). The director is prompted to output a ReAct-
style [65] natural-language rationale followed by a single tool agent invocation, written as an XML-style
tag <agent>query</agent>, where agent is the name of the tool agent and query is the text of the
query passed to that sub-agent. Upon receipt of the query qi , the tool agent θj returns to the director agent a
natural-language report Ri , which is the corresponding report for the round. This report is injected into the
director agent’s conversation and used as context for the next query. The director runs as a persistent multi-
turn conversation with sub-agents for the length of a round and invokes exactly one tool agent per query. This
recurrent director–tool-agent exchange deﬁnes the inner-loop information ﬂow (Supplementary Figure 1).
Director as the central controller. The director, given a hypothesis and optional human guidance (described
in Sec. 4.7), decides the shape of a round. This ﬂexibility allows the director to repeat failed queries, retry
queries where results may be ambiguous, or to stop a round early if the director has enough evidence to
validate or invalidate the hypothesis. When enabled, we prompt the director to emit the reserved <<STOP>>
output once it judges the hypothesis sufﬁciently explored; the workﬂow detects this signal, terminates the
round before exhausting its query budget, and proceeds to report generation. Through prompting, we restrict
the director to executing only analyses explicitly related to the round’s hypothesis and guidance, preventing
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                               26


diverging inquiries that were not requested by the hypothesis selector or human driving the research process.
This gives scientists more explicit control over the research process. These behaviors distinguish D ISCOV-
ERYAGENT from agents that execute a predetermined sequence of steps; the director decides when an analysis
is complete, when to repeat work, and when to move on. Since the director agent is a crucial point for success
of D ISCOVERYAGENT, we often use a larger and more performant LLM for this component, such as Claude
Opus-level models (see Sec. 4.11 for details). The per-component model assignments and the round and
query budgets used in each experiment are given in Supplementary Note 41 and Supplementary Table 35.
Context management. Long rounds with verbose tool outputs can exceed the director’s context window.
We perform context isolation on the director-side through two mechanisms. First, the report structure com-
presses sub-agent work into one central natural-language description of the research, preventing context bloat
for the director agent for routine tool calls. Second, the director compacts its own context. Once its estimated
input length crosses a threshold, earlier turns are replaced by a model-generated summary that preserves the
hypothesis, the results obtained so far, and the outstanding next steps. The same mechanism is triggered
as a fallback when a context-overﬂow error is returned mid-query, so that rounds with large per-query out-
puts continue without manual intervention. When compaction is unavailable, the query budget bounds the
conversation length. Supplementary Note 42 gives the provider requirements, the trigger thresholds, and
the information the summary is required to retain. Note that this context management is separate from the
memory, which operates on the outer loop, discussed in Methods Sec. 4.5.
4.3 Inner loop: tool agents
The director dispatches work to a set of tool agents, each exposing a narrow interface and registered in the
director’s catalog with a short description of when to use it and what it returns. Three general-purpose tool
agents are available in every run.
Web-search agent. The web-search agent performs a single-shot search using the underlying model’s native
web-search capability and returns a short text answer. It is meant for lightweight lookups—conﬁrming a
deﬁnition, locating a canonical reference, or ﬁnding a publicly reported statistic—that do not justify the cost
of a deep research query.
Deep-research agent. The deep-research agent issues a long-form query to a dedicated deep-research model
and returns a structured report with an executive summary, key ﬁndings, a methodology note, limitations,
and a list of sources. It is used for questions that require synthesis across many sources, such as surveying
prior approaches to a task or summarizing the literature around a candidate mechanism. We use Perplexity’s
sonar-deep-research model throughout our experiments, but the D ISCOVERYAGENT system supports
other models as well.
       Before a run, D ISCOVERYAGENT can seed hypothesis generation with a literature background report.
When no reference collection is supplied, the deep-research agent uses the dataset description and research
brief to synthesize relevant prior work, established methods, and candidate research questions; alternatively, a
scientist can supply a collection of literature, which D ISCOVERYAGENT summarizes and synthesizes without
drawing on outside sources. In both modes, D ISCOVERYAGENT converts the literature into a single report
that is provided as context during hypothesis generation rather than inserting the full source documents into
agent conversations. The director can subsequently invoke the deep-research agent for targeted questions that
arise during hypothesis testing.
Execution agent. The execution agent handles the computational work such as loading data, running statis-
tical tests, ﬁtting models, generating ﬁgures, and following up on intermediate results. It is an autonomous
coding agent built on the claude agent sdk, with standard ﬁle and shell tools inside a sandboxed work-
ing directory [66]. Each invocation is a self-contained session where the agent receives a query from the
director, writes and runs code, saves ﬁgures and intermediate artifacts to the working directory, and returns
only a concise summary of what it did and found. Returning the summary rather than the full tool-call trace
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                               27


keeps the director’s context focused on scientiﬁc content rather than implementation detail. To reduce acci-
dental access to ﬁles and benchmark results outside the research workspace, we use layered application-level
containment where pre-tool hooks constrain direct ﬁle operations and screen common shell access patterns,
a post-tool guard terminates sessions after detected out-of-sandbox writes or edits, and a targeted URL hook
blocks known ProteinGym result sources. Supplementary Note 1 describes the execution environment and
summary generation, while Supplementary Note 17 details the containment hooks, the ProteinGym access
event, and the subsequent hardening and rollback.
Director agent orchestration of tool agents. The director agent acts as the central intermediary between all
the tool agents, passing relevant ﬁndings between sub-agents to facilitate the testing of the hypothesis. Within
individual hypothesis-testing rounds, the director generally followed a primary test → diagnosis → challenge
→ validation sequence. It ﬁrst operationalized the central prediction and requested a direct comparison,
then used the result to select targeted tests of confounding, alternative explanations, subgroup or condition
dependence, and robustness. For example, in the functional genomics study, an unexpected enrichment of
primary immunodeﬁciency genes among relay genes led the director to request logistic regression, expression
adjustment, condition stratiﬁcation, overlap exclusion, permutation testing, and in-degree-matched controls.
In the photometric-redshift prediction study in extragalactic astronomy, an initial performance improvement
accompanied by rapid overﬁtting prompted successive tests of regularization, backbone freezing, alternative
architectures, boosted trees, and ensembles. Specialized agents typically entered this sequence to resolve
a speciﬁc information bottleneck—such as acquiring Census variables in the economics study or disease
annotations from OpenTargets in the functional genomics study—after which the execution agent integrated
the new evidence and performed the hypothesis test. Across the three case studies, the execution agent
accounted for 87.5–94.6% of classiﬁable within-round calls, while web search and domain-speciﬁc agents
were used selectively when a test required external evidence (Supplementary Note 43). Deep research agents
were used sparingly by the director agent, with the only deep research usage arising in the DA-Steer trial
of the ProteinGym experiments. The web search agents were more often used to gather quick references or
more precise queries. This was most likely due to either the seeding of deep research reports in each run or
the scientist-supplied hypotheses often already including relevant literature details, making comprehensive
deep research queries less useful in the context of the round-wise budgets.
4.4 Outer loop: hypothesis branching
The outer loop wraps the inner-loop workﬂow and runs it over a conﬁgurable number of rounds. Each round
proceeds in three phases: a hypothesis is selected and used to condition the director (deﬁned as hypothesis
branches); the inner loop runs until it terminates or exhausts its query budget; and the resulting report is
folded into the accumulated memory. Between rounds the in-round conversation is reset, so each round starts
from a clean director context while the accumulated memory persists and is re-injected as background at the
start of the next round. In this section, we’ll describe the overview of the outer loop, and subsequent sections
will outline the hypothesis generation.
       D ISCOVERYAGENT can take on two modes based on outer loop autonomy. In automatic mode, a
hypothesis-generation agent proposes a single hypothesis—a statement with a justiﬁcation grounded in the
literature or in the accumulated memory, and an optional plan for testing it—which is directly passed to
the director. In hypothesis-steering mode, described in later sections, the generated hypotheses are handed
to a human scientist, giving the scientist agency to edit, select, and reﬁne hypotheses before choosing to
run them in the inner loop. Optionally, the ﬁrst few rounds can be run in an exploratory mode in which
no hypothesis is ﬁxed and the director is instead asked to broadly characterize the dataset, seeding later
hypothesis generation with observations from the data rather than from the literature alone. Supplementary
Note 41 reports the round, branch, exploratory-round, and per-round query settings used in each experiment,
while Supplementary Note 44 speciﬁes the candidate, selected-hypothesis, round-report, and accumulated-
memory artifacts that connect successive rounds.

4.5 Memory: empirical and procedural
D ISCOVERYAGENT keeps two separate kinds of long-lived state across rounds: an empirical memory of
ﬁndings and a procedural memory of code. Our central intuition behind this separation is that the director
agents need only understand what has been found (empirical memory), and the execution agent needs to
understand what has been implemented.
Empirical memory. The empirical memory is an agentic memory that integrates each new round’s report
while pruning redundancy, using a three-agent pipeline adapted from the ACE context-engineering archi-
tecture [67]. An integrator merges the accumulated memory with the new report with completeness as its
explicit objective, retaining every data point and technical detail from both. An analyzer then identiﬁes pas-
sages that could be consolidated and assigns each a risk level (low, medium, or high) based on the technical
detail that consolidation might remove, while specifying the information that must be preserved. A reﬁner
applies only the low-risk consolidations, verifying that information ﬂagged as critical is preserved. Similar
to the motivation behind ACE, splitting the update into three narrowly scoped agents avoids dropping details
and making overgeneralizations that result when a single agent is asked to integrate, analyze, and rewrite at
once. This memory is injected into the director and the hypothesis-generation agent at the start of each round.
This is how D ISCOVERYAGENT accumulates ﬁndings across rounds without ever passing a full per-round
conversation forward. Supplementary Note 2 details the pipeline.
Procedural memory. The empirical memory is crucial for long-range understanding of research results, but
it is not built to hold low-level details about codebase construction. Since execution agents are launched
with fresh context at each query in which the director agent invokes them, we use a procedural memory to
track what has previously been implemented. We use an active memory component and a passive memory
component, both implemented via markdown ﬁles in the codebase. The active memory component is a short
markdown ﬁle to orient the execution agent to the codebase when launching a fresh agent into the codebase.
The passive memory component is a longer documentation ﬁle that can be accessed on-demand with tools
such as grep. We build these memory components through two sub-agents that maintain each document;
these sub-agents are managed by the central execution agent, which is prompted to maintain documentation
of its code to increase codebase reusability. We prompt these sub-agents to 1) keep the active memory as
an overview of the codebase and 2) to make the passive memory comprehensive but greppable, which is a
useful framework to enable more efﬁcient coding agent use of the ﬁle [68]. Supplementary Note 3 gives
the ﬁle names and the sub-agent workﬂow. We enforce some additional structure on the codebase structure
to ensure that the execution agent grows the codebase in a reusable and modular way, borrowing proper
software engineering principles.
4.6 Review mechanism
D ISCOVERYAGENT injects an automated reviewer into the research loop at a conﬁgurable query frequency.
When a review ﬁres, the round’s cumulative report is passed to a reviewer LLM that produces a critique
focused on novelty, rigor, and direction; the critique is fed back to the director as feedback that shapes the
next query. During exploratory rounds, we omit the reviewer in order to allow D ISCOVERYAGENT to explore
the dataset without critique overhead; when executing speciﬁc hypotheses, we enable the reviewer. The
reviewer can optionally be granted web-search access to check whether a ﬁnding has already been reported
in the literature.
Review designer. Rather than a single ﬁxed reviewer prompt across all tasks, D ISCOVERYAGENT uses a
review designer that generates a task-speciﬁc reviewer prompt at the start of each run from the dataset de-
scription and the research task. For example, the reviewer can emphasize causal rigor for an epidemiology
task, or novelty for a mechanism-discovery task. This ties the reviewer to the research goal rather than to
a generic notion of scientiﬁc quality, and removes the need to hand-write a reviewer prompt for each run.
The generated prompt is checkpointed with the rest of the run conﬁguration so the reviewer’s behavior can
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                                 29


be audited. The experiment-speciﬁc review intervals and reviewer model assignments are reported in Sup-
plementary Note 41; Supplementary Note 44 describes how the active reviewer prompt and review-relevant
workﬂow state are included in each query checkpoint.
4.7 Steerability: Hypothesis Steering
Scientist intervention at hypothesis boundaries. In hypothesis-steering, we concentrate scientist interven-
tion at the boundaries between research rounds (Figure 1b). After each round, D ISCOVERYAGENT preserves
the latest round report, accumulated empirical memory, checkpoint trace, and research codebase, then re-
turns control to the scientist before beginning the next branch. The steering interface supplies the original
research task, accumulated memory, and latest round report to a hypothesis generation agent, which proposes
a conﬁgurable set of candidate directions grounded in the evidence accumulated so far. The checkpoint trace
and codebase remain available for separate manual inspection by the scientist. Each candidate comprises a
testable hypothesis, a justiﬁcation, and an analysis plan; the scientist can additionally provide initial guidance
to constrain the candidate set toward a scientiﬁc question, mechanism, or methodological priority.
Evidence-grounded selection and revision of hypotheses. To support an informed choice rather than a
review of candidate text alone, the interface exposes the accumulated memory and latest round report along-
side conversational agents that can answer questions about the reports, checkpoint trace, and codebase. The
scientist can therefore interrogate the evidence and assumptions underlying a proposed direction before 1)
accepting a candidate, 2) editing it directly, 3) asking the hypothesis-generation agent to revise or regenerate
it from written feedback, or 4) replacing it with a custom hypothesis. The hypothesis selection process is not
a passive multiple-choice selection process but rather an active engagement from the scientist, allowing for
rich internal feedback through a conversational format with the hypothesis generation agent. In addition, the
scientist has access to the audit agents, which allow for in-depth searching over the research checkpoints and
codebase, allowing for deeper understanding of the scientiﬁc process to inform selection. The scientist may
also attach overall research guidance that applies beyond the hypothesis itself, such as a requirement to avoid
causal language, use ﬁner-grained data, or prioritize a particular validation strategy.
Handoff to autonomous execution. Finalization produces a single selected hypothesis artifact containing
the hypothesis itself, its justiﬁcation, an optional analysis plan, and any scientist feedback or overall guid-
ance. When the run resumes, D ISCOVERYAGENT loads this artifact and injects it into the director’s ﬁrst
query context with an explicit instruction to test the scientist-supplied hypothesis and integrate the accompa-
nying feedback; the selected direction then remains in the director’s conversation throughout the round. The
director autonomously decomposes this direction into queries and executes the inner loop under the exist-
ing query budget, while the accumulated empirical memory and version-controlled codebase persist across
rounds. Hypothesis steering thus adds a low-frequency, high-leverage intervention at the point where the
research objective changes, rather than requiring the scientist to supervise individual tool calls. Supplemen-
tary Note 45 describes the interface state, revision operations, saved artifacts, and consistency controls, and
Supplementary Note 44 details the checkpoint and resume machinery.
4.8 Transparency: checkpointing and version control
D ISCOVERYAGENT implements an extensive checkpointing mechanism that logs each query and round ac-
tion. The checkpoint store is organized by round and by query: each query has its own JSON checkpoint of
the full workﬂow state—the director and hypothesis-agent conversations, the intermediate results, the running
report, any active reviewer prompt, and the run conﬁguration—together with a human-readable Markdown
rendering of the query and its result. Each round additionally writes its starting conﬁguration, its report
before and after memory integration, its log, any generated reviewer prompt, and—where applicable—the
selected hypothesis and the candidate list used to seed it.
      Separately, the execution agent’s working code directory is placed under Git [69] version control, and
changes are committed as the run proceeds with messages that tag each commit by round and query. This
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                               30


yields a navigable history of the code, data, and ﬁgures produced during a run, so that a reviewer can inspect
the working directory at any point in the trace and diff between steps. Together, the checkpoint tree (the state
of the agents) and the git history (the state of the code and data) record both what the agents were reasoning
over and what was on disk at every step. Supplementary Note 44 describes the store layout and the commit
scheme.
Checkpoint resumption. At startup, the outer loop scans the checkpoint tree to distinguish completed rounds
from an incomplete next round. A completed round advances the run to the next hypothesis boundary with
its accumulated memory and, in steering mode, the selected-hypothesis artifact; an incomplete round instead
restores the most recent query checkpoint, including the running report, agent conversations, reviewer state,
and query counter. This separation allows a scientist to pause deliberately at a hypothesis boundary while
also allowing interrupted tool-agent work to resume without treating it as a new scientiﬁc branch.
4.9 Auditability: Audit agents
D ISCOVERYAGENT exposes the trace (checkpoints, memory ﬁles, and version control) to a set of audit
agents. An audit pipeline consumes the per-query checkpoints and renders a run as a navigable, round-
by-round timeline of queries, results, hypotheses, reviews, and code changes. Audit agents are practically
implemented with the Claude Agent SDK harness, which allows for access like grep and progressive context
disclosure [66]. However, audit agents could feasibly admit any harness, allowing for ﬂexibility by down-
stream users; indeed, we use audit agents with the Codex [70] harness in audit experiments (Section 2.3).
The construction of D ISCOVERYAGENT’s checkpoints is built speciﬁcally to enable navigation towards de-
tailed round-by-round, query-by-query transcripts, leveraging progressive context disclosure for efﬁciency
and completeness. We demonstrate the efﬁcacy of this construction, as well as the power of audit agents
equipped with D ISCOVERYAGENT’s provenance structure, in Section 2.3.
       Oriented with these components, audit agents operate in two distinct conﬁgurations that differ in what
they are permitted to do to the trace. The read-only conﬁguration observes the research from outside it. Its
tool allow-list contains only ﬁle reading, content search, globbing, and shell commands, with no write, edit,
or web-fetch capability, so a session cannot alter or rerun the research under review. This is the conﬁgura-
tion exposed through the audit interface during hypothesis steering (Supplementary Note 12), given to the
Audit cohort of the human study, and used as the ﬁxed auditor in the corrupted-claim benchmark; it answers
questions and traces the provenance of an implementation or ﬁnding, but every claim it makes is grounded
in artifacts that already exist. The execution-enabled conﬁguration additionally receives write and execute
permissions so that it can reproduce a reported result or run a follow-up analysis. It mounts the checkpoint
tree and research codebase read-only and writes into a separate analysis directory, so the original D ISCOV-
ERYAGENT implementation is preserved and any new script is distinguishable from agent-generated research
code. We used this conﬁguration for the post hoc reproductions and extensions reported in the case studies,
including the economics premium-trend and price-to-rent analyses and the functional genomics topology,
tractability, and target-prioritization follow-ups; the reproduction scripts are named in the corresponding
Supplementary Notes. Read-only auditing is therefore the conﬁguration that supports steering and review,
whereas execution-enabled auditing is what produces new analysis on top of a completed run.
       Audit agents are a simple yet powerful framework, and the D ISCOVERYAGENT system is built to
natively integrate them into both hypothesis steering and follow-up analyses.
4.10 Specialized agents
The modularity of the tool agents allows D ISCOVERYAGENT to integrate specialized agents that can integrate
domain-speciﬁc tooling. We use specialized tool agents in two of our case studies to expose curated access
to speciﬁc data sources and domains. These agents follow the same interface as the general-purpose agents
but are enabled only on runs where their data source is relevant; we describe the two used in this work below.
Census data agent. The economics experiment is served by the census data agent, a data-delivery agent that

exposes three public-use data backends through a single search-then-download interface: published Ameri-
can Community Survey (ACS) summary tables, conﬁdentiality-protected ACS Public Use Microdata Sam-
ple (PUMS) microdata, and harmonized IPUMS microdata (the USA, CPS, NHIS, and MEPS collections).

4.11 Shared agent conﬁgurations
All experiments use the architecture described above while varying the operating mode, component models,
round and query budgets, review frequency, context compaction, early stopping, and availability of special-
ized agents. StarEmbed and the autonomous protein variant-effect prediction run (DA-Auto) use automatic
hypothesis selection, whereas the extragalactic astronomy, economics, functional genomics, and scientist-
steered protein experiment (DA-Steer) allow a scientist to select or edit hypotheses between rounds. Supple-
mentary Note 41 reports the complete component assignments and budgets for each experiment.
      Across the ﬁve scientiﬁc studies, the director, hypothesis, review, review-design, memory, and web-
search components were conﬁgured with Claude Opus 4.6, while deep-research calls use Perplexity’s ﬂag-
ship agent sonar-deep-research. Execution models differ by study: StarEmbed uses Claude Son-
net 4.5, the ProteinGym conﬁguration uses Sonnet 4.6, the extragalactic astronomy and economics studies
use Opus 4.6, and the functional genomics campaign changes execution models during the trace. Query
caps are 10 per round for StarEmbed, ProteinGym, and extragalactic astronomy, and 25 for economics and
functional genomics; available assignments and deviations are listed in Supplementary Note 41. Longer
query numbers are given to the economics and functional genomics experiments because these experiments
required iterative interaction with a data acquisition specialized sub-agent, which may require longer rounds.
For literature/deep-research seeding, we supplied a small collection of initial papers to the extragalactic as-
tronomy experiment, whereas the other four studies began with an automatically generated deep-research
background report.
4.12 Predictive modeling evaluation
We separated model development from ﬁnal assessment in predictive modeling evaluations for StarEmbed
and ProteinGym. Before exploration, we ﬁxed the test split and provided each agent with only the labeled
research data. For StarEmbed, the research data included a preselected validation set; for ProteinGym, it
contained only the training variants, and each agent selected its own internal validation data. Agents could use
validation data throughout exploration to compare hypotheses, features, model classes, and hyperparameters,
but no part of the test set was exposed in the research workspace. To separate these sets, we deﬁne these as
the research split, or the data given to the agent to perform the research, and the evaluation split, the data
used to evaluate the agent. We speciﬁed task-appropriate reporting metrics in advance and used the same
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                                32


metric deﬁnitions for validation and ﬁnal testing; validation metrics guided development, although individual
training procedures could optimize auxiliary objectives such as multiclass log loss, whereas test metrics were
reserved for ﬁnal performance reporting. In the extragalactic astronomy case study, we retrain and evaluate
selected recipes on a separately constructed sample from the same survey sources (Supplementary Note 18).
Its training, validation, and test splits are mutually disjoint, but 26.7% of its test galaxies also occur in the
original research training or validation data due to overlaps in source data; Supplementary Note 23 reports
sensitivity analyses excluding these galaxies and shows that excluding these galaxies does not meaningfully
change performance.
       After exploration concluded, we mechanistically identiﬁed the best model reported by each agent, with-
out using test-set performance to choose among candidates. We then extracted the corresponding training and
evaluation code from the agent’s research artifacts, including its preprocessing, feature construction, model
conﬁguration, and metric implementation. This procedure ties each reported result to the representation
and model class selected within the original research process. Supplementary Notes 6, 5, and 8 document
implementation-speciﬁc reﬁtting and stored-prediction decisions.
       Once the selected representation, model class, and ﬁnal training procedure were ﬁxed, we retrained
on the research split where reﬁtting was required, and evaluated on the untouched test set. We report the
resulting prespeciﬁed test metrics as ﬁnal model performance. Supplementary Notes 4 and 5 describe the
StarEmbed data release, selected artifacts, metrics, and held-out evaluation. Supplementary Notes 8, 10,
and 9 provide the corresponding details for the ProteinGym case study.

StarEmbed setup. We ask D ISCOVERYAGENT to develop a seven-class variable-star classiﬁer using the

trajectory, selected classiﬁer, and evaluation protocol.

gation step. The per-round query cap is ten, increased to ﬁfteen for DA-Steer Round 10 and the subsequent
aggregation step. We evaluate the resulting predictors on 140,810 held-out variants from non-overlapping
residue positions within the same assays, reporting per-assay Spearman correlation. The evaluation split
contained contiguous region holdouts, ensuring strong generalization requirements that avoided memorizing
local structural patterns for the protein. We score all 106 ProteinGym reference predictors on these same

4.14 Baseline agents
We compare D ISCOVERYAGENT with four agentic baselines spanning general-purpose coding, parallel multi-
agent investigation, iterative hypothesis testing, and agentic tree search; task inputs, budgets, models where
exposed, artifact-selection rules, and held-out evaluation procedures are reported in the Supplementary Infor-
mation. For each benchmark, the baseline agents received the same released research split; system-speciﬁc
setup and prompting are documented in Supplementary Notes 4 and 8. We exclude methods designed for
strictly veriﬁable tasks because D ISCOVERYAGENT does not assume an external veriﬁer and the held-out test
sets remain unavailable during research.
Coding agent. We evaluate a general-purpose coding agent in single-session (referred to as “Coding
agent@1”) and ﬁxed-goal continuation settings (referred to as “Coding agent loop”). Coding agent@1
received one prompt per task, with experiment-speciﬁc turn and scheduler caps, while the Coding agent
loop received the initial prompt followed by a ﬁxed number of continuation prompts. Experiment-speciﬁc
conﬁgurations and artifacts are reported in Supplementary Notes 4, 5, and 8.
Kosmos. Kosmos is an autonomous AI scientist system that decomposes an open-ended research objective
into parallel investigations and integrates their ﬁndings into a report [9]. We use Kosmos through Edison’s
provided online interface [71]. Prompts, hosted-interface settings, artifacts, and evaluation decisions are
reported in Supplementary Notes 4, 5, 8, and 7.
AutoDiscovery. AutoDiscovery is another autonomous AI scientist system that iteratively proposes and tests
hypotheses using Bayesian surprise [10]. Similar to Kosmos, we use the online interface for AutoDiscov-
ery for our tasks [72]. Experiment budgets, prompts, returned hypotheses, and downstream evaluation are
reported in Supplementary Notes 4, 5, and 8.
AI-Scientist-v2. AI-Scientist-v2 uses agentic tree search to implement and reﬁne generated research
ideas [7]. Since AI-Scientist-v2 was originally designed to source problem statements from literature instead
of working on ﬁxed datasets, we adapted AI-Scientist-v2 with a project-authored wrapper that supplied
each benchmark’s task description and local data to its native ideation and tree-search stages, omitted its
paper-writing pipeline, and applied its reviewer separately to the stage summaries. Because of AI-Scientist-
v2’s original design to gather datasets as part of its process, it refused to use the provided datasets during
its analyses, despite being given access to the same datasets as the other baseline agents. This resulted
in the methods built by the agent often being built on synthetic data. The prompts, search conﬁgurations,
candidate-selection procedures, and evaluation provenance are reported in Supplementary Notes 4, 5, and 8.
Model selection and resource control. For selecting models from each agent, we followed closely our im-
plementation of model selection from D ISCOVERYAGENT. We used the agent’s own internal reporting to
rank the best model to choose; this was based on either an explicit recommendation from the baseline agent
or reported validation losses. The model-selection decisions are documented in Supplementary Notes 5
and 8. We also controlled resources for each agent to the furthest conﬁgurable extent. Both coding agent im-
plementations as well as the AI-Scientist-v2 implementations were given access to the same computational
resources as D ISCOVERYAGENT for the tasks. We were unable to provide this same control for Kosmos and
AutoDiscovery as these baselines were run through the available online interfaces. On agent budget, we also
attempted to control the number of experiments for which each agent could implement models on the dataset.
This was very feasible for all agents except for Kosmos, which is given no lever to control experimentation
effort through the online interface. The baseline resource and experiment budgets are documented in Supple-
mentary Notes 4 and 8 and Supplementary Table 4. The allocations, realized budgets, and cost accounting
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                              34


for every locally executed run are given in Supplementary Note 43 and Supplementary Table 36.
4.15 Evaluation of audit agents
We evaluate audit agents in an automated benchmark measuring whether a ﬁxed auditor can identify cor-
rupted claims from research traces and in a human study measuring whether access to an audit agent im-
proves expert review of an agent-generated paper. The automated benchmark varies the trace while holding
the auditor ﬁxed, whereas the human study varies whether reviewers receive the read-only audit interface in
addition to the paper and codebase. We score the automated benchmark mechanically from known corrupted
claim identiﬁers; the human study requires a separate matching rubric for free-text reviewer ﬁndings.
Automated corrupted-claim identiﬁcation. We ﬁrst extract 50 claims from each ﬁnal report produced
by D ISCOVERYAGENT, Kosmos, and the coding agent loop baseline on StarEmbed. For each system, we
construct ﬁve independently sampled cases in which 25 claims are corrupted using a ﬁxed catalog of eight
corruption types. The corruption catalog comprises numeric alteration, reframing, effect-size inﬂation, lim-
itation removal, method misstatement, signiﬁcance misstatement, validation misstatement, and sample-size
alteration. Thus, a case may change a reported value or sample size, strengthen the interpretation of an ef-
fect, remove a stated caveat, or claim a validation procedure that the trace does not contain. We sample the
corrupted claim identiﬁers with a ﬁxed seed for each case, while the corruption type assigned to a selected
claim follows the ﬁxed generation protocol described in Supplementary Note 13.
       We then feed these 50 claims from each agent, along with that respective agent’s research artifacts
(Supplementary Note 14), to coding agent auditors. We evaluate GPT-5.6-Sol, GPT-5.6-Terra, and GPT-5.6-
Luna with medium reasoning in the same Codex harness [70, 73]. Within each auditor-model comparison,
we hold reasoning effort, tool access, and response schema ﬁxed across trace sources. We used GPT-5.6
in this case to differentiate from the Claude models used to insert corruptions. The auditor labels every
claim as supported, misrepresented, or uncertain, and we score only the misrepresented verdict as a positive
detection against the known corruption set. The scoring is done deterministically, using each “corrupted”
claim number and matching against ground-truth; this avoids ambiguous LLM-as-a-judge adjudications. We
also run a matched report-only control that removes access to the trace while holding the reports, corruptions,
and auditor ﬁxed. Each run uses a fresh read-only environment with network access disabled and with
memory, apps, plugins, and nested agents disabled. Supplementary Note 13 gives the full construction and
scoring protocol.
       One possible explanation for the performance gap is that the inserted corruptions differed in difﬁculty
across systems. To assess this, we obtained post hoc, system-blinded difﬁculty ratings from independent
LLM graders and evaluated recall by difﬁculty stratum and with logistic adjustment, without reselecting or
rebalancing the corruptions. Although difﬁculty distributions differed, D ISCOVERYAGENT retained higher
recall within the shared difﬁculty strata and after adjustment (Supplementary Note 15, Supplementary Ta-
ble 10).
Human-assisted error identiﬁcation. We conduct an experiment to test whether audit agents help machine-
learning researchers identify errors in an agent-generated research paper. Using an intermediate run of the
DA-Auto run for the DMS experiment (version with ﬁve completed rounds), we used audit agents to generate
a report in the style of an academic paper. We then used LLMs to identify areas of the paper that could be
manipulated or misrepresented to insert corruptions. Using LLMs, we identiﬁed 25 corruptions per paper,
prompting the LLM to classify each error as “easy”, “medium”, or “hard” to ﬁnd. We repeated this process
3 times, yielding 75 total corruptions. We ﬁrst removed the “easy” corruptions as most of these involved
obvious, large changes to the manuscript that would have been easily detectable. We then manually sorted
through each of the corruptions, removing duplicates and those that would be almost impossible to ﬁnd in
the timeframe such as slight numeric changes that are within error bars. This left us with 36 errors; we then
used an LLM to apply these changes to the paper.
      We recruited 8 participants and randomly sorted them into either the code group (n = 4) or the audit
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                              35


group (n = 4). The code group received only the codebase and ﬁnal corrupted report. The audit group was
given the codebase, the agent checkpoints, the corrupted ﬁnal report, and the audit agents attached to each of
these artifacts. All participants received the same corrupted paper and codebase, holding the inserted errors
ﬁxed across cohorts. The code group was explicitly instructed not to use AI tools, including chat interfaces,
coding agents, or AI-enabled editors; standard code-search tools such as keyword search were permitted.
Further, we facilitated the audit group through an online user interface which gave them access to the audit
agent for consistency. Importantly, all auditors across both cohorts were given the same corrupted paper
and codebase to control for strength of corruptions. We then asked reviewers to identify inconsistencies or
errors as in a conference review and to record a short summary, location, and explanation for each ﬁnding
under a self-tracked one-hour limit (Supplementary Note 16). This read-only interface allowed participants
to ask questions grounded in the codebase and checkpoints and inspect supporting artifacts (Supplementary
Note 12).
      To score error identiﬁcation, we use GPT-5.6-Sol at medium reasoning effort to match each partic-
ipant’s original ﬁndings to the catalog of 36 applied errors, withholding participant identity, cohort, and
historical matching assignments from the judge. A match requires identiﬁcation of the speciﬁc altered claim.
We repeat the evaluation ﬁve times per participant with identical inputs, yielding 40 independent judgments
of 54 ﬁndings. Match sets agree across all ﬁve runs for 53 of 54 ﬁndings (98.1%), with exact agreement in
536 of 540 pairwise comparisons (99.3%). We manually adjudicate the two ﬁndings that either vary across
runs or disagree with historical assignments, retaining the unanimous matches for all other ﬁndings. For
each participant, we count the reported ﬁndings, ﬁnding–error matching assignments, and unique inserted
errors recovered, deduplicating catalog identiﬁers across ﬁndings for the last measure. Figure 3f reports total
ﬁndings and unique inserted errors recovered per participant. Supplementary Note 49 details the matching
rubric, robustness analysis, and manual adjudication.
4.16 Scientist-steered discovery case studies
We use hypothesis steering for three open-ended case studies in extragalactic astronomy, economics, and
functional genomics. In each study, a scientist supplies the hypothesis for each subsequent round, and D IS -
COVERYAGENT resumes from the preceding checkpoint. We describe the input data, study-speciﬁc con-
ﬁgurations, and completed runs below; shared settings are given in Methods Sec. 4.11 and Supplementary
Note 41.
Extragalactic astronomy experiment setup. We study low-redshift galaxy selection and photometric-
redshift regression using 128 × 128 Legacy Survey image cutouts in the g, r, and z bands together with
catalog photometry and reference redshifts. The research split contains 181,959 training and 12,130 valida-
tion galaxies; the original run deﬁnes the low-redshift target as z < 0.03. We seed hypothesis generation with
a small collection of scientist-supplied papers and use Claude Opus 4.6 for both the director and execution
agent, with director context compaction and early stopping enabled. This experiment was driven primarily
by a research scientist in astrophysics who has a background in machine learning applied to astronomy but
limited background in photometric redshift. The scientist collaborated with a PhD student in machine learn-
ing as well as a postdoctoral researcher with more experience in astronomical catalog analysis. An initial
exploratory round characterizes the data, after which a scientist supplies hypotheses for 15 further rounds.
We set a ten-query cap per round, increased to twenty in Round 9 due to longer experimentation requirements.
Across the 16 rounds, validation results guide model development and selection; the ﬁnal round consolidates
standardized predictions for 125 models, of which 123 support classiﬁcation metrics. We subsequently re-
train selected recipes on separate external development data and evaluate them on an external test split, with
the balanced v4 evaluation using a z < 0.05 target. Supplementary Notes 19 and 18 describe the trajectory
and split deﬁnitions, and Supplementary Table 13 gives the training conﬁgurations. Supplementary Note 20
gives the architectures and training conﬁgurations of the two single-modality baselines.
Economics experiment setup. We begin with an open-ended instruction to identify economically interest-
DiscoveryAgent                                                                                           36


ing patterns in American Community Survey (ACS) data and an empty data directory. We enable the Census
data agent to retrieve ACS summary tables, conﬁdentiality-protected Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS)
records, and harmonized IPUMS data on demand, returning data ﬁles and variable dictionaries for the ex-
ecution agent to analyze. The study draws on state- and county-level demographic, income, housing, and
mobility tables concentrated in 2014, 2023, and 2024; 2023 PUMS records for California, Florida, Louisiana,
Minnesota, Ohio, and Texas; 2018 Florida housing records; and a 2023 IPUMS USA householder extract.
We use Claude Opus 4.6 for the director and execution agent, enable director context compaction, and allow
up to 25 tool-agent queries per round to accommodate iterative data retrieval and analysis. An economist,
who is a PhD student and expert in insurance markets, supplies hypotheses between rounds. Supplementary
Note 50 summarizes the hypothesis and outcome of every round. The run completed seven rounds and 160
queries: ten in the initial round and 25 in each subsequent round. Supplementary Note 25 describes the
accessed data; the follow-up analyses below distinguish subsequent reproductions and extensions from this
checkpointed run.
Functional genomics experiment setup. We ask D ISCOVERYAGENT to investigate how gene perturbations
can inform the interpretation of non-coding variation, using a genome-scale CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)
study targeting 12,748 genes in primary human CD4+ T cells from four donors [50]. We supply a differential-
expression object containing 33,983 perturbation–condition proﬁles across 10,282 measured genes under rest-
ing, 8-hour-stimulated, and 48-hour-stimulated conditions. We enable the OpenTargets data agent through-
out to retrieve curated target–disease associations and related annotations for downstream analysis, and add
BioMCP as an execution-agent skill beginning in Round 4. The director uses Claude Opus 4.6 throughout;
execution is conﬁgured with Sonnet 4.5 for Rounds 0–2 and Opus 4.6 thereafter. We enable director context
compaction and early stopping, with a cap of 25 tool-agent queries per round. Following an exploratory ﬁrst
round, a scientist supplies hypotheses for 12 further rounds, including the focus on autoimmune disease that
develops during the study. The completed run spans 13 rounds. Supplementary Notes 51 and 30 describe the
original research trajectory and the subsequent standardized network analyses used for the reported follow-
ups.
